# NCLEX for International Nurses: The Complete U.S. Licensure Guide

**Published:** 2026-08-11 | **Reading Time:** 12 min

---

## Quick Summary

If you trained as a nurse outside the United States, the path to U.S. licensure has additional steps: credential evaluation, English proficiency testing, and understanding U.S.-specific nursing norms. This guide walks you through every step from foreign graduate to licensed RN.

---

## The One Thing to Remember

> **Your nursing knowledge is valid. The NCLEX tests clinical judgment, not where you studied.** The challenge is translating your training to U.S. expectations — and navigating the bureaucratic requirements.

---

## Step 1: Credential Evaluation

Before you can sit for the NCLEX, a credentialing agency must verify that your foreign nursing education is equivalent to a U.S. nursing program.

### Approved Credentialing Agencies

| Agency | Cost | Timeline | Notes |
|--------|------|----------|-------|
| **CGFNS (Commission on Graduates of Foreign Nursing Schools)** | $445–$595 | 2–6 months | Most widely accepted; required by many states |
| **IERF (International Education Research Foundation)** | $200–$400 | 2–4 weeks | Faster option; accepted by some states |
| **ECE (Educational Credential Evaluators)** | $200–$350 | 2–3 weeks | Good for Canadian and UK credentials |

### CGFNS Certification Program

The most common path. Requirements:

1. **Credentials review** — transcripts from all nursing schools attended
2. **License validation** — verification from your country's nursing regulatory body
3. **English proficiency** (if required — see Step 2)
4. **CGFNS Qualifying Exam** (for some states; not the NCLEX)

**States that REQUIRE CGFNS Certification before NCLEX:**
- Arizona, California, Florida, Georgia, Illinois, Louisiana, Maryland, Michigan, Nevada, New York, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Texas, Virginia, Washington

**Tip:** Check your target state's Board of Nursing website. Requirements vary and change.

### Documents You'll Need

- Nursing diploma/degree (translated if not in English)
- Official transcripts from all nursing schools
- Current nursing license from your home country
- License verification sent directly from your regulatory body
- Passport or government ID
- Application fee

## Step 2: English Proficiency

Most states require proof of English proficiency if your nursing education was not in English.

### Accepted Tests

| Test | Minimum Score (Typical) | Cost | Validity |
|------|------------------------|------|----------|
| **TOEFL iBT** | 83 overall (speaking ≥ 26 for some states) | ~$200 | 2 years |
| **IELTS Academic** | 6.5 overall (speaking ≥ 7.0 for some states) | ~$220 | 2 years |
| **TOEIC** | Varies by state | ~$200 | 2 years |

### Exceptions (No English Test Required)

You may be exempt if:
- You completed nursing education in English (documented)
- You are from an English-speaking country (varies by state)
- You hold a current U.S. nursing license

**States with strictest English requirements:**
- California, New York, Florida, Texas

**Tip:** The speaking score matters most. Many states require higher speaking scores because nursing requires clear patient communication.

## Step 3: Choose Your State

### Why State Choice Matters

Each U.S. state has its own Board of Nursing with different:
- Credential evaluation requirements
- English proficiency thresholds
- Additional coursework requirements
- Temporary/limited license options

### Nurse Licensure Compact (NLC)

If you want to work in multiple states, apply for licensure in a **compact state** first.

**Compact states (as of 2026):** 41 states participate. Check [NLC website](https://www.nursys.com) for current list.

**Advantage:** One multistate license lets you practice in any compact state.

### Best States for International Nurses

| State | Why | Notes |
|-------|-----|-------|
| **Texas** | Large international nurse population, streamlined process | Requires CGFNS |
| **Florida** | High demand, diverse population | Strict English requirements |
| **California** | High pay, strong job market | Most stringent requirements |
| **New York** | Urban opportunities, diverse population | Requires CGFNS, strict screening |
| **Illinois** | Good job market, moderate requirements | CGFNS required |
| **Arizona** | Compact state, growing market | CGFNS required |

**Strategy:** If you plan to move, get licensed in a compact state first, then apply for licensure by endorsement in your target state.

## Step 4: Apply for Licensure by Examination

### The Process

1. **Submit application** to your chosen state's Board of Nursing
2. **Complete credential evaluation** (CGFNS or equivalent)
3. **Submit English proficiency** scores (if required)
4. **Register with Pearson VUE** for NCLEX ($200)
5. **Receive ATT (Authorization to Test)** — valid for 90 days
6. **Schedule and take NCLEX**

### Timeline

| Step | Typical Duration |
|------|-----------------|
| Credential evaluation | 2–6 months |
| Board application review | 4–12 weeks |
| ATT issuance | 2–4 weeks after approved |
| NCLEX scheduling | 1–4 weeks |
| **Total** | **4–9 months** |

**Realistic expectation:** Plan for 6–12 months from start to sitting for the NCLEX.

## Step 5: Pass the NCLEX

### What's Different for International Nurses

| Challenge | Solution |
|-----------|----------|
| Unfamiliar with U.S. healthcare system | Study U.S.-specific content: HIPAA, Scope of Practice, Nurse Practice Acts |
| Different nursing norms | Learn U.S. expectations: patient autonomy, informed consent, documentation standards |
| Test anxiety | Practice with NCLEX-style questions until comfortable |
| Language barrier | Focus on understanding question stems; eliminate answers systematically |

### U.S.-Specific Content to Study

**HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act)**
- Patient privacy rules
- What you can and cannot share
- Documentation requirements

**Scope of Practice**
- What RNs can do vs. LPNs vs. NPs
- Delegation rules (varies by state)
- When to call the provider

**Informed Consent**
- Who can give consent
- Exceptions (emergencies, minors)
- Documentation requirements

**Restraint and Seclusion**
- Strict federal rules
- Time limits
- Provider orders required

**Advance Directives**
- Living will, DPOA, DNR orders
- Patient autonomy in end-of-life care

### NGN-Specific Challenges

The Next Generation NCLEX tests clinical judgment with case studies. International nurses may find:
- **Cultural differences** in patient interactions
- **Unfamiliar scenarios** (e.g., home health, insurance issues)
- **Different documentation styles**

**Solution:** Practice with case studies that include U.S. healthcare context.

> **Our [NGN Case Study Bank](https://obiomacare.com) includes scenarios with U.S. healthcare context** — insurance questions, HIPAA scenarios, and delegation scenarios that international nurses find challenging.

## Step 6: Secure Employment & Visa

### Common Visa Options for Nurses

| Visa | Requirements | Timeline | Notes |
|------|-------------|----------|-------|
| **H-1B** | Bachelor's degree, employer sponsor | Lottery system (April) | Competitive; not guaranteed |
| **EB-3 (Green Card)** | Nursing license, labor certification | 1–3 years | Most common for nurses |
| **TN (Canada/Mexico)** | Nursing license, job offer | Days to weeks | Under USMCA (NAFTA 2.0) |
| **EB-2 NIW** | Advanced degree or exceptional ability | 1–2 years | Self-petition; no employer needed |

### The EB-3 Process (Most Common)

1. **Pass NCLEX** and get state license
2. **Find employer sponsor** (hospital, staffing agency)
3. **Employer files PERM labor certification** (proves no U.S. worker available)
4. **File I-140 petition**
5. **Wait for priority date** (varies by country — India/China have longer waits)
6. **File I-485 adjustment of status** (if in U.S.) or consular processing
7. **Receive green card**

**Current wait times:**
- Philippines: ~2–3 years
- India: ~5–8 years
- Rest of world: ~1–2 years

### Nurse Staffing Agencies

Many agencies specialize in placing international nurses:

- O'Grady Peyton (International)
- Avant Healthcare Professionals
- MedPro International
- Connetics USA

**Research carefully:** Some agencies require contracts (2–3 years) and may charge fees.

## Step 7: Adapt to U.S. Nursing Practice

### Cultural Adjustments

| Difference | What to Know |
|------------|-------------|
| **Patient autonomy** | U.S. patients have strong rights to refuse treatment. Respect this even if you disagree. |
| **Informed consent** | Detailed explanation required. Document everything. |
| **Team-based care** | RNs work with LPNs, CNAs, PT, OT, RT. Delegation is expected. |
| **Documentation** | "If it's not documented, it didn't happen." Thorough, objective charting. |
| **Time management** | High patient loads (5–8 on med-surg). Efficiency is critical. |
| **Technology** | Epic, Cerner, Meditech — electronic charting is standard. |

### Common Challenges

**1. Different Scope of Practice**
- In some countries, nurses have more autonomy (prescribing, diagnosing)
- In the U.S., scope is more restricted — know your state's Nurse Practice Act

**2. Communication Styles**
- U.S. healthcare values direct communication
- Assertiveness is expected — advocate for your patients
- SBAR is the standard handoff format

**3. Medication Names**
- Generic names are standard in U.S. practice (not brand names)
- Learn common U.S. brand names if you studied with different ones

**4. Documentation Burden**
- U.S. nurses spend significant time charting
- Learn your EHR system thoroughly
- Chart in real-time when possible

## NCLEX Practice Question (U.S. Context)

### Question 1
A nurse from the Philippines is working in a U.S. hospital. A patient with terminal cancer refuses chemotherapy, stating they want to focus on quality of life. The patient's family insists the nurse convince the patient to continue treatment. What is the nurse's best action?

A. Explain to the family that the patient has the right to refuse treatment  
B. Try to persuade the patient to continue chemotherapy for the family's sake  
C. Ask the provider to speak with the patient  
D. Tell the family to respect the patient's wishes and leave

<details>
<summary>Click for answer</summary>

**A. Explain to the family that the patient has the right to refuse treatment**

In the U.S., patient autonomy is paramount. Competent adults have the right to refuse treatment, even if family disagrees. The nurse should support the patient's decision and educate the family about this right. The provider may need to be involved, but supporting patient autonomy is the priority.

</details>
