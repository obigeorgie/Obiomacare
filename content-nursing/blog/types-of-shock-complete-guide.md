# Types of Shock: The Complete NCLEX Guide for Nursing Students

**Published:** 2026-08-11 | **Reading Time:** 12 min | **NCLEX Category:** Physiological Integrity / Reduction of Risk Potential

---

## Quick Summary

Shock is a life-threatening state of inadequate tissue perfusion. On the NCLEX, you'll need to **recognize the type**, **identify the priority intervention**, and **differentiate hemodynamic patterns** — often under pressure in a case study format.

| Type | Core Problem | Heart Rate | Skin | Key Differentiator |
|------|-------------|------------|------|-------------------|
| **Hypovolemic** | Volume loss | ↑ Tachycardia | Cool, pale | ↓ Urine output, weak pulses |
| **Cardiogenic** | Pump failure | ↑ Tachycardia | Cool, clammy | Crackles, JVD, S3 sound |
| **Septic** (distributive) | Vasodilation from infection | ↑ Tachycardia | Warm → cool | Fever, ↑ lactate, bounding pulses early |
| **Neurogenic** (distributive) | Loss of sympathetic tone | **↓ Bradycardia** | Warm, dry | **Bradycardia + hypotension** (unique!) |
| **Anaphylactic** (distributive) | IgE-mediated vasodilation | ↑ Tachycardia | Hives, edema | Stridor, rapid onset, known allergen |
| **Obstructive** | Physical blockage | ↑ Tachycardia | Cyanosis | JVD, Beck's triad, unilateral absent breath sounds |

---

## The One Thing to Remember

> **Neurogenic shock is the ONLY type with bradycardia.** Every other shock type triggers tachycardia as a compensatory response. If you see hypotension with a slow heart rate, think spinal cord injury above T6.

## 1. Hypovolemic Shock

### What's Happening
The circulatory system has lost too much fluid or blood. The heart can't pump what it doesn't have.

### Common Causes
- **Hemorrhage:** Trauma, surgery, GI bleeding, postpartum hemorrhage
- **Fluid loss:** Severe vomiting/diarrhea, burns, dehydration, diabetic ketoacidosis

### Clinical Signs
| Sign | Why It Happens |
|------|---------------|
| Tachycardia | Heart tries to compensate for low volume |
| Hypotension | Less blood volume = less pressure |
| Cool, pale skin | Peripheral vasoconstriction shunts blood to vital organs |
| ↓ Urine output (<30 mL/hr) | Kidneys try to preserve fluid |
| Weak, thready pulse | Reduced stroke volume |
| Altered mental status | Brain hypoperfusion |
| ↑ Capillary refill (>3 sec) | Sluggish peripheral perfusion |

### Nursing Priorities (In Order)
1. **Stop the bleeding** — direct pressure, tourniquet if extremity
2. **Two large-bore IVs** — 14- or 16-gauge; anticipate blood products
3. **Isotonic crystalloid bolus** — LR or 0.9% NS (20 mL/kg)
4. **Elevate legs** (modified Trendelenburg) unless spinal injury suspected
5. **Monitor I&Os closely** — goal urine output >30 mL/hr
6. **Type & crossmatch** for blood transfusion

### NCLEX Trap
Don't give **D5W** as a resuscitation fluid — it redistributes quickly and doesn't expand intravascular volume effectively.

## 2. Cardiogenic Shock

### What's Happening
The heart itself has failed as a pump — usually after a massive MI, severe arrhythmia, or end-stage heart failure.

### Common Causes
- Acute myocardial infarction (most common)
- Severe arrhythmias
- Cardiomyopathy
- Myocarditis
- Valvular dysfunction

### Clinical Signs
| Sign | Why It Happens |
|------|---------------|
| Tachycardia | Compensatory response to low cardiac output |
| Hypotension | Inadequate pump function |
| Cool, clammy skin | Peripheral vasoconstriction |
| Pulmonary crackles | Fluid backs up into lungs (left-sided failure) |
| Jugular venous distention (JVD) | Fluid backs up into venous system (right-sided failure) |
| S3 heart sound | Volume overload, ventricular dysfunction |
| Chest pain | Ongoing ischemia |
| Anxiety, restlessness | Hypoperfusion of brain |

### Nursing Priorities (In Order)
1. **Oxygen** — 2-4 L NC, titrate to SpO₂ >92%
2. **EKG monitoring** — continuous telemetry
3. **Positioning** — elevate head of bed (reduces pulmonary congestion)
4. **Inotropes/vasopressors** — dobutamine, milrinone, dopamine (per orders)
5. **Diuretics** — if fluid overloaded (furosemide)
6. **Adequate pain relief** — reduces myocardial oxygen demand
7. **Prepare for interventions** — possible balloon pump, emergent cath lab

### Critical NCLEX Point
**Do NOT give aggressive IV fluids in cardiogenic shock.** Unlike hypovolemic shock, the problem is pump failure, not volume loss. Extra fluid worsens pulmonary edema. If fluids are given at all, they're given cautiously and with hemodynamic monitoring.

## 3. Septic Shock

### What's Happening
A severe infection triggers a dysregulated inflammatory response, causing massive vasodilation and capillary leak. Blood pressure drops despite normal or increased cardiac output initially.

### Common Causes
- Pneumonia
- Urinary tract infection (pyelonephritis)
- Abdominal infection (peritonitis, appendicitis)
- Wound infections
- Meningitis
- Catheter-associated infections

### Clinical Signs — Two Phases

#### Warm Phase (Early)
| Sign | Explanation |
|------|-------------|
| Fever or hypothermia | Dysregulated temperature control |
| Flushed, warm skin | Peripheral vasodilation |
| Bounding pulses | High cardiac output state |
| Tachycardia | Compensatory |
| Normal or slightly low BP | Early compensation |

#### Cold Phase (Late — Progression)
| Sign | Explanation |
|------|-------------|
| Cool, clammy skin | Peripheral vasoconstriction as compensation fails |
| Hypotension (profound) | Refractory to fluids |
| Altered mental status | Cerebral hypoperfusion |
| Oliguria/anuria | Renal failure |
| Lactic acidosis (>2 mmol/L) | Cellular hypoxia, anaerobic metabolism |

### Nursing Priorities (In Order)
1. **Blood cultures x2 BEFORE antibiotics** — ideally within 30 min of recognition
2. **Broad-spectrum antibiotics within 1 hour** — every hour of delay increases mortality
3. **Aggressive fluid resuscitation** — 30 mL/kg crystalloid bolus (usually 1.5-3 L in adults)
4. **Vasopressors if fluid-refractory** — norepinephrine (Levophed) is first-line
5. **Monitor lactate** — trending down = good response
6. **Source control** — remove infected lines, drain abscesses, debride wounds

### Sepsis Bundle (Know This for NCLEX)
The Surviving Sepsis Campaign bundle — all within 3 hours:
- Measure lactate
- Obtain blood cultures
- Administer broad-spectrum antibiotics
- Give 30 mL/kg crystalloid for hypotension or lactate ≥4

## 4. Neurogenic Shock

### What's Happening
A spinal cord injury (usually above T6) damages sympathetic pathways. Without sympathetic tone, blood vessels dilate massively and the heart can't increase rate.

### Common Causes
- Spinal cord trauma (MVC, falls, sports injuries)
- Spinal anesthesia (high level)
- Guillain-Barré syndrome (rare)

### Clinical Signs — The Pattern That Stands Out
| Sign | Why It's Different |
|------|-------------------|
| **Bradycardia** | Loss of sympathetic input to heart — the SA node slows |
| Hypotension | Massive vasodilation |
| Warm, dry skin below injury | No sympathetic-mediated vasoconstriction |
| Poikilothermia | Can't regulate temperature below injury level |
| Flaccid paralysis | Loss of motor function |
| Absent reflexes below injury | Cord transection |

### The Key Differentiator
> **Neurogenic shock = hypotension + bradycardia + warm dry skin**
>
> Every other shock type: hypotension + tachycardia + cool skin

### Nursing Priorities (In Order)
1. **Vasopressors** — phenylephrine, norepinephrine, dopamine to maintain perfusion
2. **Atropine** — for symptomatic bradycardia (HR <50 with hypotension)
3. **Spinal precautions** — maintain alignment, log-roll
4. **Temperature management** — prevent hypothermia (can't vasoconstrict to conserve heat)
5. **Monitor for autonomic dysreflexia** — sudden severe hypertension, bradycardia, headache (occurs later, injury above T6)

## 5. Anaphylactic Shock

### What's Happening
A severe IgE-mediated allergic reaction causes massive vasodilation, increased capillary permeability, and bronchoconstriction. It progresses rapidly — minutes to hours after exposure.

### Common Triggers
- Foods: peanuts, tree nuts, shellfish, eggs, milk
- Medications: penicillin, sulfa, NSAIDs, contrast dye
- Insect stings: bees, wasps, fire ants
- Latex

### Clinical Signs
| System | Signs |
|--------|-------|
| **Airway** | Angioedema, stridor, hoarseness, sense of impending doom |
| **Breathing** | Wheezing, bronchospasm, tachypnea, respiratory distress |
| **Circulation** | Hypotension, tachycardia, weak pulses, syncope |
| **Skin** | Urticaria (hives), pruritus, flushing, angioedema |
| **GI** | Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal cramps |

### Nursing Priorities (In Order)
1. **EPINEPHRINE IM immediately** — 0.3-0.5 mg of 1:1000 in lateral thigh (first-line, no exceptions)
2. **Call for help** — this is an emergency
3. **Airway management** — oxygen, possible intubation if stridor progresses
4. **Remove the trigger** — if still present
5. **IV access + fluids** — crystalloid bolus for hypotension
6. **Adjunct medications** — H1 blocker (diphenhydramine), H2 blocker (famotidine), corticosteroids (methylprednisolone — for prolonged reaction, not immediate)
7. **Monitor for biphasic reaction** — can recur 4-8 hours later

### NCLEX Critical Point
> **Never delay epinephrine to give antihistamines or steroids first.** Epinephrine is the only drug that reverses vasodilation and bronchospasm. Antihistamines treat only hives/itching — not the life-threatening components.

## 6. Obstructive Shock

### What's Happening
Something is physically blocking blood flow or preventing the heart from filling. The heart itself may be fine, but it can't pump effectively.

### Common Causes
| Cause | Mechanism |
|-------|-----------|
| **Pulmonary embolism** | Blockage in pulmonary artery → right heart can't pump to lungs |
| **Cardiac tamponade** | Fluid in pericardium compresses heart, prevents filling |
| **Tension pneumothorax** | Air trapped in pleural space shifts mediastinum, compresses vena cava |
| **Severe aortic stenosis** | Outflow obstruction |

### Clinical Signs by Cause

#### Cardiac Tamponade — Beck's Triad (HIGH YIELD)
1. **Hypotension** — reduced cardiac output
2. **Jugular venous distention** — blood backs up into venous system
3. **Muffled heart sounds** — fluid dampens heart sounds

Plus: **Pulsus paradoxus** — exaggerated drop in systolic BP during inspiration (>10 mmHg)

#### Tension Pneumothorax
- Severe respiratory distress
- Tracheal deviation **away** from affected side
- Absent breath sounds on affected side
- Distended neck veins
- Tympanic percussion on affected side
- **This is a needle decompression emergency**

#### Massive Pulmonary Embolism
- Sudden onset dyspnea, tachypnea
- Pleuritic chest pain
- Tachycardia, hypotension
- Hemoptysis
- Loud P2 (pulmonic valve closure)
- **Prepare for thrombolytics or embolectomy**

### Nursing Priorities (In Order)
1. **Treat the obstruction**
   - Tamponade → pericardiocentesis (emergent)
   - Tension pneumo → needle decompression (2nd intercostal space, midclavicular line)
   - PE → anticoagulation, possible thrombolytics
2. **Oxygen + support**
3. **IV access + fluids** — careful, small boluses
4. **Prepare for definitive intervention**

## Quick Comparison Table

| Feature | Hypovolemic | Cardiogenic | Septic | Neurogenic | Anaphylactic | Obstructive |
|---------|-------------|-------------|--------|------------|--------------|-------------|
| **Primary Problem** | Volume loss | Pump failure | Vasodilation + infection | Loss of sympathetic tone | IgE-mediated allergy | Physical blockage |
| **Heart Rate** | ↑ | ↑ | ↑ | **↓** | ↑ | ↑ |
| **Blood Pressure** | ↓ | ↓ | ↓ | ↓ | ↓ | ↓ |
| **Skin** | Cool, pale | Cool, clammy | Warm → cool | Warm, dry | Hives, flushed | Cyanosis |
| **Unique Sign** | ↓ UOP, weak pulses | Crackles, JVD, S3 | Fever, ↑ lactate | Bradycardia | Stridor, angioedema | JVD, Beck's triad |
| **First-Line Treatment** | Fluids, blood | Inotropes, O₂ | Antibiotics + fluids | Vasopressors | **Epinephrine** | Remove obstruction |
| **Fluid Strategy** | Aggressive resuscitation | Cautious/avoid | Aggressive 30 mL/kg | Vasopressors first | Supportive | Small boluses |

## Mnemonic: "SHOCK-ED" Assessment

Use this for rapid bedside assessment:

- **S** — Skin: color, temperature, moisture
- **H** — Heart rate & rhythm
- **O** — Output: urine, mental status
- **C** — Capillary refill, CRT
- **K** — Keep O₂ on, monitor SpO₂
- **E** — Evaluate BP trends
- **D** — Determine type based on pattern

## NCLEX Practice Questions

### Question 1
A patient is admitted after a motor vehicle collision. Vital signs: BP 78/52, HR 128, RR 24, SpO₂ 94% on room air. Skin is cool and pale. Urine output is 15 mL in the past hour. What type of shock does the nurse suspect?

<details>
<summary>Click for answer</summary>

**Hypovolemic shock.** Classic presentation: trauma + tachycardia + hypotension + cool pale skin + oliguria. Priority: two large-bore IVs, isotonic fluid bolus, type & cross for blood.

</details>

### Question 2
A patient with a T4 spinal cord injury has BP 82/56 and HR 52. The skin below the injury is warm and dry. Which intervention should the nurse prepare first?

<details>
<summary>Click for answer</summary>

**Vasopressors.** This is neurogenic shock — the bradycardia is the key differentiator. All other shock types cause tachycardia. Priority: vasopressors to maintain perfusion; atropine if symptomatic bradycardia persists.

</details>

### Question 3
A patient with a known bee allergy was stung 5 minutes ago and presents with stridor, urticaria, and BP 68/40. What is the absolute priority nursing action?

<details>
<summary>Click for answer</summary>

**Administer epinephrine IM immediately.** This is anaphylactic shock. Epinephrine is first-line — it reverses bronchospasm and supports BP. Do not delay for other medications or assessments.

</details>

### Question 4 (NGN-Style Case Study)
A 67-year-old patient was admitted for pneumonia 3 days ago. Over the past 6 hours, the nurse notes: temp 38.9°C (102°F), HR 118, RR 28, BP 88/52, SpO₂ 89% on 2L NC. The patient is confused and agitated. Lactate is 4.2 mmol/L.

**Which actions should the nurse take?** (Select all that apply)

- [ ] A. Increase oxygen to maintain SpO₂ >92%
- [ ] B. Obtain blood cultures
- [ ] C. Administer acetaminophen for fever
- [ ] D. Begin fluid resuscitation with 30 mL/kg crystalloid
- [ ] E. Notify provider for antibiotic orders
- [ ] F. Place patient in Trendelenburg position

<details>
<summary>Click for answer</summary>

**Correct: A, B, D, E**

This is septic shock. The lactate of 4.2 + hypotension + fever + suspected infection meets sepsis criteria.
- **A.** Yes — SpO₂ 89% is too low; titrate O₂
- **B.** Yes — blood cultures before antibiotics (sepsis bundle)
- **C.** No — fever workup first; acetaminophen masks fever trend and isn't a priority in shock
- **D.** Yes — 30 mL/kg crystalloid is the standard resuscitation volume
- **E.** Yes — antibiotics within 1 hour is critical; provider must order them
- **F.** No — Trendelenburg is not evidence-based for shock and may impair breathing; supine or head elevated is preferred

</details>

## How This Connects to Clinical Judgment (NGN)

The NCSBN Clinical Judgment Measurement Model is exactly what you use at the bedside with a patient in shock:

1. **Recognize Cues** — Tachycardia + hypotension + cool skin = shock. Bradycardia instead? Think neurogenic.
2. **Analyze Cues** — Is this volume loss (hypovolemic), pump failure (cardiogenic), vasodilation (septic/neurogenic/anaphylactic), or obstruction?
3. **Prioritize Hypotheses** — What's the most life-threatening issue right now?
4. **Generate Solutions** — Fluids? Epinephrine? Antibiotics? Surgery?
5. **Take Action** — Execute the priority intervention first
6. **Evaluate Outcomes** — Did BP improve? Is urine output >30 mL/hr? Trending lactate down?
