# Fluids and Electrolytes: NCLEX Deep Dive ## Introduction Fluids and electrolytes are the foundation of physiologic stability. The NCLEX tests this constantly — not because the numbers are hard, but because context changes everything. A potassium of 3.2 means one thing in a stable patient and something completely different in a patient on digoxin. This guide covers the high-yield electrolytes, acid-base balance, and fluid management — with the clinical reasoning the NGN demands. --- ## Sodium (Na+) — 135-145 mEq/L ### Hyponatremia (<135 mEq/L) **Causes (SALT LOSS mnemonic):** - **S**yndrome of Inappropriate ADH (SIADH) - **A**drenal insufficiency (Addison's) - **L**oop diuretics - **T**hiazide diuretics - **L**iver cirrhosis - **O**verload (fluid overload — dilutional) - **S**S (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors) - **S**weating, vomiting, diarrhea **Symptoms:** - <130: Nausea, malaise, headache - <125: Confusion, lethargy, cramps - <120: Seizures, coma, respiratory arrest **NCLEX key point:** Rate of correction matters. Too fast → osmotic demyelination syndrome (central pontine myelinolysis). **Correction rate:** - Acute (<48 hours): 1-2 mEq/L/hour, max 8-12 mEq/L/day - Chronic (>48 hours): 0.5 mEq/L/hour, max 6-8 mEq/L/day **Treatment by cause:** - Hypovolemic hyponatremia: NS or LR (restore volume) - Euvolemic (SIADH): Fluid restriction (800-1200 mL/day), salt tablets - Hypervolemic: Fluid restriction, diuretics, treat underlying cause --- ### Hypernatremia (>145 mEq/L) **Causes:** - Dehydration (most common) - Diabetes insipidus - Excessive Na+ intake (IV fluids, TPN) - Cushing's syndrome **Symptoms:** - Thirst (early, reliable sign) - Restlessness, weakness - Seizures, coma (severe) **Treatment:** - Hypovolemic: D5W or hypotonic saline (slowly!) - Correction rate: 0.5 mEq/L/hour, max 8-12 mEq/L/day - Diabetes insipidus: Desmopressin (DDAVP) **NCLEX key point:** Hypernatremia usually indicates water deficit, not sodium excess. Give water back slowly. --- ## Potassium (K+) — 3.5-5.0 mEq/L ### Hypokalemia (<3.5 mEq/L) **Causes (MURDER mnemonic):** - **M**edications (diuretics, insulin, beta-agonists) - **U**rine loss (diuretics, aldosteronism) - **R**eplenishment inadequate (poor intake) - **D**iarrhea, vomiting, NG suction - **E**ndocrine (hyperaldosteronism, Cushing's) - **R**enal losses (RTA, diuretics) **Symptoms:** - Muscle weakness, cramps - Constipation, ileus - Arrhythmias (PVCs, flat T waves, U waves on ECG) - Paralysis (severe) **ECG changes:** - Flat T waves - ST depression - Prominent U waves - PVCs, VTach risk **Treatment:** - Oral replacement: 40-80 mEq/day (divided doses) - IV replacement: 10-20 mEq/hour (peripheral line, max 10 mEq/hour) - Never give IV push — cardiac arrest risk - Use cardiac monitor during IV replacement **NCLEX scenario:** > "A patient on digoxin has K+ 3.2 and PVCs on monitor. What is the FIRST action?" **Answer:** Replace potassium. Digoxin toxicity risk increases with hypokalemia. Potassium and digoxin compete for the same binding site on the Na+/K+ ATPase pump. Low K+ = more digoxin binding = toxicity. --- ### Hyperkalemia (>5.0 mEq/L) **Causes (MURDER reversed — think opposite):** - **R**enal failure (most common) - **T**issue breakdown (rhabdomyolysis, hemolysis, tumor lysis) - **A**cidosis (H+ shifts into cells, K+ shifts out) - **M**edications (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, K+-sparing diuretics, NSAIDs) - **P**otassium supplements/foods - **H**ypoaldosteronism (Addison's, Type IV RTA) **Symptoms:** - Muscle weakness, paralysis - Paresthesias - Cardiac arrest (peaked T waves → widened QRS → sine wave → VFib) **ECG progression (memorize this):** 1. Peaked T waves (tenting) 2. Prolonged PR interval 3. Widened QRS 4. Sine wave pattern 5. Ventricular fibrillation **Treatment (memorize the sequence):** | Priority | Treatment | Mechanism | |----------|-----------|-----------| | 1st | Calcium gluconate (1 g IV) | Stabilizes cardiac membrane (minutes) | | 2nd | Insulin + D50 (10U regular + 50mL D50) | Shifts K+ into cells (15-30 min) | | 3rd | Albuterol nebulizer | Shifts K+ into cells (15-30 min) | | 4th | Sodium bicarbonate (if acidotic) | Shifts K+ into cells | | 5th | Kayexalate (sodium polystyrene) | Removes K+ via GI (hours) | | 6th | Dialysis | Removes K+ directly (definitive) | **NCLEX scenario:** > "A patient with ESRD has K+ 7.2. ECG shows peaked T waves and widened QRS. What is the FIRST medication?" **Answer:** Calcium gluconate. It does NOT lower potassium — it protects the heart from lethal arrhythmias. Then insulin + D50 to shift K+ into cells. --- ## Calcium (Ca2+) — 8.5-10.5 mg/dL (ionized: 4.5-5.5 mg/dL) ### Hypocalcemia (<8.5 mg/dL) **Causes (CATS mnemonic):** - **C**itrate (massive transfusion) - **A**cute pancreatitis - **T**umor lysis - **S**urgery (parathyroidectomy) - Hypoparathyroidism - Vitamin D deficiency - Renal failure **Symptoms:** - Paresthesias (perioral, fingers) - Muscle cramps, tetany - Chvostek's sign (facial twitch with tap) - Trousseau's sign (carpal spasm with BP cuff) - Seizures - Prolonged QT interval (ECG) **Treatment:** - Acute: Calcium gluconate or calcium chloride IV - Chronic: Calcium carbonate + vitamin D **NCLEX tip:** Always check albumin with calcium. Low albumin = falsely low total calcium. Formula: Corrected Ca = Measured Ca + 0.8 × (4.0 - Albumin). --- ### Hypercalcemia (>10.5 mg/dL) **Causes (mnemonic: Stones, Bones, Groans, Thrones, Psychiatric Overtones):** - **Stones:** Kidney stones - **Bones:** Bone metastases, multiple myeloma - **Groans:** Constipation, abdominal pain, peptic ulcers - **Thrones:** Polyuria (thrones = toilet) - **Psychiatric:** Confusion, lethargy, depression **Treatment:** - Hydration: NS at 200-300 mL/hour (mobilize calcium) - Loop diuretics (after hydration) - Calcitonin (fast onset, short duration) - Bisphosphonates (slow onset, long duration) - Dialysis (if refractory) --- ## Magnesium (Mg2+) — 1.7-2.2 mg/dL ### Hypomagnesemia (<1.7 mg/dL) **Causes:** - Chronic alcoholism (most common) - Diuretics - Diarrhea - PPIs (long-term use) - Aminoglycosides **Symptoms:** - Similar to hypocalcemia (tetany, Chvostek's, Trousseau's) - Arrhythmias (VTach, Torsades de pointes) - Hypokalemia and hypocalcemia (Mg required for K and Ca metabolism) **Treatment:** - Oral: Magnesium oxide - IV: Magnesium sulfate 1-2 g over 15-60 minutes - Monitor for hypotension and bradycardia during infusion **NCLEX key point:** Hypomagnesemia causes refractory hypokalemia and hypocalcemia. Must replete Mg first. --- ### Hypermagnesemia (>2.2 mg/dL) **Causes:** - Renal failure - Excessive antacids or laxatives - Magnesium sulfate (preeclampsia treatment) **Symptoms:** - Flushing, hypotension - Nausea, vomiting - Muscle weakness - Bradycardia, heart block - Respiratory depression - Coma (severe) **Treatment:** - Calcium gluconate (antagonizes Mg) - Hydration + loop diuretics - Dialysis if severe **NCLEX scenario:** > "A patient receiving magnesium sulfate for preeclampsia has RR 10, absent DTRs, and flushing. What is the FIRST action?" **Answer:** Stop magnesium sulfate. These are signs of toxicity. Antidote is calcium gluconate. Monitor for respiratory depression. --- ## Phosphorus (PO4) — 2.5-4.5 mg/dL ### Hypophosphatemia (<2.5 mg/dL) **Causes:** - Refeeding syndrome (most important) - Alcohol withdrawal - Diuretics - Respiratory alkalosis **Symptoms:** - Muscle weakness (respiratory and cardiac) - Hemolysis - Rhabdomyolysis - Confusion **Treatment:** Oral or IV phosphorus replacement --- ### Hyperphosphatemia (>4.5 mg/dL) **Causes:** - Renal failure (most common) - Tumor lysis syndrome - Excessive intake **Symptoms:** - Hypocalcemia (phosphate binds calcium) - Soft tissue calcification **Treatment:** Calcium acetate, sevelamer, dietary restriction --- ## Acid-Base Balance ### The Big Four | Disorder | pH | PaCO2 | HCO3 | Primary Problem | |----------|-----|-------|------|-----------------| | Respiratory Acidosis | ↓ | ↑ | Normal/↑ | Hypoventilation | | Respiratory Alkalosis | ↑ | ↓ | Normal/↓ | Hyperventilation | | Metabolic Acidosis | ↓ | Normal/↓ | ↓ | HCO3 loss or acid gain | | Metabolic Alkalosis | ↑ | Normal/↑ | ↑ | HCO3 gain or acid loss | ### ROME Method - **R**espiratory **O**pposite: pH and PaCO2 move in opposite directions - **M**etabolic **E**qual: pH and HCO3 move in the same direction --- ### Metabolic Acidosis **Anion Gap = Na - (Cl + HCO3)** - Normal: 8-12 mEq/L - High anion gap: >12 - Normal anion gap: 8-12 **High Anion Gap (MUDPILES):** - **M**ethanol - **U**remia - **D**KA - **P**ropylene glycol, paraldehyde - **I**ron, INH - **L**actic acidosis - **E**thylene glycol - **S**alicylates **Normal Anion Gap (HARDASS):** - **H**yperalimentation - **A**cetazolamide - **R**enal tubular acidosis - **D**iarrhea - **A**ddison's - **S**pironolactone - **S**aline infusion **Compensation:** - For every 1 mEq/L decrease in HCO3, PaCO2 decreases by 1.2 mmHg - Winter's formula: Expected PaCO2 = 1.5 × HCO3 + 8 ± 2 **Treatment:** - Treat underlying cause - Sodium bicarbonate if pH < 7.1 and hemodynamically unstable --- ### Metabolic Alkalosis **Causes:** - Vomiting/NG suction (HCl loss) - Diuretics (volume contraction) - Steroids - Antacids - Excessive bicarbonate administration **Treatment:** - Chloride-responsive: NS (give Cl back) - Chloride-resistant: Treat underlying cause (hyperaldosteronism) - Acetazolamide (if diuretic-induced) --- ### Respiratory Acidosis **Causes:** - CNS depression (opioids, brain injury) - Neuromuscular disease - COPD, asthma - Chest wall disorders - Acute airway obstruction **Compensation:** - Acute: HCO3 increases 1 mEq/L for every 10 mmHg increase in PaCO2 - Chronic: HCO3 increases 3.5-4 mEq/L for every 10 mmHg increase **Treatment:** - Improve ventilation (treat underlying cause) - Mechanical ventilation if severe - NEVER give sodium bicarbonate in respiratory acidosis (worsens CO2 retention) --- ### Respiratory Alkalosis **Causes:** - Anxiety/hyperventilation - Pain - Fever - Hypoxemia - CNS stimulation - Early sepsis - Salicylate toxicity **Treatment:** - Treat underlying cause - Rebreathing mask for anxiety (rarely needed) - Correct hypoxemia --- ## Fluid Management ### Fluid Compartments | Compartment | Percentage of Body Weight | |-------------|--------------------------| | Total body water | 60% (50% elderly, 70% infants) | - Intracellular (ICF) | 40% | - Extracellular (ECF) | 20% | - Interstitial | 15% | - Intravascular (plasma) | 5% | ### Types of IV Fluids | Fluid | Tonicity | Use | |-------|----------|-----| | D5W | Isotonic (becomes hypotonic) | Free water deficit, hypernatremia | | 0.9% NS | Isotonic | Volume expansion, hyponatremia | | LR | Isotonic | Trauma, surgery, burns (most physiologic) | | 0.45% NS | Hypotonic | Cellular dehydration | | 3% NS | Hypertonic | Severe hyponatremia (careful!) | | D5NS | Hypertonic | Maintenance with some calories | **NCLEX tip:** LR contains potassium (4 mEq/L) and lactate. Avoid in hyperkalemia and liver failure (can't metabolize lactate). --- ### Fluid Volume Deficit (Hypovolemia) **Signs:** - Orthostatic hypotension (first sign) - Tachycardia - Decreased skin turgor - Dry mucous membranes - Decreased urine output (<30 mL/hr) - Flat neck veins - Confusion (severe) **Treatment:** - Mild (3-5%): Oral rehydration - Moderate (6-9%): IV crystalloids (NS or LR) - Severe (>10%): Aggressive IV fluids, possible blood products --- ### Fluid Volume Excess (Hypervolemia) **Signs:** - Weight gain - Edema (peripheral, pulmonary) - JVD - Hypertension - Crackles on auscultation - Decreased BUN/creatinine ratio **Treatment:** - Diuretics - Fluid restriction - Sodium restriction - Treat underlying cause --- ## NCLEX Practice Questions **Question 1:** > "A patient with DKA has pH 7.25, PaCO2 28, HCO3 14. What type of acid-base disorder is present?" A) Respiratory acidosis B) Metabolic acidosis with respiratory compensation C) Metabolic alkalosis D) Respiratory alkalosis **Answer: B** — Low pH, low HCO3 = metabolic acidosis. PaCO2 is low (compensatory hyperventilation). ROME method: Metabolic = equal direction (both pH and HCO3 are low). --- **Question 2:** > "A patient on diuretics has K+ 3.0. ECG shows ST depression and U waves. Which action is PRIORITY?" A) Increase diuretic dose B) Start potassium replacement C) Check magnesium level D) All of the above **Answer: B** — Potassium replacement is priority. But also check C — hypomagnesemia often accompanies hypokalemia and must be repleted for K+ to normalize. A is wrong — would worsen hypokalemia. --- **Question 3:** > "A patient with renal failure has K+ 6.8. ECG shows peaked T waves. What is the FIRST medication?" A) Insulin and D50 B) Calcium gluconate C) Kayexalate D) Sodium bicarbonate **Answer: B** — Calcium gluconate first (cardioprotection). Then insulin + D50 (shifts K+ into cells). Kayexalate takes hours. Bicarbonate if acidotic. --- **Question 4:** > "A postoperative patient has the following ABG: pH 7.52, PaCO2 48, HCO3 38. What is the interpretation?" A) Respiratory alkalosis B) Metabolic alkalosis with respiratory compensation C) Combined respiratory and metabolic alkalosis D) Metabolic acidosis **Answer: B** — pH high (alkalosis), HCO3 high (metabolic). PaCO2 is elevated (compensatory hypoventilation). ROME: Metabolic = equal direction (both pH and HCO3 elevated). --- ## Summary Fluids and electrolytes are about context and relationships. **Key relationships to know:** 1. Na+ and water — osmolality and volume status 2. K+ and pH — acidosis shifts K+ out of cells 3. Ca2+ and Mg2+ — hypomagnesemia causes hypocalcemia 4. Ca2+ and PO4 — inverse relationship 5. HCO3 and PaCO2 — compensation in acid-base disorders **The NCLEX tests:** - Recognition of abnormal values - Understanding of clinical significance - Prioritization of interventions - Knowledge of replacement protocols Master the mnemonics (SALT LOSS, MURDER, MUDPILES, ROME) and the clinical scenarios, and these questions become automatic. --- **Practice:** Test your knowledge with interactive fluid and electrolyte cases at [Obioma Care Case Engine](/case-engine.html) — free, no signup. *Written by Nnamdi Georgie, RN. 15 years emergency nursing experience with extensive critical care background.*
