Genetics & Genomics in Nursing

Inheritance patterns, genetic disorders, pharmacogenomics, genetic testing, and nursing implications for the NCLEX.

Inheritance Patterns

PatternDescriptionExamples
Autosomal DominantOne mutated allele needed; affected parent has 50% chance per pregnancy; M=F; vertical transmissionHuntington's disease, Marfan syndrome, neurofibromatosis type 1, familial hypercholesterolemia, polycystic kidney disease (adult), BRCA1/2 (hereditary breast/ovarian cancer)
Autosomal RecessiveBoth alleles must be mutated; carriers asymptomatic; 25% risk if both parents carriers; M=F; horizontal transmissionCystic fibrosis, sickle cell disease, Tay-Sachs, phenylketonuria (PKU), galactosemia, albinism
X-Linked RecessiveMutation on X chromosome; males affected (XY), females carriers (XX); no male-to-male transmissionHemophilia A & B, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, fragile X syndrome, red-green color blindness
X-Linked DominantMutation on X chromosome; both males and females affected; males more severely affectedFragile X syndrome (can be dominant in females), hypophosphatemic rickets, Rett syndrome (mostly females — lethal in males)
MitochondrialMaternal inheritance only; all offspring of affected mother inherit; variable expressionMELAS, MERRF, Leber hereditary optic neuropathy, mitochondrial myopathy
Mnemonic: "Dominant = DAD can pass to ALL" — Autosomal dominant affects males and females equally and can be passed from father to son (unlike X-linked).

Common Genetic Disorders

Cystic Fibrosis (CF) — Autosomal Recessive

Mutation: CFTR gene (chromosome 7) → defective chloride transport → thick, sticky secretions

Manifestations:
  • Pulmonary: Chronic cough, thick sputum, bronchiectasis, recurrent pneumonia (Pseudomonas, Staph aureus, Burkholderia cepacia), hemoptysis, pneumothorax
  • GI: Pancreatic insufficiency (malabsorption of fat and fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, K), meconium ileus in newborns, distal intestinal obstruction syndrome (DIOS), rectal prolapse, liver disease
  • Other: Salty-tasting skin, male infertility (absent vas deferens), delayed puberty, CF-related diabetes
Nursing Priority: Airway clearance (chest physiotherapy, oscillating positive expiratory pressure device, high-frequency chest wall oscillation). Pancreatic enzyme replacement with meals/snacks. High-calorie, high-fat diet (unlike general population). Vitamin supplementation. Infection control (avoid cross-contamination between CF patients). Newborn screening.

Sickle Cell Disease — Autosomal Recessive

Mutation: HBB gene → hemoglobin S (HbS) → red blood cells sickle under low oxygen, dehydration, cold, infection, stress

Manifestations:
  • Vaso-occlusive crisis (pain crisis): Most common — severe bone pain (back, chest, extremities), dactylitis in children (hand-foot syndrome)
  • Acute chest syndrome: Chest pain, fever, cough, hypoxia, pulmonary infiltrates — leading cause of death
  • Splenic sequestration: Sudden massive pooling in spleen → anemia, shock — EMERGENCY in children
  • Aplastic crisis: Parvovirus B19 infection → transient RBC aplasia
  • Stroke: Children at risk — transcranial Doppler screening
  • Chronic: Jaundice, gallstones, avascular necrosis, leg ulcers, priapism, retinopathy, nephropathy
Nursing Priority: Pain management (opioids — do NOT undertreat). Hydration (IV fluids). Oxygen if hypoxemic. Infection prevention (penicillin prophylaxis until age 5, immunizations). Hydroxyurea (increases HbF, reduces crises). Blood transfusions for severe anemia or stroke prevention. Stem cell transplant is only cure.
NCLEX Trap: Sickle cell trait (carrier, HbAS) is generally asymptomatic. Sickle cell disease (HbSS) causes symptoms. Also, avoid cold, dehydration, and high altitude — all precipitate sickling. Supplemental oxygen does NOT reverse sickling, only prevents further sickling.

Huntington's Disease — Autosomal Dominant

Mutation: HTT gene (chromosome 4) → CAG trinucleotide repeat expansion. Anticipation: worse in successive generations.

Manifestations:
  • Motor: Chorea (involuntary dance-like movements), dystonia, rigidity (late), bradykinesia
  • Cognitive: Executive dysfunction, impaired judgment, slowed processing, dementia
  • Psychiatric: Depression, apathy, irritability, anxiety, suicide risk

Average onset: 30–50 years. Fatal 15–20 years after onset.

Nursing Priority: Genetic counseling (50% risk to offspring). Pre-symptomatic testing available but psychologically complex. Safety (chorea causes falls). Swallowing precautions (aspiration risk). Psychiatric support. Advance care planning early. Tetrabenazine or deutetrabenazine for chorea.

Pharmacogenomics

Gene/EnzymeDrug AffectedClinical Impact
CYP2D6Codeine, tramadol, tamoxifen, many beta-blockers, antidepressants, antipsychoticsPoor metabolizers: codeine ineffective (can't convert to morphine). Ultra-rapid metabolizers: toxicity from "normal" codeine doses. FDA black box warning for codeine in children post-tonsillectomy (ultra-rapid metabolizers)
CYP2C19Clopidogrel, omeprazole, diazepam, phenytoinPoor metabolizers: clopidogrel less effective (can't convert to active form). May need alternative antiplatelet (prasugrel, ticagrelor)

HLA-B*5701Abacavir (HIV)Positive test = hypersensitivity reaction risk — do NOT give abacavir
HLA-B*1502CarbamazepineAsian ancestry positive = Stevens-Johnson syndrome risk — avoid carbamazepine

NCLEX Trap: Codeine effectiveness depends on CYP2D6 conversion to morphine. Poor metabolizers get NO pain relief. Ultra-rapid metabolizers get TOO MUCH morphine — especially dangerous in children (respiratory depression). Tramadol has similar issues.

Genetic Testing & Counseling

Types of Genetic Testing

  • Diagnostic: Confirms diagnosis when symptoms present (CF, Huntington's)
  • Predictive/Presymptomatic: Tests asymptomatic at-risk individuals (Huntington's, BRCA1/2)
  • Carrier: Identifies carriers of recessive conditions (CF, sickle cell, Tay-Sachs)
  • Prenatal: CVS, amniocentesis, cell-free DNA screening
  • Pharmacogenomic: Guides medication selection and dosing
  • Newborn screening: PKU, CF, sickle cell, hypothyroidism, galactosemia
Genetic Counseling Nursing Role: Provide emotional support. Ensure informed consent (voluntary, understanding of implications). Protect genetic privacy (GINA protects against health insurance and employment discrimination but NOT life, disability, or long-term care insurance). Refer to genetic counselor. Address family implications. Respect autonomy — patient can decline testing.

Clinical Judgment Case Study

Genetics BRCA

Case: Genetic Testing Decision

A 35-year-old female requests BRCA1/2 testing after her mother and maternal aunt were diagnosed with breast cancer at ages 42 and 45. She is anxious and wants to know if she should have prophylactic surgery.

What is the nurse's PRIORITY action?

  1. Refer to genetic counseling before testing
  2. Schedule the BRCA test immediately since she is high-risk
  3. Discuss prophylactic mastectomy options with the surgeon
  4. Reassure her that early detection is most important
Answer: 1 — Refer to genetic counseling before testing.

Genetic testing has profound psychological, medical, and insurance implications. Pre-test genetic counseling is standard of care — it ensures informed consent, discusses implications for family members, addresses insurance discrimination concerns (GINA protects against health insurance discrimination but not life/disability), and prepares the patient for possible results. Surgery decisions come AFTER testing and counseling.
