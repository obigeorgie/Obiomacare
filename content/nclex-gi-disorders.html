NCLEX GI Disorders: Complete Study Guide

Peptic ulcer, IBD, liver disease, pancreatitis, and appendicitis — pathophysiology, nursing priorities, and NCLEX traps.

1. Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD)

📋 Gastric vs. Duodenal Ulcer

FeatureGastric UlcerDuodenal Ulcer
Pain patternPain WITH eating (food irritates ulcer)Pain 2-3 hours AFTER eating (acid without food buffer)
H. pyloriLess commonVery common
NSAID riskHighLower
Malignancy riskHigher — always biopsyAlmost never malignant
HemorrhageGastric bleeding (coffee-ground emesis)Duodenal bleeding (melena)
✅ Nursing Priorities:
• NPO or bland diet (no caffeine, alcohol, NSAIDs)
• PPIs (omeprazole) — first line
• H. pylori triple therapy (PPI + clarithromycin + amoxicillin)
• Monitor for bleeding (hematemesis, melena, hypotension)
• Test for H. pylori (stool antigen, urea breath test, biopsy)
🚨 NCLEX Trap: For gastric ulcers, pain occurs WITH eating — patients may lose weight from avoiding food. For duodenal ulcers, pain is relieved by eating — patients may GAIN weight.

2. Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD)

📋 Crohn's Disease vs. Ulcerative Colitis

FeatureCrohn's DiseaseUlcerative Colitis
LocationAnywhere (mouth to anus) — skip lesionsColon and rectum only — continuous
DepthTransmural (full thickness)Mucosal and submucosal only
StoolFatty, foul-smelling steatorrheaBloody mucus-filled diarrhea
ComplicationsFistulas, strictures, abscesses, perianal diseaseToxic megacolon, colon cancer risk
SurgeryNot curative (recurs)Curative (total colectomy)
CobblestoningYesNo
💡 Mnemonic — "CROHN'S"
Cobblestones | Regional enteritis | Occurs anywhere | Has granulomas | Non-caseating | Skip lesions, Strictures, Steatorrhea
✅ Nursing Priorities:
• Monitor bowel patterns, stool characteristics
• Hydration and electrolyte replacement
• Low-residue diet during flares
• Medications: 5-ASA (sulfasalazine), corticosteroids, immunomodulators, biologics
• Skin care around perianal area
• Emotional support (chronic, unpredictable disease)
🚨 NCLEX Trap: Toxic megacolon in UC = medical emergency. Signs: fever, tachycardia, abdominal distension, decreased bowel sounds. NEVER give antidiarrheals (loperamide) — can worsen toxic megacolon!

3. Cirrhosis & Liver Disease

📋 Compensated vs. Decompensated Cirrhosis

Early (Compensated): Fatigue, anorexia, mild RUQ discomfort
Late (Decompensated): Jaundice, ascites, variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy, coagulopathy

Common Causes: Alcohol, hepatitis C, NAFLD/NASH, biliary disease, hemochromatosis, Wilson's disease.

✅ Nursing Priorities:
• Monitor for complications: ascites, encephalopathy, bleeding
• Sodium restriction (2g/day) for ascites
• Diuretics: spironolactone + furosemide (ratio 100:40)
• Lactulose + rifaximin for hepatic encephalopathy
• Vitamin K for coagulopathy
• Beta-blockers (propranolol) for variceal prophylaxis
• Paracentesis care (measure, document, monitor for SBP)
💡 Child-Pugh Criteria — Assess Severity:
Bilirubin | Albumin | INR | Ascites | Encephalopathy → Class A (5-6), B (7-9), C (10-15) — Class C = poor prognosis
🚨 NCLEX Trap: Hepatic encephalopathy is caused by ammonia accumulation. Lactulose works by acidifying the colon → ammonia converts to ammonium (can't cross BBB) → excreted. Monitor for diarrhea but don't stop lactulose — the diarrhea IS the treatment!

4. Acute Pancreatitis

📋 Acute Pancreatitis

Symptoms — "BURNING EPIGASTRIC PAIN"
Severe epigastric pain radiating to BACK | N/V | Fever | Tachycardia | Cullen's sign (periumbilical bruising) | Grey Turner's sign (flank bruising)

Common Causes: Gallstones (most common), alcohol, hypertriglyceridemia, ERCP, trauma, medications.

✅ Nursing Priorities:
• NPO initially — rest the pancreas
• IV fluids (aggressive — risk of third spacing)
• Pain management (morphine or hydromorphone — meperidine is outdated)
• Monitor amylase and lipase (lipase more specific)
• NG suction if severe vomiting/ileus
• Monitor for complications: pseudocyst, abscess, ARDS, DIC
🚨 NCLEX Trap: Cullen's and Grey Turner's signs indicate HEMORRHAGIC pancreatitis — medical emergency. Also, calcium levels DROP in severe pancreatitis (saponification) — hypocalcemia is a poor prognostic sign.

5. Appendicitis

📋 Acute Appendicitis

Classic Presentation: Periumbilical pain → migrates to RLQ (McBurney's point) | Rebound tenderness | Rovsing's sign (LLQ palpation causes RLQ pain) | Psoas sign | Obturator sign | Low-grade fever | Anorexia
✅ Nursing Priorities:
• NPO (possible surgery)
• IV fluids, antibiotics
• Pain assessment (don't mask with analgesics pre-diagnosis if possible)
• Monitor for perforation (sudden relief then worsening pain, rigid abdomen)
• Post-op: ambulation, wound care, monitor for infection
🚨 NCLEX Trap: If appendicitis is suspected, NEVER apply heat, laxatives, or enemas — can cause perforation. In older adults and children, presentation may be atypical (diffuse pain, minimal fever).

🧩 NGN Case Study: Upper GI Bleed

Client Scenario

Mr. Patel, 58-year-old male, is admitted to the medical floor after presenting to the ED with vomiting "coffee grounds" and passing black, tarry stools. He has a history of NSAID use for knee arthritis (ibuprofen 800mg 3x daily) and occasional alcohol use. He denies abdominal pain but feels weak and dizzy.

Vital Signs Physical Assessment Labs

Vital Signs: BP 94/62, HR 118, RR 22, Temp 36.8°C, SpO₂ 95%

Physical Assessment: Pale, diaphoretic. Alert but fatigued. Abdomen soft, non-tender, no guarding. BS present all quadrants. No jaundice. Capillary refill 3 seconds. Stool guaiac strongly positive.

Labs: Hgb 7.2 g/dL, Hct 22%, Platelets 180,000, INR 1.1, BUN 48, Creatinine 1.1, Type and screen sent

Question 1 — Recognize Cues: Which finding is most consistent with an upper GI bleed?

  • A. Hematochezia (bright red blood per rectum)
  • B. Coffee ground emesis and melena
  • C. Abdominal guarding and rebound tenderness
  • D. Elevated liver enzymes and jaundice

Correct: B. Coffee ground emesis and melena

Coffee ground emesis indicates blood that has been partially digested by gastric acid (upper GI source). Melena (black, tarry stools) indicates blood that has been digested as it passed through the intestines. Both indicate upper GI bleeding (above the ligament of Treitz). Hematochezia (A) typically indicates lower GI bleeding. Guarding (C) suggests peritonitis. Jaundice (D) suggests liver disease, not acute bleed.

Question 2 — Analyze Cues: The BUN is 48 with a normal creatinine. What is the most likely cause of the elevated BUN?

  • A. Prerenal acute kidney injury from hypovolemia
  • B. Blood protein digestion in the GI tract (protein load)
  • C. Dehydration causing concentrated blood
  • D. Liver dysfunction impairing BUN metabolism

Correct: B. Blood protein digestion in the GI tract (protein load)

In upper GI bleeding, blood protein is digested in the GI tract and absorbed as amino acids, which are converted to urea by the liver — causing an elevated BUN out of proportion to creatinine. This is a classic finding. While prerenal AKI (A) can elevate BUN, the normal creatinine makes this less likely. The BUN:creatinine ratio is typically >20:1 in GI bleed (here it's ~44:1). This is a high-yield NCLEX concept.

Question 3 — Prioritize Hypotheses: The provider orders 2 units PRBCs, IV PPI 80mg bolus then 8mg/hr infusion, and IV fluids. Which action should the nurse implement first?

  • A. Insert a large-bore IV (18g or 16g) and start fluid resuscitation
  • B. Administer the PPI bolus to stop the bleeding
  • C. Transfuse the PRBCs immediately
  • D. Place an NG tube for gastric lavage

Correct: A. Insert a large-bore IV (18g or 16g) and start fluid resuscitation

With hypotension (94/62) and tachycardia (118), the patient is in hemorrhagic shock. The priority is establishing vascular access for fluid resuscitation. Two large-bore IVs are standard. Blood transfusion (C) takes time (type and crossmatch). PPI (B) is important but not emergent. NG tube (D) is diagnostic/therapeutic but secondary to hemodynamic stabilization. The ABCs and circulation take priority.

Question 4 — Generate Solutions: After transfusion, Hgb improves to 9.0. The patient is stable. EGD reveals a gastric ulcer with a visible vessel. Which post-procedure instruction is highest priority?

  • A. "You can resume your ibuprofen once the pain returns"
  • B. "Take omeprazole 20mg daily for 8 weeks"
  • C. "Avoid alcohol for 1 week"
  • D. "Eat a bland diet for the rest of your life"

Correct: B. "Take omeprazole 20mg daily for 8 weeks"

PPI therapy for 8 weeks is standard for healing peptic ulcers and preventing rebleeding. NSAIDs (A) are absolutely contraindicated — they caused this ulcer. Alcohol (C) should be avoided long-term, not just 1 week. Bland diet (D) is unnecessary — patients can eat normally, just avoid irritants. Testing for H. pylori should also be done, as it's a common cause of ulcers.

Question 5 — Take Action: The patient asks why he needs to avoid NSAIDs. He states, "But ibuprofen is the only thing that helps my knee pain." What is the nurse's best response?

  • A. "You can take ibuprofen with food to protect your stomach"
  • B. "NSAIDs inhibit prostaglandins, which protect your stomach lining — that's why you bled"
  • C. "Your doctor will prescribe something stronger for your knee"
  • D. "You should try acetaminophen instead — it's just as effective"

Correct: B. "NSAIDs inhibit prostaglandins, which protect your stomach lining — that's why you bled"

Patient education should include the mechanism so the patient understands WHY the change is necessary. NSAIDs inhibit COX-1, reducing prostaglandins that maintain gastric mucosal blood flow and bicarbonate secretion. Acetaminophen (D) is safer for the stomach but not "just as effective" for inflammatory arthritis — the patient may need an alternative like topical NSAIDs, acetaminophen, or referral to rheumatology. Simply offering a different drug (C) doesn't educate the patient.

Question 6 — Evaluate Outcomes: At 2-week follow-up, which finding indicates the treatment plan is effective?

  • A. Patient reports occasional heartburn after meals
  • B. Hgb is 10.5 g/dL and stool guaiac is negative
  • C. Patient has stopped taking omeprazole because he feels better
  • D. Patient switched to naproxen for knee pain

Correct: B. Hgb is 10.5 g/dL and stool guaiac is negative

Rising Hgb and negative stool guaiac confirm bleeding has stopped and anemia is resolving. Occasional heartburn (A) may indicate ongoing acid issues. Stopping PPI early (C) risks rebleeding — the full 8-week course is needed. Switching to another NSAID (D) is dangerous and shows the education failed. Effective treatment = no active bleeding, healing ulcer, medication adherence, and lifestyle modifications.
