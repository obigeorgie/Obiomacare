NCLEX High-Alert Medications

ISMP high-alert medications that require extra safeguards. The NCLEX tests these constantly because errors cause the most harm.

⚠️ What Makes a Medication "High-Alert"?

According to the Institute for Safe Medication Practices (ISMP), high-alert medications are those that bear a heightened risk of causing significant patient harm when used in error. The NCLEX prioritizes safety — knowing these medications and their safeguards is critical.

1. Insulins

RISK Hypoglycemia, hyperglycemia, DKA, HHS
✅ SAFETY ACTIONS:
• Double-check dose with another RN (independent verification)
• Verify blood glucose before administration
• Know onset/peak/duration of each type
• Never mix rapid and long-acting in same syringe
• Have dextrose 50% available for hypoglycemia
• Check patient identity using two identifiers

2. Anticoagulants

RISK Bleeding, hemorrhage, HIT (heparin)
✅ SAFETY ACTIONS:
• Heparin: Monitor aPTT q6h until therapeutic. Use smart pumps. Check platelets for HIT.
• Warfarin: Monitor INR. Therapeutic = 2-3 (mechanical valve 2.5-3.5). Vitamin K = reversal.
• DOACs (apixaban, rivaroxaban): No routine monitoring. Check renal function. Reversal: andexanet alfa.
• All: Check for contraindications (active bleeding, recent surgery, low platelets)

3. Opioids

RISK Respiratory depression, sedation, hypotension, addiction
✅ SAFETY ACTIONS:
• Monitor respiratory rate and sedation level (Pasero Opioid-Induced Sedation Scale)
• Have naloxone (Narcan) readily available
• Use PCA smart pumps with lockout intervals
• Never leave first dose unattended
• Assess pain using valid scale (0-10 or Wong-Baker FACES)
• Document bowel regimen — constipation is expected

4. Neuromuscular Blocking Agents

RISK Paralysis without sedation, awareness, anaphylaxis
✅ SAFETY ACTIONS:
• NEVER give without confirmed sedation/anesthesia
• Verify ventilator is functioning before administration
• Store separately from other meds (different color bins)
• Ensure emergency airway equipment at bedside
• Examples: Succinylcholine, Rocuronium, Vecuronium

5. Chemotherapy

RISK Extravasation, myelosuppression, cardiotoxicity, teratogenicity
✅ SAFETY ACTIONS:
• Double-check calculations (BSA-based dosing)
• Verify vesicant vs irritant vs non-vesicant
• Monitor CBC before each cycle
• Use PPE during handling (gown, gloves, eye protection)
• Ensure vesicant administration through central line when possible

6. Electrolyte Solutions

RISK Cardiac arrest (K⁺, Mg²⁺), fluid overload, phlebitis
✅ SAFETY ACTIONS:
• Potassium: NEVER IV push. Max 10 mEq/hr via peripheral line, 20 mEq/hr central. Must be on cardiac monitor.
• Magnesium: Monitor reflexes, respiratory rate. Have calcium gluconate ready.
• Hypertonic saline (3% NaCl): Central line only. Monitor neurologic status closely.

7. Sedatives / Hypnotics

RISK Respiratory depression, falls, paradoxical reactions
✅ SAFETY ACTIONS:
• Monitor respiratory rate, SpO₂, level of consciousness
• Implement fall precautions
• Avoid combining multiple CNS depressants
• Have reversal agents available (flumazenil for benzos, naloxone for opioids)

ISMP Safety Strategies for High-Alert Meds

  1. Independent double check: Two RNs verify dose independently before administration
  2. Standardize concentrations: Use premixed solutions when available
  3. Smart pump technology: Use dose error reduction systems (DERS)
  4. Limit access: High-alert meds stored separately with visual alerts
  5. Patient education: Teach patients about their high-alert medications

🧩 NGN Case Study: Insulin Error & Hypoglycemia

Client Scenario

Mr. Davis, 58-year-old male, is admitted for uncontrolled Type 2 diabetes (A1C 10.2%). He is on a basal-bolus insulin regimen: glargine 20 units at bedtime, lispro with meals (sliding scale). His breakfast tray arrives at 8:00 AM. The nurse administers 8 units of lispro at 7:45 AM.

Vital Signs Blood Glucose Medications

Vital Signs: BP 134/78, HR 82, RR 16, Temp 36.6°C

Blood Glucose: Fasting 142 mg/dL (7:30 AM). The breakfast tray is placed on the over-bed table but the patient states he's "not hungry yet."

Medications: Lispro 8 units given at 7:45 AM. Glargine 20 units due at HS. Metformin 1000mg BID (held for procedure).

Question 1 — Recognize Cues: The nurse gave lispro at 7:45 AM but the patient hasn't eaten. What is the most likely complication?

  • A. Hyperglycemia from inadequate insulin coverage
  • B. Hypoglycemia from insulin given without food
  • C. Diabetic ketoacidosis from missed metformin dose
  • D. No complication — lispro can be given before or after meals

Correct: B. Hypoglycemia from insulin given without food

Lispro is a rapid-acting insulin with onset 15 minutes, peak 1 hour. Giving it without food will cause hypoglycemia within 30-60 minutes. The "right time" for mealtime insulin is WITH the meal or immediately before — never give rapid-acting insulin if the patient is NPO or refusing to eat. This is a high-alert medication error that requires immediate intervention (provide carbohydrates, monitor glucose closely).

Question 2 — Analyze Cues: At 8:30 AM, the patient is diaphoretic, confused, and states he feels "shaky." His glucose is 48 mg/dL. What is the nurse's first action?

  • A. Give 1 mg glucagon IM
  • B. Give 15g fast-acting carbohydrates (glucose tablets or juice)
  • C. Call the provider for new insulin orders
  • D. Recheck glucose in 15 minutes

Correct: B. Give 15g fast-acting carbohydrates (glucose tablets or juice)

For conscious patients with hypoglycemia, the "15-15 rule" applies: give 15g fast-acting carbs, recheck in 15 minutes, repeat if still <70. Glucagon (A) is for unconscious patients or when oral intake is impossible. The patient is conscious but confused — oral treatment is appropriate. Calling the provider (C) delays treatment. Rechecking (D) without treating first is inappropriate.

Question 3 — Prioritize Hypotheses: After treatment, the patient's glucose is 78 mg/dL at 8:50 AM. He wants to eat his breakfast now. What is the nurse's best action?

  • A. Allow him to eat — his glucose is now in normal range
  • B. Give him a snack with protein and carbs, then reassess
  • C. Hold his breakfast and keep him NPO until next scheduled glucose check
  • D. Call the provider before allowing any food intake

Correct: B. Give him a snack with protein and carbs, then reassess

After hypoglycemia treatment, the patient needs a snack with complex carbs and protein to prevent recurrent hypoglycemia (the insulin is still active). The breakfast tray is appropriate, but the nurse should ensure he eats and monitor for rebound hypoglycemia. Simply allowing him to eat (A) is okay but doesn't address the need for monitoring. Keeping him NPO (C) is dangerous — he needs nutrition. Provider call (D) is unnecessary for this expected situation.

Question 4 — Generate Solutions: Which system-level intervention would best prevent this type of insulin error?

  • A. Require a second nurse to verify all insulin doses
  • B. Use a "meal ticket" system — insulin not given until tray is at bedside
  • C. Switch all patients to long-acting insulin only
  • D. Eliminate sliding scale insulin from all protocols

Correct: B. Use a "meal ticket" system — insulin not given until tray is at bedside

The root cause was insulin given before food availability. A meal ticket system ensures mealtime insulin is only given when the tray is present or the patient is eating. Independent double-check (A) verifies the dose but not the timing. Switching to basal-only (C) is not appropriate for many patients. Eliminating sliding scale (D) may be appropriate for some but doesn't address the timing issue.

Question 5 — Take Action: The patient's evening glargine dose is due. He is scheduled for a colonoscopy at 8:00 AM tomorrow (NPO after midnight). What is the nurse's best action regarding glargine?

  • A. Hold glargine — he's NPO after midnight
  • B. Give the usual 20 units at bedtime
  • C. Give a reduced dose of 10 units at bedtime
  • D. Switch to regular insulin for better control

Correct: B. Give the usual 20 units at bedtime

Basal insulin (glargine, detemir, NPH) should NOT be held for NPO status — it prevents ketosis and maintains baseline glucose. The patient still needs basal insulin even when not eating. Only mealtime (bolus) insulin is held when NPO. Reducing the dose (C) may be appropriate if there are concerns about hypoglycemia, but the standard is to continue basal insulin. Regular insulin (D) is not appropriate for basal coverage.

Question 6 — Evaluate Outcomes: At discharge, which patient statement indicates effective understanding of insulin safety?

  • A. "I'll take my mealtime insulin right before I eat"
  • B. "I can skip my insulin if I'm not eating a full meal"
  • C. "I'll keep my insulin in the freezer so it stays fresh"
  • D. "I only need to check my glucose when I feel symptomatic"

Correct: A. "I'll take my mealtime insulin right before I eat"

Taking rapid-acting insulin with meals is the correct and safe practice. Skipping insulin (B) leads to hyperglycemia and DKA risk. Insulin should be refrigerated, NOT frozen (C) — freezing destroys the protein. Glucose should be checked as prescribed (usually before meals and HS), not just when symptomatic (D).
