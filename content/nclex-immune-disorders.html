NCLEX Immune Disorders

HIV/AIDS, autoimmune diseases, transplant rejection, and immunosuppressants for NCLEX success.

1. HIV/AIDS

📋 Pathophysiology and Staging

HIV attacks CD4+ T-helper cells → immune dysfunction → opportunistic infections when CD4 <200 or AIDS-defining illness.

StageCD4 CountCharacteristics
Acute infectionNormal but fallingFlu-like symptoms (fever, rash, lymphadenopathy), high viral load, very contagious
Clinical latency200-500Asymptomatic or mild symptoms, ongoing viral replication
Symptomatic<350Recurrent infections, weight loss, night sweats, fatigue
AIDS<200 or AIDS-defining illnessSevere opportunistic infections, malignancies
💡 AIDS-Defining Opportunistic Infections: "TPPPCCHHH"
Toxoplasmosis | PCP (Pneumocystis pneumonia) | Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy | Persistent candidiasis | Cryptococcal meningitis | CMV | HSV | Histoplasmosis | HIV wasting syndrome

📋 HIV Nursing Priorities

✅ Nursing Care:
• Standard precautions (NOT contact or airborne — HIV is blood/body fluid borne)
• Monitor CD4 count and viral load
• Adherence to ART (antiretroviral therapy) — missing doses causes resistance
• Prevent opportunistic infections (prophylaxis when CD4 <200)
• Nutrition support (high calorie, high protein)
• Psychosocial support (stigma, depression, disclosure issues)
• Safer sex education, needle exchange if applicable
• Screen for TB (common co-infection)
🚨 NCLEX Trap: A patient with HIV and CD4 of 150 should receive PCP prophylaxis (TMP-SMX or alternative). A patient with CD4 <50 should receive MAC (Mycobacterium avium complex) prophylaxis. Know the CD4 thresholds for prophylaxis — they're commonly tested.

📋 Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP) and Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP)

✅ PEP:
• Must start within 72 hours of exposure (sooner is better)
• 3-drug regimen for 28 days
• Baseline HIV test, then follow-up testing at 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months
• Report to occupational health (needlestick)
✅ PrEP:
• For HIV-negative individuals at high risk
• Daily tenofovir/emtricitabine (Truvada or Descovy)
• Must be HIV-negative before starting (test every 3 months)
• Does NOT protect against other STIs

2. Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)

📋 Multisystem Autoimmune Disease

Common Manifestations:
• Malar (butterfly) rash — spares nasolabial folds
• Discoid rash
• Photosensitivity
• Oral ulcers
• Arthritis (non-erosive)
• Serositis (pleuritis, pericarditis)
• Renal involvement (proteinuria, hematuria — lupus nephritis)
• Neurological (seizures, psychosis)
• Hematologic (anemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia)
• Immunologic (ANA+, anti-dsDNA+, anti-Smith+)
💡 SLE Diagnosis Criteria: "SOAP BRAIN MD"
Serositis | Oral ulcers | Arthritis | Photosensitivity | Blood disorders | Renal | ANA | Immunologic | Neuro | Malar rash | Discoid rash
✅ Nursing Priorities:
• Sun protection (SPF 50+, protective clothing, avoid midday sun)
• Monitor renal function (BUN/Cr, urinalysis)
• Monitor for flares (increased fatigue, rash, joint pain)
• Medications: NSAIDs, antimalarials (hydroxychloroquine — baseline eye exam), corticosteroids, immunosuppressants
• Hydroxychloroquine: ophthalmology exams q6-12 months (retinopathy risk)
• Pregnancy counseling (high-risk, flares common)

3. Multiple Sclerosis (MS)

📋 Demyelination of CNS

Common Symptoms: Fatigue, optic neuritis (vision loss), paresthesia, weakness, spasticity, bladder/bowel dysfunction, cognitive changes, Lhermitte's sign (electric shock down spine with neck flexion)
✅ Nursing Priorities:
• Prevent falls (ataxia, weakness)
• Bladder management (intermittent catheterization, anticholinergics)
• Bowel regimen (constipation common)
• Temperature regulation (heat worsens symptoms — Uthoff's phenomenon)
• Spasticity management (baclofen, tizanidine, stretching)
• Fatigue management (energy conservation, amantadine, modafinil)
• Emotional support (depression common)
• Disease-modifying therapies: interferons, glatiramer acetate, fingolimod, ocrelizumab
🚨 NCLEX Trap: Heat exacerbates MS symptoms (Uthoff's phenomenon) — hot baths, fever, exercise in heat. Cold does NOT worsen symptoms and may actually help. For an MS patient with increased weakness during a fever, treat the fever (antipyretics, cooling) — don't assume disease progression.

4. Transplant Rejection

📋 Types of Rejection

TypeTimingMechanismTreatment
HyperacuteMinutes to hoursPre-existing antibodies → vascular thrombosisRemove graft immediately (irreversible)
AcuteDays to monthsT-cell mediatedHigh-dose corticosteroids, antilymphocyte antibodies, adjust immunosuppression
ChronicMonths to yearsProgressive fibrosis, vessel narrowingLimited treatment, may need re-transplant
✅ Immunosuppressant Medications:
• Calcineurin inhibitors: Tacrolimus, cyclosporine (nephrotoxic, monitor trough levels)
• Antiproliferatives: Mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine (bone marrow suppression)
• mTOR inhibitors: Sirolimus, everolimus (impaired wound healing)
• Corticosteroids: Prednisone (many side effects)
• Induction: Basiliximab, thymoglobulin (prevent early rejection)
🚨 NCLEX Trap: Transplant patients on immunosuppressants are at risk for OPPORTUNISTIC INFECTIONS and MALIGNANCIES (especially skin cancer and lymphoma). They should avoid live vaccines. Grapefruit juice is contraindicated with calcineurin inhibitors (increases blood levels).

5. Myasthenia Gravis

📋 Autoimmune Antibodies Against Acetylcholine Receptors

Cardinal Feature: Muscle weakness that WORSENS with activity and IMPROVES with rest
Common: Ptosis, diplopia, dysphagia, dysarthria, proximal muscle weakness
Crisis: Myasthenic crisis (severe weakness, respiratory failure) vs cholinergic crisis (excess cholinesterase inhibitors — SLUDGE symptoms)
✅ Nursing Priorities:
• Monitor respiratory function (FVC, negative inspiratory force) — respiratory failure is main cause of death
• Schedule medications on time (pyridostigmine — 30-60 min before meals for swallowing)
• Avoid medications that worsen MG: aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, beta-blockers, magnesium
• Myasthenic crisis: plasmapheresis, IVIG, mechanical ventilation
• Cholinergic crisis: hold anticholinesterases, atropine PRN, supportive care
• Thymectomy may be curative
🚨 NCLEX Trap: Tensilon (edrophonium) test: improvement = myasthenic crisis (needs MORE medication). Worsening = cholinergic crisis (needs LESS medication). KNOW the difference — both present with weakness but treatment is opposite.

6. Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS)

📋 Ascending Paralysis — Autoimmune Demyelination of Peripheral Nerves

Classic Presentation: Ascending symmetric weakness starting in legs, areflexia, paresthesia, may progress to respiratory failure. Often preceded by infection (Campylobacter, CMV, EBV).
✅ Nursing Priorities:
• Respiratory monitoring (FVC, NIF) — intubate if FVC <15 mL/kg or NIF <-20 cmH₂O
• Autonomic instability (labile BP, arrhythmias) — continuous cardiac monitoring
• DVT prophylaxis
• Pain management (neuropathic pain common)
• Bowel/bladder management
• Emotional support (paralysis is terrifying)
• Plasmapheresis or IVIG (equally effective, speeds recovery)
• Recovery: ascending paralysis → descending recovery
🚨 NCLEX Trap: GBS causes ASCENDING paralysis (starts in feet, moves up). It does NOT cause sensory loss above the level of a spinal lesion (that's spinal cord injury). The main cause of death in GBS is RESPIRATORY FAILURE — monitor respiratory function closely.
