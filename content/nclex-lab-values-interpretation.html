NCLEX Lab Values Interpretation: High-Yield Guide

What high and low results mean for sodium, potassium, calcium, glucose, creatinine, hemoglobin, A1C, and cholesterol — the interpretation logic the NCLEX rewards. Reference ranges vary by laboratory; this guide focuses on the clinical meaning of abnormal results.

1. Sodium

What it reflects: Sodium concentration in the blood, normally kept in a very narrow range.

Higher than normal may be a sign of:

  • Dehydration, which may be caused by not drinking enough, diarrhea, or taking diuretic medicines (water pills)
  • A disorder of the adrenal glands
  • A kidney disease
  • Diabetes insipidus (uncommon)

Lower than normal may be a sign of:

  • A loss of sodium from diarrhea or vomiting
  • A condition that may cause the body to hold onto extra fluid (which dilutes sodium), including kidney diseases, cirrhosis of the liver, heart failure, and certain brain and lung diseases

2. Potassium

Higher than normal (hyperkalemia) is often the result of two or more causes, which could include:

  • Kidney disease — kidneys not working well
  • Addison disease, a disorder of the adrenal glands
  • Injuries, burns, or surgery, which can cause cells to release extra potassium into the blood
  • Type 1 diabetes that is not well controlled
  • The side effects of certain medicines, such as diuretics or antibiotics
  • A diet too high in potassium (not common)

3. Calcium

Higher than normal (hypercalcemia) may be a sign of:

  • Overactive parathyroid glands (hyperparathyroidism)
  • Certain types of cancer, including cancer that spreads to the bone
  • Bone disorders, including Paget's disease of bone
  • Taking too much vitamin D over a long period of time

Lower than normal (hypocalcemia) may be a sign of:

  • Low blood protein levels, which may be caused by liver disease or malnutrition
  • Underactive parathyroid glands (hypoparathyroidism)

4. Glucose

Higher than normal glucose levels may mean the patient has or is at risk for diabetes. Prediabetes and diabetes are the most common causes, but other possible causes include:

  • An overactive thyroid gland (hyperthyroidism)
  • A problem with the pancreas, including pancreatitis
  • Stress from surgery, very serious illness, or trauma
  • A problem with the adrenal glands, including Cushing's syndrome and pheochromocytoma
  • Certain medicines

5. Creatinine

  • A normal creatinine test result does not always mean the kidneys are healthy — levels can remain normal during the early stages of kidney disease and rise as the condition becomes more serious.
  • If the provider suspects a kidney condition, other kidney tests are likely even if creatinine results seem normal.
  • If results are abnormal, a single high creatinine test can't diagnose a kidney condition by itself.

6. Hemoglobin

Low hemoglobin levels may be a sign of:

  • Different types of anemia
  • Thalassemia
  • Iron deficiency
  • Liver disease
  • Cancer and other diseases
  • Blood loss from injury, a heavy menstrual period, or other problems
  • Lack of certain nutrients

The test can't tell what's causing an abnormal level, but knowing how high or low the hemoglobin is helps the provider determine how severe the condition may be.

7. Hemoglobin A1C

A1C results tell you what percentage of your hemoglobin is coated with glucose. To diagnose diabetes or prediabetes, the percentages commonly used are:

  • Normal: A1C below 5.7%
  • Prediabetes: A1C between 5.7% and 6.4%
  • Diabetes: A1C of 6.5% or higher

Providers often use more than one test to diagnose diabetes. If a result is higher than normal, the patient may have another A1C test or a different type of diabetes test — usually either a fasting blood glucose test or an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT).

8. Cholesterol

Cholesterol is usually measured in milligrams (mg) of cholesterol per deciliter (dL) of blood. In general, low LDL levels and high HDL cholesterol levels are good for heart health.

Men age 20 or older — healthy levels:

  • Total cholesterol: less than 200 mg/dL
  • Non-HDL: less than 130 mg/dL
  • LDL: less than 100 mg/dL
  • HDL: greater than or equal to 60 mg/dL is best; levels less than 40 mg/dL are considered low

Women age 20 or older — healthy levels:

  • Total cholesterol: less than 200 mg/dL
  • Non-HDL: less than 130 mg/dL
  • LDL: less than 100 mg/dL
  • HDL: greater than or equal to 60 mg/dL is best

Anyone age 19 or younger — healthy levels:

  • Total cholesterol: less than 170 mg/dL
  • Non-HDL: less than 120 mg/dL
  • LDL: less than 110 mg/dL
  • HDL: more than 45 mg/dL
