Ethical Principles

1. Autonomy

Definition: The patient's right to self-determination — to make their own decisions about their care.

  • Respect the patient's right to refuse treatment
  • Provide all information needed for informed decisions
  • Honor advance directives

Example: A Jehovah's Witness refuses blood transfusion. Even if the nurse disagrees, the patient's autonomy must be respected (assuming competent decision-making).

2. Beneficence

Definition: Acting in the patient's best interest — doing good.

  • Advocate for the patient
  • Prevent harm
  • Remove conditions that cause harm

3. Non-maleficence

Definition: "First, do no harm" — avoiding actions that could harm the patient.

  • Double-check medication dosages
  • Verify patient identity
  • Report unsafe conditions

Remember: Beneficence = DO good. Non-maleficence = DON'T do harm.

4. Justice

Definition: Fair and equitable distribution of resources and treatment.

  • Treat all patients equally regardless of background
  • Allocate resources fairly
  • Advocate for underserved populations

5. Fidelity

Definition: Being faithful to your commitments — keeping promises to patients.

  • Follow through on what you tell the patient
  • Be trustworthy
  • Maintain confidentiality

6. Veracity

Definition: Telling the truth — honesty with patients.

  • Provide accurate information
  • Don't hide or distort information
  • Admit errors

Informed Consent

Elements Required

For consent to be valid, the patient must understand:

  • Nature of the procedure — What will be done?
  • Risks and benefits — What could go right or wrong?
  • Alternatives — What are the other options?
  • Consequences of refusal — What happens if they say no?

Who Can Give Consent?

Situation Who Consents?
Competent adult Patient themselves
Incompetent adult Healthcare proxy, power of attorney, or guardian
Emergency (life-threatening, no proxy) Implied consent — treat under emergency doctrine
Minor Parent/guardian (except emancipated minors, some reproductive care)

NCLEX Tip: The provider (MD/DO/NP/PA) is responsible for obtaining informed consent. The nurse's role is to witness the signature and confirm the patient understands. If the patient seems confused or pressured, notify the provider.

When Consent Is NOT Valid

  • Under influence of drugs/alcohol (impaired judgment)
  • Severe pain or distress affecting comprehension
  • Coercion or undue pressure
  • Language barrier without interpreter
  • Patient lacks decision-making capacity

Advance Directives

Types

Type What It Does Key Points
Living Will States wishes for end-of-life care Only effective when patient is terminal or permanently unconscious
Durable Power of Attorney for Healthcare Designates someone to make medical decisions Takes effect when patient is incapacitated; can override living will
DNR/DNI Do Not Resuscitate / Do Not Intubate Must be honored; does NOT mean "do not treat"
POLST/MOLST Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment Provider-signed medical order; follows patient across settings

Critical: A DNR order does NOT mean "do not treat." The patient still receives medications, comfort care, nutrition, and all other appropriate treatments. It only means no CPR if cardiac arrest occurs.

What If Family Disagrees With Advance Directive?

  • Follow the advance directive — it represents the PATIENT'S wishes
  • Contact ethics committee if conflict persists
  • Document thoroughly
  • The healthcare proxy's decision supersedes family objections

HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act)

What Is Protected?

PHI (Protected Health Information): Any information that can identify a patient and relates to their health.

  • Name, address, DOB, SSN, MR number
  • Diagnosis, treatment, medications
  • Photos, voice recordings
  • Any information that could reasonably identify the patient

When Can PHI Be Disclosed?

Situation Permitted?
Treatment, payment, healthcare operations (TPO) Yes — without patient authorization
To family/friends involved in care (with patient permission) Yes
Public health reporting (diseases, injuries) Yes — required by law
Court order or subpoena Yes — if valid
Report of abuse/neglect Yes — mandatory reporting
Media inquiry No — without explicit authorization
Curiosity (looking up celebrity's chart) No — NEVER

Patient Rights Under HIPAA

  • Right to access their medical records
  • Right to request amendments to records
  • Right to know who has accessed their information
  • Right to file a complaint

Common NCLEX Trap: A family member calls asking about a patient. The nurse CANNOT disclose information unless the patient has specifically authorized it — even to spouse or parents (if adult).

‍ Scope of Practice

What Determines Scope?

  • State Nurse Practice Act — defines what RNs, LPNs, and APRNs can do
  • Facility policies — may be more restrictive
  • Education and competency — must be trained and competent
  • Delegation rules — what can be delegated to whom

Delegation Rules

Task RN LPN/LVN UAP
Assessment Data collection only

IV medication (piggyback) Varies by state
IV push
Oral medications
Catheterization
Vital signs (stable)
Bathing, ambulation

Remember the 5 Rights of Delegation:

  1. Right task — can it be delegated?
  2. Right circumstance — is the patient stable?
  3. Right person — are they competent?
  4. Right direction — clear instructions given?
  5. Right supervision — appropriate oversight?

Malpractice & Negligence

Elements of Malpractice

To prove malpractice, ALL four elements must be present:

  1. Duty — Nurse owed duty of care to the patient
  2. Breach — Nurse failed to meet standard of care
  3. Causation — The breach caused the injury
  4. Damages — The patient suffered harm

Common Examples of Negligence

  • Medication errors (wrong dose, wrong patient, wrong route)
  • Failure to monitor (not checking vitals, not responding to alarms)
  • Failure to report (not notifying provider of changes)
  • Documentation errors
  • Patient falls (inadequate safety measures)
  • Pressure injuries (inadequate repositioning)

Protecting Yourself

  • Follow policies and procedures
  • Document thoroughly and objectively
  • Communicate clearly with team and patient
  • Know your scope of practice
  • Obtain malpractice insurance
  • Report errors immediately — cover-ups are worse than the error

Key NCLEX Concept: If you make an error, the FIRST action is to assess the patient and ensure their safety. Then report to the charge nurse/provider. Never try to hide or destroy evidence.

NGN Case Study: Ethical Dilemma

Scenario

Mr. Garcia, 82 y/o, has advanced COPD and is admitted with respiratory failure. He is alert and oriented. His daughter, who is his healthcare proxy, insists on intubation and full code status. Mr. Garcia tells the nurse privately that he does not want to be intubated and wants to be DNR.

  • Advance directive: None on file
  • Capacity: Alert, oriented, coherent
  • Daughter: Healthcare proxy, disagrees with patient's wishes

What is the nurse's best action?

Correct: B

Rationale: A competent patient has the right to make their own medical decisions (autonomy). The healthcare proxy only becomes decision-maker when the patient lacks capacity. Since Mr. Garcia is alert and oriented, his wishes take precedence over his daughter's preferences. The nurse should:

  1. Honor his autonomy
  2. Notify the provider of his wishes
  3. Document the conversation thoroughly
  4. Offer to involve ethics if needed

The proxy cannot override a competent patient's decisions.
