Liver & Pancreas Disorders

Cirrhosis, hepatitis, pancreatitis, gallbladder disease, and nursing management for the NCLEX.

🫁 Cirrhosis

Pathophysiology & Causes

Definition: Irreversible fibrosis and nodular regeneration of the liver → portal hypertension → complications

Causes: Chronic alcohol use (most common in US), chronic hepatitis B/C, NAFLD/NASH, autoimmune hepatitis, hemochromatosis, Wilson disease, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, primary biliary cholangitis

Mnemonic: "Hepatic Cirrhosis Causes Alcoholism" — Hepatitis, Cirrhosis, Cryptogenic, Alcohol, Autoimmune

Manifestations

Early: Fatigue, weight loss, anorexia, nausea, hepatomegaly
Late (decompensated):
  • Jaundice
  • Spider angiomas, palmar erythema
  • Gynecomastia, testicular atrophy (decreased estrogen metabolism)
  • Caput medusae (dilated periumbilical veins)
  • Ascites
  • Peripheral edema
  • Hepatic encephalopathy
  • Coagulopathy (decreased clotting factors)

Complications

Ascites

Pathophysiology: Portal hypertension + hypoalbuminemia → fluid accumulation in peritoneum

Management:
  • Sodium restriction (<2 g/day)
  • Diuretics: Spironolactone + furosemide (100:40 ratio)
  • Fluid restriction if severe hyponatremia (Na <125)
  • Paracentesis (diagnostic or therapeutic)
  • TIPS (transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt) for refractory ascites

Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis (SBP)

Definition: Infection of ascitic fluid without identifiable intra-abdominal source

Manifestations: Fever, abdominal pain/tenderness, altered mental status, worsening renal function
Management: Paracentesis → PMN >250 cells/mm³ = SBP. Treat with ceftriaxone or cefotaxime x 5-7 days. Albumin on day 1 and 3 (reduces hepatorenal syndrome). Secondary prophylaxis with norfloxacin or TMP-SMX.

Esophageal Varices

Pathophysiology: Portal hypertension → collateral circulation → dilated esophageal veins

Prevention: Non-selective beta-blockers (propranolol, nadolol) — reduce portal pressure
Acute Bleeding Management:
  1. Airway protection (intubation if altered mental status)
  2. IV access x2, fluid resuscitation, blood products
  3. Octreotide (somatostatin analog) — reduces splanchnic blood flow
  4. Antibiotics prophylaxis (ceftriaxone) — reduces infection risk and mortality
  5. Endoscopic band ligation (EVL) or sclerotherapy
  6. Balloon tamponade (Sengstaken-Blakemore) if refractory
  7. TIPS if recurrent bleeding

Hepatic Encephalopathy

Pathophysiology: Accumulation of ammonia and other neurotoxins → altered neurotransmission

Manifestations: Confusion, asterixis (flapping tremor), constructional apraxia (can't draw star), sleep-wake reversal, fetor hepaticus (musty breath)
Management:
  • Lactulose: Traps ammonia in gut, promotes excretion. Titrate to 2-3 soft stools/day.
  • Rifaximin: Reduces ammonia-producing gut bacteria (add to lactulose if recurrent)
  • Treat precipitating cause: infection, GI bleed, constipation, sedatives, dehydration, high protein intake
NCLEX Trap: Hepatic encephalopathy is often precipitated by constipation, infection, GI bleeding, or high protein intake. Monitor ammonia levels but treat clinically (ammonia doesn't always correlate with severity). Do NOT restrict protein long-term (malnutrition worse than encephalopathy).

Hepatorenal Syndrome (HRS)

Definition: Functional renal failure in advanced liver disease without intrinsic kidney pathology

Diagnosis: Creatinine >1.5 mg/dL, no response to albumin challenge, no shock, no nephrotoxic drugs, no intrinsic renal disease
Management: Terlipressin + albumin (Type 1 HRS), TIPS, liver transplant (definitive). Avoid nephrotoxins (NSAIDs, aminoglycosides, contrast).

🦠 Hepatitis

Types Comparison

Type Transmission Chronic Risk Vaccine
HAV Fecal-oral No Yes
HBV Blood, sexual, perinatal 5-10% (adults), 90% (infants) Yes
HCV Blood (IVDU most common) 75-85% No
HDV Blood (requires HBV) Yes HBV vaccine prevents
HEV Fecal-oral (waterborne) No (except immunocompromised) No (available in some countries)
Standard Precautions for All: All hepatitis patients require standard precautions. HBV, HCV, HDV require additional blood/body fluid precautions.

Acute Hepatitis Manifestations

Prodromal: Fatigue, malaise, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, RUQ pain
Icteric: Jaundice, dark urine (bilirubinuria), pale stools (acholic), pruritus, hepatomegaly
Convalescent: Gradual resolution, may take weeks to months
Nursing Care: Rest, adequate nutrition (high calorie, moderate protein, low fat if nauseated), avoid alcohol and hepatotoxic drugs (acetaminophen), strict hand hygiene for HAV/HEV.

🩹 Pancreatitis

Acute Pancreatitis

Causes: Gallstones (most common), alcohol (2nd most common), hypertriglyceridemia, trauma, ERCP, medications, infections

Manifestations:
  • Severe epigastric pain radiating to back
  • Nausea, vomiting
  • Fever, tachycardia
  • Abdominal distension, guarding
  • Hypotension (3rd spacing, vasodilation)
  • Ecchymosis: Cullen's sign (periumbilical), Grey-Turner's sign (flank)
Management:
  • NPO with NG tube if vomiting/ileus
  • IV fluids — aggressive hydration (lactated Ringer's preferred)
  • Pain control: IV opioids (morphine, hydromorphone — meperidine NOT preferred due to neurotoxicity)
  • Antibiotics: Only if infected necrosis (NOT routine)
  • Nutrition: Early enteral feeding within 48-72h (reduces infection vs TPN)
NCLEX Trap: Do NOT give morphine? FALSE — morphine is acceptable (old myth about sphincter of Oddi spasm). Avoid IM injections (poor absorption in shock). Monitor for hypocalcemia (fat saponification), hyperglycemia, and hypoxemia (ARDS).

Chronic Pancreatitis

Manifestations: Recurrent epigastric pain, malabsorption (steatorrhea), weight loss, diabetes (islet cell destruction)

Management: Pancreatic enzyme replacement (take with meals), fat-soluble vitamin supplementation (A, D, E, K), analgesics, alcohol cessation, insulin if diabetic.

🧩 NGN Case Study: Hepatic Encephalopathy & Ascites

Client Scenario

Mr. James Carter, 58-year-old male, is admitted with worsening confusion and abdominal distention over the past week. His wife reports he has been "sleepy during the day but awake all night," has had trouble writing his name, and is increasingly irritable. He has a 20-year history of alcohol use disorder and was diagnosed with cirrhosis 3 years ago. He takes furosemide 40 mg daily and spironolactone 100 mg daily at home.

Vital Signs Nurse's Notes Lab Results Provider Orders

📊 Vital Signs & Assessment

  • BP: 98/62 mmHg
  • HR: 96 bpm, regular
  • RR: 18 breaths/min
  • Temp: 37.2°C (99.0°F)
  • SpO₂: 94% on room air
  • Weight: 78 kg (usual weight 68 kg)
  • Appearance: Jaundiced sclera, spider angiomas on chest, palmar erythema
  • Abdomen: Markedly distended, shifting dullness on percussion, fluid wave positive
  • Neuro: Oriented to person and place but not time, asterixis (hand flapping) present, unable to draw a clock correctly
  • Skin: Multiple ecchymoses, capillary refill 3 seconds

📝 Nurse's Notes

Patient's wife reports he stopped taking his diuretics 2 weeks ago because he "didn't think they were helping." She also reports he had a "stomach bug" with 2 days of loose stools last week. He has been eating poorly. Last alcohol use was 3 years ago at diagnosis. No history of GI bleeding. No fever until today.

🔬 Lab Results

  • AST: 142 U/L
  • ALT: 68 U/L
  • Total bilirubin: 4.8 mg/dL
  • Albumin: 2.4 g/dL
  • INR: 1.8
  • Ammonia: 98 mcg/dL (normal <50)
  • Sodium: 128 mEq/L
  • Potassium: 3.2 mEq/L
  • Creatinine: 1.6 mg/dL
  • WBC: 8.4 × 10⁹/L
  • Platelets: 88 × 10⁹/L

📋 Provider Orders

  1. Paracentesis — diagnostic and therapeutic (4-6L)
  2. Lactulose 30 mL PO TID, titrate to 3-4 soft stools/day
  3. Rifaximin 550 mg PO BID
  4. Albumin 25 g IV if >5L removed
  5. Restart furosemide 40 mg and spironolactone 100 mg PO daily
  6. Fluid restriction: 1.5L/day
  7. Low-sodium diet
  8. Liver ultrasound with Doppler

Clinical Judgment Questions

Question 1 — Recognize Cues: Which findings are consistent with hepatic encephalopathy (HE)? (Select all that apply)

  1. Day-night sleep reversal
  2. Asterixis (hand flapping)
  3. Inability to draw a clock
  4. Jaundiced sclera
  5. Irritability and confusion
  6. Spider angiomas
Correct: A, B, C, E
HE findings include neuropsychiatric changes: sleep-wake reversal (A), asterixis (B — specific to metabolic encephalopathies), constructional apraxia (C — can't draw clock/write), confusion/personality changes (E). Jaundice (D) and spider angiomas (F) are signs of chronic liver disease/cirrhosis but NOT specific to HE.

Question 2 — Analyze Cues: The nurse notes the AST is more than double the ALT. What does this pattern suggest?

  1. Acute viral hepatitis
  2. Alcoholic liver disease
  3. Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease
  4. Acetaminophen toxicity
Correct: B
AST:ALT ratio >2:1 is highly suggestive of alcoholic liver disease. Alcohol is toxic to mitochondria (AST is mitochondrial and cytosolic; ALT is cytosolic only), so AST rises more than ALT. In viral hepatitis (A) and NAFLD (C), ALT > AST. In acetaminophen toxicity (D), AST and ALT are massively elevated (>1000) with ratio near 1:1.

Question 3 — Prioritize Hypotheses: What is the MOST likely precipitant of this episode of hepatic encephalopathy?

  1. Nonadherence with diuretics causing fluid retention
  2. Electrolyte imbalance from diarrhea
  3. GI bleeding from esophageal varices
  4. Progression of cirrhosis to hepatocellular carcinoma
Correct: B
The patient had 2 days of diarrhea (electrolyte loss, especially K⁺), stopped diuretics (causing fluid/electrolyte shifts), and has hypokalemia (3.2). Hypokalemia is a major precipitant of HE because K⁺ deficiency causes intracellular K⁺ to shift out, and H⁺/NH₄⁺ to shift into cells, increasing ammonia delivery to the brain. While nonadherence (A) contributed, the electrolyte disturbance is the direct precipitant.

Question 4 — Generate Solutions: The provider orders lactulose 30 mL TID. Which statement by the nurse to the patient is MOST accurate?

  1. "Lactulose works by killing bacteria in your gut that produce ammonia."
  2. "Lactulose traps ammonia in your stool by making your colon more acidic."
  3. "Lactulose reduces ammonia production by your liver cells."
  4. "Lactulose works best if you take it with a large glass of water."
Correct: B
Lactulose is a non-absorbable disaccharide. Colonic bacteria metabolize it to lactic acid and acetic acid, lowering colonic pH. In acidic environment, ammonia (NH₃) converts to ammonium (NH₄⁺), which is trapped in stool and excreted. Rifaximin (antibiotic) kills ammonia-producing bacteria. Lactulose does NOT act on liver cells (C) and water does not affect its mechanism (D).

Question 5 — Take Action: During paracentesis, 5L of clear yellow fluid is removed. The patient's BP drops to 84/50. What is the nurse's BEST immediate action?

  1. Call the provider and request albumin replacement
  2. Position the patient supine and elevate legs
  3. Increase the IV fluid rate to compensate
  4. Stop the procedure and apply pressure
Correct: A
Large-volume paracentesis (>5L) can cause circulatory dysfunction (post-paracentesis circulatory dysfunction) due to rapid fluid shifts. Guidelines recommend albumin 6-8 g per liter removed when >5L is removed. While positioning (B) helps, albumin is the specific treatment. IV fluids (C) can worsen ascites. The procedure can continue (D is not the priority).

Question 6 — Evaluate Outcomes: On day 3, the patient is alert and oriented, ammonia is 52, and he is having 3 soft stools daily. Which dietary instruction is MOST appropriate at discharge?

  1. "You should follow a high-protein diet to help your liver regenerate."
  2. "Limit sodium to 2 grams per day and avoid alcohol completely."
  3. "Drink at least 3 liters of water daily to prevent dehydration."
  4. "Avoid all protein since it worsens hepatic encephalopathy."
Correct: B
Sodium restriction (2g/day) is essential for ascites management. Absolute alcohol abstinence is critical — even small amounts worsen cirrhosis. Protein restriction (D) is no longer recommended in HE — patients need adequate protein to prevent muscle wasting (muscle metabolizes ammonia). Fluid restriction (not increased intake, C) is typically needed in hyponatremia/ascites.
🧠 Case Study Learning Points:
  • AST:ALT >2:1 = alcohol — Classic pattern for alcoholic liver disease
  • HE precipitants: P-D-G-H-E-L-P-S — Protein excess, Drugs, GI bleed, Hypokalemia, Electrolytes, Liver failure, Paracentesis, Sepsis/Sedatives
  • Lactulose acidifies colon — Traps NH₃ as NH₄⁺ in stool
  • Albumin for >5L removed — Prevents post-paracentesis circulatory dysfunction
  • Do NOT restrict protein — Modern guidelines: adequate protein prevents muscle wasting
