NCLEX Maternity Study Guide

Pregnancy, labor & delivery, postpartum, newborn care, and high-risk OB conditions — everything you need for the NCLEX.

1. Pregnancy Physiology

Cardiovascular Changes

  • Cardiac output ↑ 30-50% (peaks at 28-32 weeks)
  • Blood volume ↑ 40-50% (physiologic anemia is normal)
  • BP ↓ slightly in 2nd trimester (lowest at 24-32 weeks), returns to baseline by 3rd trimester
  • HR ↑ 10-15 bpm
BP >140/90 after 20 weeks = hypertension in pregnancy
• Chronic hypertension: Before 20 weeks or persists 12 weeks postpartum
• Gestational hypertension: After 20 weeks, no proteinuria
• Preeclampsia: Hypertension + proteinuria (>300mg/24h) or end-organ dysfunction

2. Preeclampsia & Eclampsia

Preeclampsia Assessment

ParameterMildSevere
BP≥140/90 (×2, 4h apart)≥160/110 (×2, 4h apart)
Proteinuria≥300 mg/24h or 1+ dipstick≥2 g/24h or 3-4+ dipstick
SymptomsUsually asymptomaticHA, visual changes, RUQ/epigastric pain, oliguria, hyperreflexia

Nursing Priorities for Preeclampsia:
• Seizure precautions (padded side rails, dim lights, quiet environment)
• Magnesium sulfate prophylaxis (check deep tendon reflexes, respiratory rate >12, urine output >30 mL/hr)
• Monitor BP q15-30min if severe
• Monitor for signs of worsening (HELLP syndrome)
• Strict I&O, daily weights
• Fetal monitoring (NST, BPP)
MNEMONIC: "MAG" — Magnesium Toxicity Signs
Mental status changes (lethargy, confusion)
Absent reflexes (patellar reflex lost first)
Greatly decreased respirations (<12/min)
Antidote: Calcium gluconate 1g IV

4. Fetal Heart Rate Patterns

PatternDescriptionSignificanceNursing Action
Early DecelerationsMirror image of contraction, gradual onsetHead compression — NORMALContinue monitoring
Late DecelerationsBegin after peak of contraction, gradualUteroplacental insufficiency — NONREASSURINGPosition change (left lateral), O₂, IV fluids, stop oxytocin, notify provider
Variable DecelerationsAbrupt onset, V-shaped, variable timingCord compression — NONREASSURINGPosition change (knee-chest, left lateral), amnioinfusion if recurrent, notify provider
Prolonged Deceleration>2 min but <10 minUteroplacental insufficiency, cord compression, maternal hypotensionImmediate interventions, prepare for delivery if not resolved
LATE DECELERATIONS = Priority Action
When you see late decelerations on the NCLEX, think: STOP the oxytocin, turn patient to left side, give O₂, increase IV fluids, and notify the provider. Late decels indicate placental insufficiency and require immediate intervention.

5. Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH)

Causes: "4 T's"

CauseDescriptionIntervention
Tone (most common)Uterine atony — boggy, soft uterusUterine massage, oxytocin, methylergonovine (contraindicated if HTN), carboprost (contraindicated if asthma), misoprostol
TraumaLacerations, hematomas, uterine inversionRepair lacerations, manual replacement of inverted uterus
TissueRetained placenta, clotsManual extraction, D&C
ThrombinCoagulopathy (DIC)Blood products, treat underlying cause
Nursing Priorities for PPH:
• Massage fundus until firm
• Check for bladder distention (catheterize if needed)
• Monitor vital signs q15 min
• Measure blood loss (weigh pads: 1 g = 1 mL)
• Maintain IV access, prepare for blood transfusion
• Keep patient warm (prevent coagulopathy from hypothermia)

7. High-Risk Conditions

Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM)

  • Screen at 24-28 weeks (1-hour 50g glucose challenge)
  • If >130-140 mg/dL → 3-hour 100g OGTT (need 2 abnormal values)
  • Target glucose: Fasting <95, 1-hour <140, 2-hour <120
  • Diet first, then insulin if needed (glyburide and metformin may be used but insulin is gold standard)
  • Complications: Macrosomia, shoulder dystocia, birth trauma, neonatal hypoglycemia

NGN Case Study: Preeclampsia with Severe Features

Client Scenario

Mrs. Lee, 32-year-old G2P1 at 34 weeks gestation, presents to the OB triage unit with a severe headache that started 6 hours ago and won't go away with acetaminophen. She also reports seeing "spots" in her vision and swelling in her hands and face.

Vital Signs Physical Assessment Labs

Vital Signs: BP 168/110, HR 92, RR 18, Temp 37.0°C, SpO₂ 98%

Physical Assessment: Alert and oriented. 3+ pitting edema bilateral lower extremities and hands. Facial edema present. Deep tendon reflexes 3+ with 2 beats of clonus at ankles. Epigastric tenderness on palpation. FHR 140s with moderate variability.

Labs: Platelets 98,000, Creatinine 1.3, AST 78, ALT 85, LDH 450, Urine protein 3+ on dipstick

Question 1 — Recognize Cues: Which finding confirms the diagnosis of preeclampsia with severe features?

  • A. Blood pressure 168/110 at 34 weeks
  • B. Proteinuria 3+
  • C. Platelet count 98,000
  • D. All of the above

Correct: D. All of the above

Preeclampsia is defined as BP ≥140/90 after 20 weeks with proteinuria OR signs of end-organ damage. Severe features include: BP ≥160/110, thrombocytopenia (<100,000), elevated liver enzymes (AST/ALT), renal insufficiency (Cr >1.1), pulmonary edema, new-onset cerebral/visual symptoms, or epigastric pain. This patient has ALL of these: severe BP, proteinuria, low platelets, elevated LFTs, elevated creatinine, visual disturbances (spots), and epigastric pain (indicates liver capsule stretch).

Question 2 — Analyze Cues: The clonus and hyperreflexia indicate which imminent complication?

  • A. Placental abruption
  • B. Eclampsia (seizure)
  • C. Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)
  • D. HELLP syndrome

Correct: B. Eclampsia (seizure)

Hyperreflexia and clonus are signs of neuromuscular irritability from cerebral edema and are the hallmark warning signs of impending eclampsia (seizure). The seizure threshold is lowered in preeclampsia due to cerebral vasospasm and edema. Magnesium sulfate is the anticonvulsant of choice to prevent eclampsia. While HELLP and DIC are risks in preeclampsia, clonus specifically predicts seizure activity.

Question 3 — Prioritize Hypotheses: Magnesium sulfate is ordered: 4g IV loading dose, then 2g/hr continuous infusion. Which safety measure is highest priority?

  • A. Ensure calcium gluconate is at the bedside
  • B. Monitor deep tendon reflexes hourly
  • C. Check magnesium level every 6 hours
  • D. Maintain strict intake and output

Correct: A. Ensure calcium gluconate is at the bedside

Calcium gluconate is the antidote for magnesium sulfate toxicity. Magnesium toxicity signs: loss of DTRs (first sign), respiratory depression, cardiac arrest. While monitoring DTRs (B) is important, having the antidote immediately available is the highest safety priority. DTRs should be checked q1-2h, magnesium levels are not always checked routinely, and I&O monitoring is important but secondary to antidote availability.

Question 4 — Generate Solutions: The provider discusses delivery timing. Mrs. Lee is 34 weeks. Which statement about management is correct?

  • A. Delivery should be delayed until 37 weeks if mother and fetus are stable
  • B. Immediate delivery is indicated regardless of gestational age
  • C. Steroids for fetal lung maturity should be given, then deliver at 34 weeks
  • D. Preeclampsia with severe features requires delivery after maternal stabilization

Correct: D. Preeclampsia with severe features requires delivery after maternal stabilization

Definitive treatment for preeclampsia is delivery. With severe features at ≥34 weeks, delivery is indicated after maternal stabilization (control BP with labetalol or hydralazine, start magnesium). At 34 weeks, steroids are not typically given (benefit is for <34 weeks). Waiting until 37 weeks (A) is NOT appropriate with severe features. Immediate delivery without stabilization (B) could be dangerous if BP is uncontrolled.

Question 5 — Take Action: Mrs. Lee begins having a seizure (eclampsia). What is the priority nursing action?

  • A. Turn patient on her left side and administer oxygen
  • B. Push additional magnesium sulfate 2g IV
  • C. Call for immediate cesarean section
  • D. Insert a Foley catheter to monitor output

Correct: A. Turn patient on her left side and administer oxygen

During an eclamptic seizure, first ensure safety: turn on left side (prevents aspiration of vomit, improves uteroplacental perfusion), pad side rails, administer O₂, and do NOT restrain. After the seizure, give additional magnesium if ordered. The priority during the active seizure is maternal-fetal safety, not medication administration. C-section is considered after the seizure is controlled and mother is stabilized.

Question 6 — Evaluate Outcomes: Mrs. Lee delivers a healthy infant via cesarean. On post-op day 2, her BP is 148/94. She asks when she can go home. What is the nurse's best response?

  • A. "You can go home today since your BP is improving"
  • B. "You need to stay until your BP is normal without medications"
  • C. "Preeclampsia can worsen after delivery — we need to monitor you for at least 48-72 hours postpartum"
  • D. "Your baby needs to be observed in the NICU, so you'll stay until discharge"

Correct: C. "Preeclampsia can worsen after delivery — we need to monitor you for at least 48-72 hours postpartum"

Postpartum preeclampsia/eclampsia can occur up to 6 weeks after delivery, with the highest risk in the first 48-72 hours. BP often worsens before improving. Magnesium is typically continued for 24 hours postpartum. Patients need continued BP monitoring and education on warning signs (headache, vision changes, epigastric pain) before discharge. BP does not need to be normal without meds to discharge — many patients need antihypertensives postpartum.
