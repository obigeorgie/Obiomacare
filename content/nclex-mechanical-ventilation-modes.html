Mechanical Ventilation for NCLEX: Modes, Settings & Nursing Care

Ventilator modes, alarms, suctioning, sedation, and weaning. The critical care concepts the NCLEX tests — explained with clinical scenarios.

By Nnamdi Okorafor, RN · 15-year ER/ICU nurse · Updated August 2026 · Obioma

Why ventilation matters on the NCLEX: Even if you don't work in ICU, you'll get questions on ventilator basics, alarm responses, and patient safety. The NGN case studies often include a post-op or deteriorating patient who may need ventilatory support.

Ventilator Modes: What Each One Does

ModeWhat It DoesWhen UsedNursing Priority
AC (Assist-Control)Delivers set tidal volume with every breath; patient can trigger extra breathsInitial ventilation, full supportMonitor for respiratory muscle atrophy; don't stay on too long
SIMV (Synchronized Intermittent Mandatory Ventilation)Set number of mandatory breaths; patient breathes spontaneously betweenWeaning, partial supportWatch for fatigue during spontaneous breaths
PSV (Pressure Support Ventilation)Patient initiates all breaths; vent adds pressure supportWeaning, spontaneous breathing trialsMonitor respiratory rate and effort
CPAPContinuous positive pressure; patient breathes spontaneouslyObstructive sleep apnea, mild respiratory failureMonitor for barotrauma, gastric distension
BiPAPTwo pressure levels: higher on inhale (IPAP), lower on exhale (EPAP)COPD, CHF, avoid intubationCheck mask fit; skin integrity at pressure points
APRV (Airway Pressure Release Ventilation)High pressure with brief releases; allows spontaneous breathingSevere ARDS, refractory hypoxemiaMonitor for hemodynamic instability

Key Ventilator Settings

SettingDescriptionTypical Range
FiO₂Fraction of inspired oxygen21% (room air) to 100%
Tidal Volume (Vt)Amount of air per breath6-8 mL/kg ideal body weight
Respiratory Rate (RR)Breaths per minute set by vent12-20/min
PEEPPositive end-expiratory pressure; keeps alveoli open5-15 cmH₂O (ARDS may need 15+)
Peak Inspiratory Pressure (PIP)Maximum pressure during breath<35 cmH₂O
Plateau PressurePressure at end-inspiration with no flow<30 cmH₂O
I:E RatioInspiration to expiration ratio1:2 to 1:3

NCLEX Tip — Lung Protective Strategy: Keep plateau pressure <30 cmH₂O and tidal volume 6 mL/kg ideal body weight. Higher pressures cause barotrauma and volutrauma. This is the standard for ARDS management.

Ventilator Alarms: What They Mean & What You Do

AlarmWhat It MeansYour First Action
High pressureResistance increased: secretions, kinked tubing, bronchospasm, patient biting tube, pneumothoraxCheck for kinks, suction if indicated, ensure patient not biting, auscultate lungs
Low pressureLeak in system: disconnected tubing, cuff leak, chest tube leakCheck all connections, inflate cuff if needed, check chest tube
Low exhaled volumeAir leak or patient not receiving set volumeCheck tubing connections, cuff pressure, patient-ventilator synchrony
ApneaNo spontaneous breaths detectedAssess patient (sedated? paralyzed?); if not intentional, check sensitivity settings
High respiratory ratePatient tachypneic: anxiety, pain, hypoxemia, acidosisAssess patient, check ABG, check SpO₂, consider sedation

First response to ANY vent alarm: Look at the patient first, then the machine. The patient will tell you if it's a critical problem (turning blue, desaturating, distressed) or a tubing issue.

Endotracheal Tube (ETT) Care

ETT Position

  • Verify placement with chest X-ray after insertion
  • Tip should be 3-5 cm above the carina
  • Mark the depth at the lip/teeth; check every shift
  • If tube dislodged >2 cm from mark → notify provider, prepare for reintubation

Cuff Pressure

  • Maintain 20-30 cmH₂O (or minimal occlusive volume)
  • Too low → aspiration risk
  • Too high → tracheal necrosis, stenosis
  • Check every 8 hours with manometer

Oral Care for Ventilated Patients

  • Brush teeth and gums every 12 hours
  • Chlorhexidine oral rinse every 12 hours — reduces VAP
  • Check ETT securement; retape PRN
  • Maintain head of bed 30-45°

Suctioning

Open vs. Closed Suctioning

TypeWhen UsedAdvantages
Open suctionSingle use, sterile technique requiredFull sterility, can assess secretions
Closed suction (inline)ICU, frequent suctioning neededMaintains PEEP, reduces contamination, less hypoxemia

Suctioning Steps

  1. Pre-oxygenate with 100% FiO₂ for 30-60 seconds
  2. Insert catheter without suction until resistance (carina)
  3. Pull back 1 cm, then apply suction
  4. Withdraw catheter over 10-15 seconds max
  5. Re-oxygenate after suctioning
  6. Limit to 2-3 passes

NCLEX Tip — Suctioning Time Limit: Never suction longer than 10-15 seconds. Longer causes hypoxemia, bradycardia (vagal response), and arrhythmias. If you see bradycardia during suctioning → stop immediately, bag the patient, call for help.

Sedation & Paralysis in Ventilated Patients

Sedation Goals (RASS Scale)

  • Target RASS: -2 to 0 (lightly sedated to alert and calm)
  • Daily sedation interruption (DSI) to assess neurologic status
  • Propofol: fast onset/offset, good for DSI
  • Midazolam: longer half-life, accumulates in renal failure
  • Dexmedetomidine (Precedex): arousable sedation, no respiratory depression

Neuromuscular Blockade (Paralytics)

  • Used for severe ARDS, ventilator dyssynchrony, ↑ ICP
  • Patient cannot breathe spontaneously — vent does all work
  • Never paralyze without sedation → awake paralysis = torture
  • Monitor with train-of-four (peripheral nerve stimulator)
  • Deep vein thrombosis prophylaxis essential (immobile)
  • Eye care every 2-4 hours (can't blink)

Weaning from the Ventilator

Readiness Criteria

  • Underlying cause resolved or improving
  • FiO₂ ≤ 40%, PEEP ≤ 5-8 cmH₂O
  • Hemodynamically stable (no/minimal pressors)
  • Able to protect airway (cough, gag)
  • Adequate oxygenation: PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio > 150-200
  • Adequate ventilation: pH ≥ 7.35, not severely hypercapnic

Weaning Methods

MethodDescriptionWhen Used
Spontaneous Breathing Trial (SBT)Disconnect from vent, use T-piece or low CPAP/PSV for 30-120 minMost common first step; tests ability to breathe independently
Gradual PSV weanDecrease pressure support by 2-4 cmH₂O every 2-4 hoursPatients who fail initial SBT
SIMV weanGradually decrease mandatory breathsLess preferred; increases work of breathing

NCLEX Tip — Extubation Criteria: Before extubation, the patient must: pass SBT, be awake and following commands, have adequate cough/gag, have manageable secretions, and be hemodynamically stable. The #1 reason for extubation failure is inadequate airway protection.

Post-Extubation Care

  • Humidified O₂ (prevents mucus plugging)
  • Incentive spirometry (encourage use hourly while awake)
  • Monitor for stridor (laryngeal edema from ETT) — may need racemic epinephrine
  • Vocal rest initially (hoarseness common)
  • Swallowing assessment before oral intake

ARDS & Prone Positioning

ARDS Criteria (Berlin Definition)

  • Onset within 1 week of known insult
  • Bilateral infiltrates on chest X-ray
  • Respiratory failure not from cardiac cause
  • PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio: Mild 200-300, Moderate 100-200, Severe <100

Prone Positioning

  • Used for severe ARDS (PaO₂/FiO₂ < 150)
  • Improves V/Q matching, recruits alveoli, reduces ventilator-induced lung injury
  • Minimum 16 hours/day
  • Contraindications: spinal instability, increased ICP, pregnancy, hemodynamic instability
  • Complications: facial edema, pressure injuries, tube dislodgement

NGN Case Study: Ventilator Alarm Response

Client Scenario

Mr. Chen, 68-year-old male, is intubated and on mechanical ventilation in the ICU following COPD exacerbation and pneumonia. He is on AC/VC mode: TV 500, RR 14, FiO₂ 40%, PEEP 5. He is mildly sedated with propofol. The ventilator alarms.

Vital Signs Ventilator Data Physical Assessment

Vital Signs: BP 138/82, HR 95, RR 24 (patient-initiated breaths above set rate), SpO₂ 88%

Ventilator: High pressure alarm sounding. Peak pressure 55 cmH₂O (baseline 28). Plateau pressure 18. Tidal volume delivered 320 mL.

Physical Assessment: Patient appears anxious, biting on tube, trying to sit up. Diminished breath sounds bilaterally. No wheezing. Chest rise appears asymmetrical. Trachea midline.

Question 1 — Recognize Cues: What is the most likely cause of the high pressure alarm?

  • A. Worsening pneumonia with increased secretions
  • B. Patient-ventilator asynchrony/biting the tube
  • C. Pneumothorax
  • D. Mucus plug causing atelectasis

Correct: B. Patient-ventilator asynchrony/biting the tube

The key clue is the NORMAL plateau pressure (18) with elevated peak pressure (55). This indicates increased AIRWAY resistance, not compliance issues. The patient is biting the tube (causing obstruction) and fighting the ventilator. Plateau pressure reflects alveolar pressure/compliance — if it were elevated too, we'd suspect pneumothorax, ARDS, or mucus plug. With normal plateau and high peak, the problem is in the airway/tubing.

Question 2 — Analyze Cues: The nurse disconnects the ventilator and manually bags the patient with 100% O₂. Breath sounds are equal bilaterally and the patient settles. After reconnecting, the high pressure alarm continues. What is the next priority action?

  • A. Increase the propofol sedation drip
  • B. Suction the endotracheal tube
  • C. Check for kinks in the ventilator tubing
  • D. Prepare for emergency chest tube insertion

Correct: C. Check for kinks in the ventilator tubing

Since manual bagging worked fine (equal breath sounds, patient improved), the patient's lungs and airway are patent. The problem is likely in the ventilator circuit — kinked tubing, water in the tubing, or a blocked HME filter. The NCLEX prioritizes "check the tubing first" because it's the quickest fix and most common cause of ventilator alarms. Suctioning and sedation are appropriate interventions but not the FIRST step when manual ventilation works normally.

Question 3 — Prioritize Hypotheses: The tubing is clear. The nurse suctioned moderate thick yellow secretions. The patient's SpO₂ is now 94% but his RR is 28 and he's using accessory muscles. Which intervention should the nurse implement first?

  • A. Increase the tidal volume to 600 mL
  • B. Increase FiO₂ to 60%
  • C. Assess patient-ventilator synchrony and adjust sensitivity
  • D. Administer a PRN bolus of propofol

Correct: C. Assess patient-ventilator synchrony and adjust sensitivity

The patient is tachypneic and using accessory muscles despite the ventilator — this indicates he's "fighting" the vent (dyssynchrony). The trigger sensitivity may be set too high, or the flow rate may be inadequate for his demand. Adjusting synchrony will reduce his work of breathing. Simply increasing TV or FiO₂ doesn't address the underlying problem of dyssynchrony. Sedation may be needed but first optimize the ventilator settings.

Question 4 — Generate Solutions: The RT changes the ventilator to PS (pressure support) mode: PS 10, PEEP 5, FiO₂ 40%. Two hours later, the ABG shows: pH 7.48, PaCO₂ 30, PaO₂ 85, HCO₃ 22. What is the nurse's priority response?

  • A. Decrease pressure support to reduce minute ventilation
  • B. Increase FiO₂ to maintain PaO₂ >100
  • C. Continue current settings — ABG is acceptable
  • D. Prepare for spontaneous breathing trial

Correct: A. Decrease pressure support to reduce minute ventilation

The patient is hyperventilating (pH 7.48, PaCO₂ 30) — he's receiving too much support. In PS mode, the patient controls the rate and tidal volume, but too much PS can lead to respiratory alkalosis. Decreasing PS will allow the patient to do more work and normalize PaCO₂. PaO₂ of 85 is acceptable on 40% FiO₂. An SBT is premature with an abnormal ABG.

Question 5 — Take Action: On day 5, the patient meets weaning criteria. The provider orders a spontaneous breathing trial (SBT). Which parameter indicates the SBT is successful?

  • A. Respiratory rate 28, SpO₂ 90%, no distress
  • B. Respiratory rate 22, SpO₂ 96%, mild use of accessory muscles
  • C. Respiratory rate 18, SpO₂ 94%, comfortable, no diaphoresis
  • D. Respiratory rate 14, SpO₂ 98%, slightly drowsy

Correct: C. Respiratory rate 18, SpO₂ 94%, comfortable, no diaphoresis

Successful SBT criteria: RR <30, SpO₂ >90%, no increased work of breathing, no diaphoresis, no arrhythmias, stable hemodynamics. Option C meets all criteria. Option A has tachypnea (28). Option B has accessory muscle use (indicates distress). Option D has drowsiness which could indicate CO₂ retention/hypercapnia — check ABG before extubation.

Question 6 — Evaluate Outcomes: The patient passes the SBT and is extubated to nasal cannula 4L. Thirty minutes later, his voice is hoarse and he has a mild barking cough. What is the most likely cause?

  • A. Aspiration pneumonia
  • B. Post-extubation stridor from laryngeal edema
  • C. Exacerbation of underlying COPD
  • D. Development of pulmonary embolism

Correct: B. Post-extubation stridor from laryngeal edema

Hoarseness and barking cough immediately after extubation strongly suggest laryngeal edema from endotracheal tube trauma. This is common, especially if the tube was in place for several days. Treatment: racemic epinephrine nebulizer, IV steroids (dexamethasone), cool humidified O₂. Monitor closely — may need reintubation if stridor worsens. The timing (30 min post-extubation) makes aspiration, COPD exacerbation, and PE less likely.
