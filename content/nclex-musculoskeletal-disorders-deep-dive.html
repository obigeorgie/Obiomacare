Musculoskeletal Disorders Deep Dive

Fractures, osteoporosis, osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, gout, and connective tissue disorders for the NCLEX.

🦴 Fractures

Types & Classifications

  • Complete vs. incomplete: Complete breaks through both cortices; incomplete (greenstick in children) — one cortex intact
  • Open (compound) vs. closed: Open = skin broken; closed = skin intact
  • Comminuted: Bone shattered into 3+ fragments
  • Pathologic: Fracture through diseased bone (tumor, osteoporosis)
  • Stress: Overuse, microtrauma (metatarsals, tibia)

Clinical Manifestations

5 P's of neurovascular compromise:

  • Pain (out of proportion, unrelieved by analgesics)
  • Pallor (pale, cool skin)
  • Pulselessness (absent or diminished distal pulse)
  • Paresthesia (numbness, tingling)
  • Paralysis (inability to move extremity)

Also: deformity, crepitus, edema, ecchymosis

Mnemonic: "5 P's" — Pain, Pallor, Pulselessness, Paresthesia, Paralysis. Assess neurovascular status EVERY HOUR for first 24–48 hours post-injury or post-cast.
Compartment Syndrome — EMERGENCY: Increased pressure within fascial compartment → compromised blood flow → tissue necrosis. Pain with passive stretch is EARLIEST sign. Pallor, pulselessness are LATE signs. Treatment: emergent fasciotomy. Do NOT elevate extremity above heart (reduces perfusion). Remove constrictive dressings immediately.
NCLEX Trap: With compartment syndrome, NEVER wait for all 5 P's — pain with passive stretch + increasing analgesic requirements are EARLY signs. Also, with open fractures, give tetanus prophylaxis and broad-spectrum antibiotics (cephalosporin + aminoglycoside if contaminated) immediately.

🦴 Osteoporosis

Pathophysiology

Decreased bone mass and deterioration of bone microarchitecture → increased fracture risk. Type I (postmenopausal): trabecular bone loss, wrist/vertebral fractures. Type II (age-related): cortical and trabecular loss, hip fractures.

Risk Factors

  • Female, postmenopausal, advanced age, Caucasian/Asian, low BMI
  • Family history, smoking, excessive alcohol, sedentary lifestyle
  • Long-term corticosteroids, thyroid hormone excess, anticonvulsants
  • Low calcium/vitamin D intake, malabsorption, anorexia

Prevention & Treatment

  • Calcium: 1,200 mg/day (women >50, men >70)
  • Vitamin D: 800–1,000 IU/day
  • Weight-bearing exercise: Walking, resistance training
  • Fall prevention: Home safety, vision checks, balance training
  • Bisphosphonates (alendronate, risedronate): Take first thing AM with full glass water, remain upright 30–60 min. Risk: atypical femur fractures, osteonecrosis of jaw
  • Denosumab (Prolia): SQ every 6 months. Risk: hypocalcemia, infection
  • Teriparatide (Forteo): Anabolic agent, daily SQ. Limit 2 years. Risk: osteosarcoma ( Boxed warning)
NCLEX Trap: Bisphosphonates MUST be taken with plain water only (not juice, coffee, milk) and patient must stay upright 30–60 minutes to prevent esophageal irritation/ulceration. Also, bone density testing (DEXA) — T-score ≤-2.5 = osteoporosis.

🦴 Gout

Pathophysiology

Hyperuricemia → monosodium urate crystal deposition in joints → intense inflammation. Big toe (podagra) most common first site. Risk factors: obesity, alcohol (especially beer), purine-rich foods (red meat, shellfish), diuretics, chronic kidney disease, male sex.

Acute Gout

  • Sudden onset, severe pain, redness, warmth, swelling
  • First MTP joint (podagra) — 50% of first attacks
  • May involve ankle, knee, wrist, fingers
  • Fever, malaise

Gout Treatment

Acute:

  • NSAIDs: First-line (indomethacin, naproxen) — take until 48 hours after symptoms resolve
  • Colchicine: Effective if started within 36 hours. Side effects: GI upset (nausea, diarrhea). Dose reduce with renal/hepatic impairment
  • Corticosteroids: Oral prednisone or intra-articular injection if NSAIDs/colchicine contraindicated

Chronic prophylaxis (start after acute attack resolves):

  • Allopurinol: Reduces uric acid production. Start low, titrate. Side effects: rash (rare but serious hypersensitivity syndrome), GI upset
  • Febuxostat: Alternative if allopurinol intolerant. CV risk warning
  • Probenecid: Uricosuric — increases renal excretion. Contraindicated if kidney stones or CrCl <30
NCLEX Trap: Do NOT start allopurinol during acute gout attack — it can worsen symptoms. Wait until 2 weeks after attack resolves. Also, encourage hydration (prevents uric acid kidney stones) and limit alcohol, especially beer.

🦴 Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)

Diagnostic Criteria

  • Skin: Malar rash (butterfly), discoid rash, photosensitivity
  • Joints: Non-erosive polyarthritis
  • Serous: Pleuritis, pericarditis
  • Renal: Proteinuria >0.5 g/day, cellular casts (lupus nephritis)
  • Neurologic: Seizures, psychosis
  • Hematologic: Hemolytic anemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia
  • Immunologic: Positive ANA, anti-dsDNA, anti-Smith, antiphospholipid antibodies
Mnemonic: "RASH OR PAIN" — Renal, ANA, Serositis, Hematologic, Oral/nasal ulcers, Renal (again), Photosensitivity, Arthritis, Immunologic, Neurologic
SLE Nursing Priority: Sun protection (photosensitivity). Monitor renal function (lupus nephritis is leading cause of morbidity). Assess for infection (immunosuppressive therapy). Screen for antiphospholipid syndrome (clots, miscarriages). Pregnancy counseling — high-risk, flares common.

🎯 Clinical Judgment Case Study

Musculoskeletal Compartment Syndrome

Case: Post-Fracture Complication

A 25-year-old male had ORIF of his right tibia 8 hours ago. He reports severe, escalating pain that is "out of proportion" to the injury. His toes are pale, and he has decreased sensation on the dorsum of his foot. Pain medication is not helping.

What is the nurse's immediate action?

  1. Administer stronger IV opioids
  2. Remove the cast/splint and elevate the leg above heart level
  3. Notify the surgeon immediately — suspect compartment syndrome
  4. Apply ice and reassess in 30 minutes
Answer: 3 — Notify surgeon immediately for compartment syndrome.

Classic "6 Ps" of compartment syndrome: Pain (out of proportion), Pallor, Pulselessness, Paresthesia, Paralysis, Poikilothermia. This is a surgical emergency — fasciotomy must be performed within 6 hours to prevent irreversible muscle/nerve damage. Elevation is CONTRAINDICATED (reduces perfusion further). Ice reduces blood flow. Stronger opioids mask symptoms. The cast should be bivalved ONLY while awaiting surgery.
