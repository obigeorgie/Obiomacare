NCLEX Musculoskeletal Disorders

Fractures, arthritis, joint replacements, osteoporosis, and compartment syndrome — nursing priorities and NCLEX traps.

1. Fractures

Types of Fractures

TypeDescriptionNursing Priority
Closed (simple)Skin intactImmobilize, neurovascular checks, pain management
Open (compound)Bone protrudes through skinCover with sterile dressing, antibiotics, tetanus, surgery ASAP
GreenstickIncomplete fracture (common in children)Cast immobilization, monitor for complete fracture

SpiralTwisting force, wraps around boneMay be unstable, assess for compartment syndrome

CompressionVertebral body collapsesAssess for spinal cord injury, osteoporosis workup

Pain (out of proportion) | Pallor | Pulselessness | Paresthesia | Paralysis | Pokilothermia (coldness)
Fracture Nursing Priorities:
• Neurovascular checks distal to injury (q15min initially)
• Immobilize before moving patient
• Elevation (above heart level) to reduce edema
• Ice (first 48h, 15-20 min intervals)
• Pain assessment and management
• Monitor for fat embolism syndrome (3-5 days post-injury)
• DVT prophylaxis (if immobilized)

2. Fat Embolism Syndrome (FES)

Life-Threatening Complication (Usually 24-72h Post-Fracture)

Classic Triad:
• Respiratory distress (tachypnea, hypoxemia, petechiae)
• Neurological changes (confusion, restlessness, decreased LOC)
• Petechial rash (chest, axilla, conjunctiva — pathognomonic)
Nursing Priorities:
• High-flow O₂, prepare for intubation
• Serial ABGs
• Strict bed rest (movement mobilizes more fat)
• IV fluids (prevent shock)
• Corticosteroids (controversial but may be ordered)
• Petechiae are LATE sign — don't wait for them to diagnose!
NCLEX Trap: Fat embolism typically occurs 24-72 hours after long bone fracture (femur, tibia, pelvis). If a patient suddenly develops respiratory distress and confusion 2 days after a femur fracture, think FES — NOT pulmonary embolism (though PE is also possible).

3. Compartment Syndrome

Increased Pressure in Fascial Compartment → Ischemia → Necrosis

Causes: Fracture, crush injury, burns, tight cast, bleeding into compartment
Classic Sign: Pain out of proportion, pain with PASSIVE stretch (most sensitive sign)
Nursing Priorities:
• Neurovascular checks q1h
• Elevate extremity to HEART LEVEL (NOT above — reduces perfusion)
• REMOVE constrictive dressings, splints, casts if suspected
• Do NOT ice (causes vasoconstriction)
• Measure compartment pressures (normal <10 mmHg; >30-40 mmHg = emergency)
• Fasciotomy is definitive treatment — notify surgeon IMMEDIATELY
NCLEX Trap: Do NOT elevate above heart level in suspected compartment syndrome — this further reduces perfusion. Ice is also contraindicated. The definitive treatment is EMERGENCY fasciotomy. "Assess and monitor" is NOT the correct answer when compartment syndrome is suspected — rapid surgical intervention is required.

5. Total Hip Replacement (THR)

Post-Op Nursing Care

Positioning (CRITICAL for NCLEX):
• Posterior approach (MOST COMMON): AVOID hip flexion >90°, adduction past midline, internal rotation
• Use abduction pillow between legs
• Elevated toilet seat and chair
• Do NOT cross legs
• Sleep on back or non-operative side with pillow between knees
• Anterior approach: AVOID excessive external rotation and extension (less restrictive)
NCLEX Trap: Posterior approach precautions are the DEFAULT on NCLEX unless "anterior approach" is specified. Remember: "Don't bend past 90, don't cross legs, don't turn toe inward."
Complications to Monitor:
• Dislocation (sudden severe pain, leg shortening, external rotation)
• DVT/PE (calf pain, Homans' sign is UNRELIABLE)
• Infection (fever, wound drainage, increased pain)
• Neurovascular changes

6. Total Knee Replacement (TKR)

Post-Op Nursing Care

Nursing Priorities:
• CPM (Continuous Passive Motion) machine — increases ROM, reduces stiffness
• Elevate leg with pillow under calf (NOT under knee — causes flexion contracture)
• Ice and compression
• Early ambulation (day of or POD1)
• DVT prophylaxis
• Pain management (multimodal: acetaminophen + NSAID + opioid PRN)
• Watch for signs of infection, DVT

7. Osteoporosis

Risk Factors and Prevention

Risk Factors: Postmenopausal women, Caucasian/Asian, family history, small frame, smoking, excessive alcohol, low calcium/Vit D, sedentary lifestyle, long-term steroids, hyperthyroidism
Nursing Priorities:
• Fall prevention (remove rugs, adequate lighting, assistive devices)
• Weight-bearing exercise
• Calcium (1200 mg/day) and Vitamin D (800-1000 IU/day)
• Smoking cessation, limit alcohol
• DXA scan for screening (women >65, men >70)
• Medications: Bisphosphonates (take with full glass of water, remain upright 30-60 min), Denosumab, Raloxifene
NCLEX Trap: Bisphosphonates (alendronate, risedronate) must be taken FIRST THING in the morning with a FULL glass of plain water. Patient must remain UPRIGHT (sitting or standing) for at least 30 minutes and NOT eat or drink anything else. This prevents esophageal irritation/ulceration. Calcium supplements are taken at a DIFFERENT time of day.

8. Gout

Uric Acid Crystal Deposition

Acute Attack: Sudden severe pain, redness, swelling — typically first metatarsophalangeal joint (podagra), often at night. Tophi (chronic deposits) in ear, elbow, fingers.
Acute Treatment: NSAIDs (indomethacin), colchicine, corticosteroids
Chronic Prevention: Allopurinol (xanthine oxidase inhibitor), febuxostat, probenecid (uricosuric)
Diet: Limit organ meats, shellfish, red meat, alcohol (especially beer), high-fructose corn syrup
NCLEX Trap: Do NOT start allopurinol during an acute gout attack — it can worsen the attack. Treat the acute flare FIRST, then start allopurinol once resolved. Patients on allopurinol should continue it during acute attacks (don't stop).

NGN Case Study: Total Hip Replacement Post-Op

Client Scenario

Mrs. Rodriguez, 72-year-old female, is post-op day 1 after a right total hip arthroplasty (THA) for osteoarthritis. She has a history of hypertension and takes lisinopril. Her surgery was uncomplicated, and she received spinal anesthesia with sedation.

Vital Signs Physical Assessment Orders

Vital Signs: BP 138/82, HR 88, RR 18, Temp 37.4°C, SpO₂ 96% on 2L NC

Physical Assessment: Alert and oriented. Right hip incision clean, dry, intact with a mild hematoma. Drains: 80 mL serosanguineous in past 8 hours. Right lower extremity: toes warm, capillary refill <2 seconds, sensation intact. Able to wiggle toes. Denies chest pain or shortness of breath.

Orders: Weight-bearing as tolerated (WBAT) on right leg. PT consult for ambulation. DVT prophylaxis: enoxaparin 40mg SQ daily. PCA morphine for pain. TED hose and SCDs.

Question 1 — Recognize Cues: The nurse is assessing for DVT. Which finding is the earliest indicator of a developing DVT?

  • A. Calf swelling and warmth
  • B. Sudden onset chest pain and dyspnea
  • C. Unilateral calf pain with dorsiflexion (Homans' sign)
  • D. Low-grade fever and tachycardia

Correct: A. Calf swelling and warmth

Unilateral calf swelling, warmth, and erythema are the earliest clinical signs of DVT. Homans' sign (C) is unreliable and no longer recommended — it can dislodge a clot. Chest pain and dyspnea (B) indicate pulmonary embolism (PE), a complication of DVT, not DVT itself. Fever (D) is non-specific. Post-op patients should have calf circumference measured and compared bilaterally daily.

Question 2 — Analyze Cues: The physical therapist arrives for the first ambulation. Which instruction is most important to prevent hip dislocation with a posterior approach THA?

  • A. "Do not flex your hip more than 90 degrees"
  • B. "Keep your legs crossed to maintain hip alignment"
  • C. "Sleep on your operative side for the first week"
  • D. "Use a high toilet seat and avoid low chairs"

Correct: A. "Do not flex your hip more than 90 degrees"

For posterior approach THA, the hip precautions are: NO hip flexion >90°, NO adduction past midline (don't cross legs), NO internal rotation. These positions place stress on the posterior capsule and can cause dislocation. A high toilet seat (D) helps with the 90° rule but is less fundamental than the flexion restriction itself. Crossing legs (B) is contraindicated. Sleeping on the operative side (C) depends on surgeon preference but is not a universal precaution.

Question 3 — Prioritize Hypotheses: Mrs. Rodriguez reports her pain is 8/10. The PCA shows she has pressed the button 6 times in the past hour but received only 2 doses. What is the nurse's priority action?

  • A. Increase the PCA basal rate
  • B. Assess for factors contributing to inadequate pain control
  • C. Give a PRN bolus of morphine 4mg IV push
  • D. Switch to oral oxycodone

Correct: B. Assess for factors contributing to inadequate pain control

The patient is pressing the button frequently but receiving few doses — this suggests the lockout interval may be too long, or she's not understanding how to use the PCA. Before changing the dose, assess: Is she pressing the button correctly? Is she waiting long enough between presses? Is the catheter patent? Is she experiencing breakthrough pain from positioning? Once the cause is identified, collaborate with the provider for appropriate adjustments. Don't independently increase the basal rate (A) — that's a provider order.

Question 4 — Generate Solutions: On post-op day 2, Mrs. Rodriguez's Hgb drops from 12.2 to 8.5. She is asymptomatic, HR 92, BP 128/78. What is the most appropriate nursing action?

  • A. Prepare for immediate blood transfusion
  • B. Continue monitoring — post-op Hgb drop is expected
  • C. Notify the provider and monitor for signs of active bleeding
  • D. Increase IV fluids to hemodilute and improve circulation

Correct: C. Notify the provider and monitor for signs of active bleeding

A drop from 12.2 to 8.5 is significant (3.7 g/dL) and requires notification even if asymptomatic. Post-op patients can have hemodilution from IV fluids, but a drop this large warrants evaluation for active bleeding (check incision, drain output, stool guaiac, vital signs). Transfusion (A) may be needed but is a provider decision. Simply monitoring (B) is unsafe. Increasing fluids (D) further dilutes Hgb and doesn't address the cause.

Question 5 — Take Action: Mrs. Rodriguez is being discharged home. Which home safety instruction is highest priority?

  • A. "Remove throw rugs and install grab bars in the bathroom"
  • B. "Keep your TED hose on at all times except when bathing"
  • C. "Take your pain medication exactly as prescribed"
  • D. "Call your surgeon if you develop a fever over 101°F"

Correct: A. "Remove throw rugs and install grab bars in the bathroom"

Falls are the leading cause of post-THA complications (dislocation, periprosthetic fracture). Removing fall hazards (throw rugs, clutter), installing grab bars, and ensuring adequate lighting are the highest priority home modifications. While DVT prophylaxis (B), pain management (C), and infection monitoring (D) are all important, fall prevention takes priority because a fall can cause catastrophic failure of the prosthesis requiring revision surgery.

Question 6 — Evaluate Outcomes: At 6-week follow-up, which finding indicates a complication requiring immediate provider notification?

  • A. Patient is walking with a cane and has mild hip stiffness in the morning
  • B. Incision is well-healed but there is a small area of numbness near the scar
  • C. Patient reports sudden "giving way" sensation and inability to bear weight
  • D. Patient has gained 3 pounds since discharge

Correct: C. Patient reports sudden "giving way" sensation and inability to bear weight

A "giving way" sensation with inability to bear weight suggests prosthesis loosening, dislocation, or periprosthetic fracture — all emergencies requiring immediate evaluation. Morning stiffness (A) is normal during recovery. Numbness near incision (B) is common due to nerve disruption during surgery. Mild weight gain (D) is not concerning. Any sudden change in function, instability, or inability to bear weight after THA is a red flag.
