Neurological Deep Dive

Multiple Sclerosis, Parkinson's Disease, ALS & Myasthenia Gravis — pathophysiology, nursing priorities, and pharmacology for the NCLEX.

🧠 Multiple Sclerosis (MS)

Pathophysiology

Autoimmune demyelination of CNS neurons (brain, spinal cord, optic nerves). Disrupts nerve impulse transmission. Relapsing-remitting is most common type. More common in women, ages 20–40, northern latitudes.

Clinical Manifestations

  • Visual: Optic neuritis (painful vision loss, diplopia, nystagmus)
  • Motor: Weakness, spasticity, paralysis, foot drop
  • Sensory: Paresthesia, numbness, Lhermitte sign (electric shock sensation down spine with neck flexion)
  • Cerebellar: Ataxia, intention tremor, dysmetria
  • Bladder/Bowel: Urinary urgency, frequency, retention; constipation
  • Fatigue: Most common symptom — worsens with heat (Uhthoff phenomenon)
  • Cognitive: Memory problems, slowed processing
Mnemonic: "My Symptoms" — Motor, Sensory, Visual, Sphincter, Cognitive, Fatigue, Heat sensitivity

MS Medications

Drug ClassExamplesKey Nursing Points
Disease-modifying therapies (DMTs)Interferon beta (Avonex, Betaseron), glatiramer acetate, fingolimod, ocrelizumabReduce relapse frequency. Monitor LFTs, CBC. Fingolimod: bradycardia risk — first-dose monitoring 6 hours.
CorticosteroidsIV methylprednisoloneAcute relapse treatment. Watch for hyperglycemia, mood changes, GI bleeding.
SymptomaticBaclofen (spasticity), oxybutynin (bladder), amantadine (fatigue), dalfampridine (walking speed)Titrate slowly. Monitor for sedation with baclofen.
Nursing Priority: Avoid heat exposure (hot baths, saunas, vigorous exercise in heat) — worsens symptoms via Uhthoff phenomenon. Encourage cooling strategies. Fall risk assessment. Bladder training programs.
NCLEX Trap: MS is a CNS disease — cranial nerves CAN be affected (optic neuritis). Do NOT confuse with peripheral neuropathy. Also, MS does NOT cause lower motor neuron signs (fasciculations, flaccidity) — those point to ALS or peripheral nerve disorders.

🧠 Parkinson's Disease

Pathophysiology

Degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in substantia nigra → dopamine deficiency. Dopamine normally inhibits acetylcholine; with less dopamine, there's relative excess acetylcholine. Onset typically >60 years.

Cardinal Features (TRAP)

  • Tremor at rest — pill-rolling, starts in one hand
  • Rigidity — cogwheel or lead-pipe, increased resistance to movement
  • Akinesia/Bradykinesia — slow movement, difficulty initiating movement
  • Postural Instability — late finding (not early!)

Other features: Mask-like facies, micrographia, shuffling gait, festination, dysphagia, orthostatic hypotension, depression, dementia (late), seborrhea, constipation

Mnemonic: "TRAP" — Tremor, Rigidity, Akinesia, Postural instability. Remember: postural instability is a LATE finding, not for diagnosis.

Parkinson's Medications

Drug ClassExamplesKey Nursing Points
Levodopa/Carbidopa (Sinemet)Gold standard therapyCarbidopa prevents peripheral conversion of levodopa → more reaches brain. On-off phenomenon and dyskinesias with long-term use. Give with food to reduce nausea but high-protein meals compete for absorption.
Dopamine agonistsPramipexole, ropinirole, rotigotine patchCan cause impulse control disorders (gambling, hypersexuality), orthostatic hypotension, somnolence. Rotigotine patch: rotate sites, avoid heat.
MAO-B inhibitorsSelegiline, rasagilineDelays need for levodopa. Avoid tyramine-rich foods. Risk of serotonin syndrome if combined with SSRIs.
COMT inhibitorsEntacaponeExtends levodopa effect. Monitor for diarrhea, urine discoloration (harmless).
AnticholinergicsBenztropine, trihexyphenidylHelp tremor more than rigidity. Contraindicated in older adults (delirium, urinary retention, glaucoma).
AmantadineSymmetrelMild benefit, reduces dyskinesia. Caution: livedo reticularis, edema, confusion.
Nursing Priority: FALL PREVENTION — shuffling gait, orthostatic hypotension, rigidity all increase fall risk. Swallowing evaluation (silent aspiration risk). Constipation management (high fiber, fluids, exercise). Encourage BIG movements (LSVT BIG therapy). Depression screening.
NCLEX Trap: Do NOT give anticholinergics to elderly Parkinson's patients — high risk of delirium, urinary retention, and narrow-angle glaucoma. Also, levodopa is most effective for bradykinesia/rigidity, LESS effective for tremor.

🧠 Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS / Lou Gehrig's Disease)

Pathophysiology

Degeneration of BOTH upper motor neurons (UMN) AND lower motor neurons (LMN) in cortex, brainstem, and spinal cord. Pure motor disease — NO sensory involvement. Fatal, median survival 3–5 years.

Clinical Manifestations

UMN signs: Spasticity, hyperreflexia, Babinski sign, pseudobulbar affect (uncontrollable laughing/crying)

LMN signs: Muscle weakness, atrophy, fasciculations, hyporeflexia, flaccidity

Bulbar onset: Dysarthria, dysphagia, tongue atrophy/fasciculations

Limb onset: Foot drop, hand weakness, cramping

Respiratory: Progressive respiratory muscle weakness → respiratory failure (usual cause of death)

Sensory, bowel, bladder, cognition: SPARED (unlike MS)

ALS Management

  • Riluzole: Only disease-modifying drug — prolongs survival by ~3 months. Monitor LFTs. Take on empty stomach.
  • Edaravone: Slows functional decline. IV infusion daily for 14 days, then 14 days off.
  • Supportive care: PEG feeding tube (before FVC <50%), BiPAP/ventilatory support, communication devices, multidisciplinary care
Nursing Priority: Respiratory monitoring is CRITICAL — FVC, MIP, SNIP. Discuss advance directives EARLY. Communication support as speech deteriorates. Nutrition support before significant weight loss. Psychosocial support for patient and caregivers.
NCLEX Trap: ALS spares sensation, bowel/bladder, and cognition (mostly). If a question mentions sensory loss or urinary incontinence, it's NOT ALS. Also, ALS is NOT MS — MS is demyelinating, relapsing-remitting; ALS is motor neuron degeneration, progressive.

🧠 Myasthenia Gravis (MG)

Pathophysiology

Autoimmune destruction of acetylcholine receptors (AChR) at neuromuscular junction. Antibodies block, degrade, or complement-lyse AChR → impaired neuromuscular transmission. Associated with thymoma (15%) and thymic hyperplasia (85%). Bimodal: women 20–30, men 60–80.

Clinical Manifestations — "The 3 D's of MG"

  • Diplopia (double vision)
  • Dysphagia (difficulty swallowing)
  • Dysarthria (slurred speech)

Other: Ptosis (worsens with sustained upgaze), proximal muscle weakness, fatigue with repeated use (NOT present on waking), respiratory muscle weakness (myasthenic crisis)

Key feature: Muscle weakness WORSENS with activity and IMPROVES with rest

Mnemonic: "My Gravis is grave at the end of the day" — Symptoms worsen with activity and improve with rest. Also: "MG = Myasthenia = Muscle gets tired"

MG Medications

DrugMechanism/ActionNursing Priority
Pyridostigmine (Mestinon)Acetylcholinesterase inhibitor — increases ACh at NMJGive on time (every 3–4 hours). Watch for cholinergic crisis (SLUDGE: salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation, GI distress, emesis). Best taken 30–60 min before meals to help swallowing.
PrednisoneImmunosuppressionMonitor for hyperglycemia, infection, mood changes. Taper slowly.
Azathioprine, mycophenolateSteroid-sparing immunosuppressantsMonitor CBC, LFTs. TPMT testing before azathioprine.
IVIG, plasma exchangeAcute severe exacerbations, pre-surgeryIVIG: monitor for aseptic meningitis, renal dysfunction. PLEX: monitor for hypocalcemia, hypotension.
Eculizumab, efgartigimodComplement inhibitors, FcRn antagonistsEculizumab: meningococcal vaccination required. Efgartigimod: monitor infection risk.
Nursing Priority: Monitor respiratory function (vital capacity, negative inspiratory force) — myasthenic crisis is LIFE-THREATENING. Medications on EXACT schedule. Avoid MG-exacerbating drugs: aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, beta-blockers, magnesium, certain anesthesia agents. Thymectomy if thymoma present.
NCLEX Trap: Distinguish myasthenic crisis (too little medication → weakness) from cholinergic crisis (too much medication → SLUDGE, bradycardia, miosis). Tensilon (edrophonium) test: myasthenic crisis improves; cholinergic crisis worsens. In practice, both are treated with ventilatory support and ATROPINE for cholinergic crisis.
