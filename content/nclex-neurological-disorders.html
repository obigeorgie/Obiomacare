NCLEX Neurological Disorders: Complete Study Guide

Stroke, seizures, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's, increased ICP, and meningitis — pathophysiology, nursing priorities, and NCLEX traps.

1. Stroke (Cerebrovascular Accident)

📋 Ischemic vs. Hemorrhagic Stroke

FeatureIschemic (85%)Hemorrhagic (15%)
CauseThrombosis or embolismHTN, aneurysm, AVM, anticoagulants
OnsetGradual or stepwiseSudden, "thunderclap" headache
HeadacheUsually absentSevere ("worst headache of life")
ImagingNon-contrast CT: normal early, then hypodenseCT: hyperdense (white)
TreatmenttPA (within 3-4.5h), thrombectomy, antiplateletsBP control, reverse anticoagulation, surgery
PositioningHOB 30°HOB 30-45° (reduce ICP)
💡 BE FAST — Stroke Recognition:
Balance loss | Eye changes (vision) | Face drooping | Arm weakness | Speech difficulty | Time to call 911
✅ Acute Stroke Nursing Priorities:
• Rapid assessment (NIHSS), last known well time
• CT scan STAT (rule out hemorrhage before tPA)
• Maintain BP <185/110 before tPA, <180/105 after
• NO anticoagulants, NO aspirin until hemorrhage ruled out
• NPO until swallow screening passed
• Seizure precautions, frequent neuro checks
🚨 NCLEX Trap: After tPA, the BIGGEST risk is bleeding. Monitor for: severe headache, acute HTN, N/V, neuro decline. Also: tPA is CONTRAINDICICATED if blood glucose <50 or >400, recent surgery/trauma, active bleeding, or INR >1.7.

2. Seizures

📋 Types of Seizures

TypeCharacteristicsKey Nursing Actions
Tonic-clonic (grand mal)Loss of consciousness, tonic rigidity, clonic jerking, post-ictal confusionProtect from injury, time seizure, airway, oxygen, recovery position
Absence (petit mal)Brief staring, no post-ictal confusion, common in childrenSafety, education, medication adherence
Partial (focal)Localized symptoms based on brain area; may progress to generalizedObserve and document aura, protect, monitor
Status epilepticusSeizure >5 min OR recurrent without recovery — MEDICAL EMERGENCYAirway, lorazepam IV, fosphenytoin, intubation if needed
✅ Seizure Nursing Priorities:
• Protect from injury (pad bed rails, remove dangerous objects)
• DO NOT restrain or put anything in mouth
• Turn to side (recovery position), loosen tight clothing
• Time the seizure
• O₂ after seizure, suction if needed
• Document: type, duration, body parts involved, incontinence, post-ictal state
🚨 NCLEX Trap: Status epilepticus = seizure >5 minutes. Give lorazepam IV first line. If no IV access, midazolam IM or rectal diazepam. NEVER leave a seizing patient unattended. Document aura (warning sign) — helps localize seizure focus.

3. Multiple Sclerosis (MS)

📋 Multiple Sclerosis

Patho: Autoimmune demyelination of CNS → plaques/scars in brain and spinal cord → varied neurological deficits depending on location.

Common Symptoms: Fatigue, weakness, numbness/tingling, optic neuritis (vision loss), diplopia, ataxia, spasticity, bladder/bowel dysfunction, cognitive changes
✅ Nursing Priorities:
• Energy conservation strategies (frequent rest periods)
• Cool environment (heat worsens symptoms — Uhthoff's phenomenon)
• Bladder management (intermittent catheterization if needed)
• Fall prevention (ataxia, weakness)
• Medications: interferon-beta, glatiramer acetate, natalizumab, ocrelizumab, steroids for flares
• Emotional support (unpredictable course, young adults)
🚨 NCLEX Trap: MS is characterized by RELAPSING-REMITTING course. Symptoms worsen with heat (hot bath, fever, exercise). Diagnosis: MRI shows demyelinating plaques. LP shows oligoclonal bands.

4. Parkinson's Disease

📋 Parkinson's Disease

Patho: Degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in substantia nigra → dopamine deficiency → imbalance between dopamine and acetylcholine.

💡 TRAP Symptoms:
Tremor at rest (pill-rolling) | Rigidity (cogwheel/lead pipe) | Akinesia/bradykinesia | Postural instability (late sign)
✅ Nursing Priorities:
• Fall prevention (shuffling gait, postural instability)
• Assist with ADLs while promoting independence
• High-fiber diet, fluids for constipation
• Swallowing precautions (risk of aspiration)
• Medication timing CRITICAL (levodopa/carbidopa — "on-off" phenomenon)
• Exercise and PT for mobility
🚨 NCLEX Trap: Levodopa should be given WITH meals if nausea occurs, but protein competes for absorption — give 30 min before or 1 hour after meals. "On-off" phenomenon = unpredictable fluctuations between mobility ("on") and immobility ("off"). NEVER stop levodopa abruptly.

5. Increased Intracranial Pressure (ICP)

📋 Signs of Increased ICP

Early Signs: Headache, nausea, altered mental status, pupillary changes (sluggish), restlessness
Late Signs (Cushing's Triad): Bradycardia, irregular respirations, widening pulse pressure (hypertension with increased systolic BP)
💡 Cushing's Triad — "BIR"
Bradycardia | Irregular respirations | Rising systolic BP (widening pulse pressure)
✅ ICP Nursing Priorities:
• HOB 30-45° (promotes venous drainage)
• Keep head midline, avoid neck flexion/rotation
• Avoid Valsalva maneuvers (straining, coughing)
• Prevent hypercapnia (maintain PaCO₂ 35-45)
• Mannitol or hypertonic saline for cerebral edema
• NO Trendelenburg position
• Monitor neuro checks Q1-2h
🚨 NCLEX Trap: In increased ICP, NEVER give morphine if it obscures neuro assessment. Use non-opioid pain relief when possible. Lumbar puncture is CONTRAINDICATED with signs of increased ICP — risk of herniation! Mannitol causes osmotic diuresis — monitor I&O and electrolytes closely.

6. Meningitis

📋 Bacterial vs. Viral Meningitis

FeatureBacterialViral
OnsetSuddenGradual
FeverHighLow-grade
Neck stiffnessSevereMild to moderate
Altered mental statusCommonRare
CSF glucoseLowNormal
CSF proteinHighNormal to mildly elevated
TreatmentEMPIRIC antibiotics STAT (ceftriaxone + vancomycin)Supportive
✅ Meningitis Nursing Priorities:
• DROPLET precautions (bacterial/viral) until cause known
• Antibiotics within 30 minutes of suspected bacterial meningitis
• Dark, quiet room (photophobia, headache)
• Seizure precautions
• Monitor for increased ICP, septic shock
• Contacts may need prophylaxis (rifampin, ciprofloxacin, ceftriaxone)
🚨 NCLEX Trap: Kernig's sign = pain with knee extension when hip flexed. Brudzinski's sign = hip/knee flexion with neck flexion. These indicate meningeal irritation. In infants, look for bulging fontanelle, high-pitched cry, poor feeding, irritability.

🧩 NGN Case Study: Head Trauma & Increased ICP

Client Scenario

Mr. Garcia, 28-year-old male, was brought to the ED after a motorcycle crash. He was not wearing a helmet. Witnesses report he was thrown approximately 20 feet. He was unconscious at the scene and remains unresponsive.

Vital Signs Physical Assessment CT Head

Vital Signs: BP 178/92, HR 52, RR 10 (irregular), Temp 37.8°C, SpO₂ 89% on 15L NRB

Physical Assessment: GCS 7 (E1, V2, M4). Right pupil 6mm, sluggish; left pupil 3mm, reactive. Decorticate posturing to painful stimuli. Battle's sign and raccoon eyes present. CSF draining from left ear. No other obvious injuries.

CT Head: Right temporal-parietal epidural hematoma with 8mm midline shift

Question 1 — Recognize Cues: Which vital sign pattern indicates Cushing's triad?

  • A. BP 178/92, HR 52, RR 10
  • B. BP 88/50, HR 128, RR 28
  • C. BP 120/80, HR 72, RR 16
  • D. BP 150/90, HR 110, RR 24

Correct: A. BP 178/92, HR 52, RR 10

Cushing's triad = hypertension (systolic) + bradycardia + irregular respirations. It's a late sign of severely increased intracranial pressure (ICP). The widened pulse pressure (178-92 = 86) with bradycardia indicates the brainstem is attempting to maintain perfusion. This requires IMMEDIATE intervention — mannitol, hyperventilation, and emergent neurosurgical decompression. The irregular respirations suggest brainstem compression.

Question 2 — Analyze Cues: The right pupil is 6mm and sluggish while the left is 3mm and reactive. What does this indicate?

  • A. Direct traumatic injury to the right eye
  • B. Third cranial nerve (oculomotor) compression on the right
  • C. Optic nerve transection bilaterally
  • D. Normal variation in pupil size

Correct: B. Third cranial nerve (oculomotor) compression on the right

Anisocoria (unequal pupils) with a dilated, sluggish pupil on the side of the hematoma indicates CN III (oculomotor nerve) compression from uncal herniation. The temporal lobe uncus herniates through the tentorial incisura, compressing CN III. This is a neurosurgical emergency. The "blown pupil" is on the same side as the mass lesion (ipsilateral) — a classic finding that localizes the lesion to the right hemisphere.

Question 3 — Prioritize Hypotheses: The neurosurgeon is 30 minutes away. Which intervention should the nurse implement first?

  • A. Mannitol 1g/kg IV bolus
  • B. Hyperventilate to PaCO₂ 25-30 mmHg
  • C. Elevate head of bed to 30 degrees
  • D. Insert an arterial line for continuous BP monitoring

Correct: C. Elevate head of bed to 30 degrees

While all options are appropriate for increased ICP, elevating the HOB to 30° is the FASTEST intervention that promotes venous drainage and reduces ICP. It takes seconds and has no contraindications. Mannitol requires IV access and monitoring. Hyperventilation is a temporary bridge but causes cerebral vasoconstriction that can worsen ischemia — it's not first-line. Arterial line insertion takes time and is not the immediate priority.

Question 4 — Generate Solutions: Mannitol is ordered. Which nursing consideration is highest priority?

  • A. Monitor for rebound increased ICP when mannitol wears off
  • B. Ensure an indwelling urinary catheter is in place
  • C. Administer through a filter needle because it crystallizes
  • D. Monitor serum osmolality and discontinue if >320 mOsm/kg

Correct: D. Monitor serum osmolality and discontinue if >320 mOsm/kg

Mannitol is an osmotic diuretic that draws fluid from brain tissue into the vasculature. However, it can cause renal failure and electrolyte imbalances. The most critical safety parameter is serum osmolality — mannitol should be held if osmolality exceeds 320 mOsm/kg (some protocols say 340). While rebound ICP (A), catheter placement (B), and crystallization (C) are all valid concerns, osmolality monitoring is the highest priority because it prevents acute kidney injury and cardiovascular collapse from hyperosmolar states.

Question 5 — Take Action: The patient returns from craniotomy for evacuation of the epidural hematoma. He is intubated and sedated. His BP is 190/100. What is the nurse's priority response?

  • A. Allow hypertension — it's protective for cerebral perfusion
  • B. Give labetalol 10mg IV to prevent bleeding at surgical site
  • C. Check for pain, bladder distension, or other causes first
  • D. Increase sedation to lower BP

Correct: C. Check for pain, bladder distension, or other causes first

Post-craniotomy, hypertension can cause bleeding at the surgical site. However, before giving antihypertensives, assess for reversible causes of hypertension: pain (check sedation adequacy), bladder distension, hypoxia, or increased ICP. Treating the cause is better than treating the number. That said, if SBP >160-180 persists after addressing causes, antihypertensives may be needed. Cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP = MAP - ICP) must be maintained at 60-70 mmHg, so overly aggressive BP lowering is harmful.

Question 6 — Evaluate Outcomes: On post-op day 2, the patient is extubated and following commands. His GCS is 13. Which finding would indicate a complication requiring immediate notification of the neurosurgeon?

  • A. Patient asks repeated questions about the accident
  • B. Temperature 38.2°C with clear lung sounds
  • C. New onset right-sided weakness
  • D. Patient refuses to eat breakfast

Correct: C. New onset right-sided weakness

New focal neurological deficit (right-sided weakness) after craniotomy suggests cerebral edema, rebleeding, stroke, or seizure. This requires immediate neurosurgical evaluation — possibly urgent CT head. Post-traumatic amnesia (A) is expected. Low-grade fever (B) is common post-op. Poor appetite (D) is not emergent. Any change in neuro status is the most important finding to report after brain surgery.
