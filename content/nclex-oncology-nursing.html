NCLEX Oncology Nursing

Cancer staging, chemotherapy side effects, radiation therapy, oncologic emergencies, and supportive care for NCLEX success.

1. Cancer Basics

📋 TNM Staging

ComponentDefinition
T (Tumor)Size and extent of primary tumor (T0-T4, Tis = in situ)
N (Nodes)Regional lymph node involvement (N0-N3)
M (Metastasis)Presence of distant metastasis (M0 or M1)
💡 Stage Grouping:
Stage 0: Carcinoma in situ
Stage I: Localized, small tumor
Stage II: Larger tumor or limited spread
Stage III: Extensive local spread, lymph nodes
Stage IV: Distant metastasis

📋 Grading

Differentiation: How much cancer cells resemble normal cells

GradeDescription
G1 (Well-differentiated)Cells look similar to normal; slower growing
G2 (Moderately differentiated)Intermediate
G3 (Poorly differentiated)Cells look very abnormal; faster growing
G4 (Undifferentiated)Cells look nothing like normal; very aggressive
🚨 NCLEX Trap: Higher grade = MORE aggressive, MORE metastatic potential, WORSE prognosis. Do NOT confuse grade with stage. A Stage I G3 cancer may be more aggressive than a Stage II G1 cancer.

2. Chemotherapy

📋 Major Drug Classes and Side Effects

ClassExamplesSide Effects
Alkylating agentsCyclophosphamide, cisplatinMyelosuppression, hemorrhagic cystitis (mesna protective), nephrotoxicity, nausea
AntimetabolitesMethotrexate, 5-FUMucositis, myelosuppression, hand-foot syndrome (5-FU)
Antitumor antibioticsDoxorubicin, bleomycinCardiotoxicity (doxorubicin — max lifetime dose), pulmonary fibrosis (bleomycin)
Plant alkaloidsVincristine, paclitaxelPeripheral neuropathy, myelosuppression
Topoisomerase inhibitorsEtoposide, irinotecanMyelosuppression, diarrhea (irinotecan)
✅ Chemotherapy Nursing Priorities:
• Verify orders and protocols (double-check dosing — often weight/BSA-based)
• Pre-medications: antiemetics, steroids, diphenhydramine
• Ensure adequate hydration before and after nephrotoxic agents
• Monitor for extravasation (tissue necrosis if vesicant leaks)
• Protect yourself: chemotherapy-safe PPE, closed-system transfer devices
• Patient education: infection precautions, mouth care, hydration, when to call MD

📋 Extravasation Management

Vesicants: Doxorubicin, vincristine, paclitaxel, cisplatin
Signs: Pain, burning, swelling, redness, lack of blood return, blistering
✅ If Extravasation Suspected:
1. STOP infusion immediately
2. Do NOT flush the line
3. Attempt to aspirate residual drug
4. Remove IV catheter
5. Notify provider and pharmacy
6. Apply warm or cold compress per protocol (varies by drug)
7. Elevate extremity
8. Document thoroughly with photos
9. Antidotes: hyaluronidase, dexrazoxane (for doxorubicin)
🚨 NCLEX Trap: If a patient receiving vesicant chemotherapy reports pain at IV site, STOP the infusion FIRST. Do NOT complete the dose or try to "push through" — extravasation causes severe tissue necrosis.

3. Radiation Therapy

📋 Side Effects by Site

SiteSide EffectsNursing Care
Head/NeckMucositis, xerostomia (dry mouth), altered taste, skin changesSoft diet, mouth care, artificial saliva, avoid spicy/acidic foods
ChestEsophagitis, cough, pneumonitis, skin changesSoft diet, cough suppressants, monitor for respiratory distress
Abdomen/PelvisNausea, diarrhea, cystitis, proctitisAntiemetics, antidiarrheals, hydration, bladder analgesics
Skin (any site)Erythema, dry desquamation, moist desquamationGentle cleansing, moisturize (after treatment), avoid sun, no tight clothing
✅ Radiation Safety:
• External beam: NO radioactivity after treatment (safe to be around)
• Brachytherapy (internal): MAY be radioactive — follow precautions (distance, time, shielding)
• Permanent implants (prostate seeds): Low-dose, brief precautions
• Pregnant women and children should limit close contact with brachytherapy patients

4. Oncologic Emergencies

📋 Spinal Cord Compression

Signs: Back pain (worse with movement/cough), weakness, sensory changes, bowel/bladder dysfunction, paralysis below level of compression
✅ Nursing Priorities:
• EMERGENCY — time is critical (neurological deficit can become permanent within hours)
• Dexamethasone STAT (reduces edema)
• MRI to confirm
• Radiation therapy or surgical decompression
• Log-roll for positioning
• Monitor neurological status

📋 Superior Vena Cava (SVC) Syndrome

Signs: Facial/neck swelling, distended neck veins, headache, dyspnea, cough, visual changes (worse when bending forward or lying down)
✅ Nursing Priorities:
• Elevate head of bed (gravity helps drainage)
• Oxygen
• High-dose corticosteroids
• Radiation therapy or stent placement
• Avoid upper extremity IVs and BP measurements
• Medical emergency but rarely immediately fatal

📋 Tumor Lysis Syndrome (TLS)

Patho: Rapid tumor cell lysis → release of intracellular contents → hyperuricemia, hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia
Risk: High-burden, chemosensitive tumors (ALL, Burkitt lymphoma, bulky NHL)
✅ Nursing Priorities:
• Aggressive IV hydration (2-3 L/m²/day)
• Allopurinol or rasburicase (prevents uric acid formation)
• Monitor electrolytes q6-12h
• Treat hyperkalemia (calcium gluconate, insulin/glucose, kayexalate)
• Alkalinization of urine (may help uric acid excretion)
• Dialysis if refractory
🚨 NCLEX Trap: TLS is a CHEMOTHERAPY-INDUCED emergency, NOT a disease progression. It occurs 12-72 hours after starting chemotherapy for sensitive tumors. Know the metabolic triad: hyperkalemia + hyperuricemia + hyperphosphatemia → hypocalcemia.

5. Neutropenia

📋 ANC <1500 (Severe if <500)

✅ Neutropenic Precautions:
• Private room with positive pressure if available
• HEPA filtration
• No fresh flowers, plants, or standing water
• Low-microbial diet (no raw fruits/vegetables, undercooked meats)
• Strict hand hygiene for all
• Limit visitors (no sick visitors)
• Daily baths, oral care, perineal care
• Monitor temp q4h (fever = emergency in neutropenia)
• Growth factors (G-CSF/filgrastim) to stimulate WBC production
🚨 NCLEX Trap: A neutropenic patient with fever (even low-grade) is a MEDICAL EMERGENCY. Start empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics within 1 hour (e.g., cefepime + vancomycin). Do NOT wait for cultures. A neutropenic patient may not mount a normal inflammatory response — absence of pus doesn't mean absence of infection.
