Orthopedic Surgery & Joint Replacement

Total joint arthroplasty, spinal surgery, fracture repair, and comprehensive postoperative orthopedic nursing care.

🦴 Total Hip Arthroplasty (THA)

Surgical Approaches & Hip Precautions

Posterior approach (most common):

  • Do NOT flex hip >90°
  • Do NOT adduct hip past midline
  • Do NOT internally rotate hip

Anterior approach: Fewer restrictions — avoid excessive external rotation and extension

Purpose: Prevent hip dislocation (prosthetic femoral head dislocates from acetabular cup)

Mnemonic: "Do NOT bend, cross, or twist" — Posterior approach precautions: No flexion >90°, No adduction past midline, No internal rotation
Dislocation Signs: Sudden severe hip pain, leg appears shortened and externally rotated, inability to move hip. Keep patient NPO and notify surgeon immediately — requires closed reduction or open reduction.

THA Postoperative Care

  • Positioning: Abduction pillow between legs when supine. Keep operative leg slightly abducted
  • Transfer: Logroll to unoperative side. Raise head of bed before turning. Use elevated toilet seat (prevents flexion >90°)
  • Weight-bearing: Per surgeon orders (often weight-bearing as tolerated with cemented components; toe-touch with uncemented)
  • DVT prophylaxis: Sequential compression devices, early ambulation, pharmacologic prophylaxis (heparin, LMWH, DOAC, aspirin)

🦴 Total Knee Arthroplasty (TKA)

Postoperative Priorities

  • Continuous passive motion (CPM): Start early (day 0–1) to prevent stiffness. Gradually increase ROM
  • Goal ROM: 0° extension, 90°+ flexion by discharge
  • Ice/cryotherapy: Reduces swelling and pain
  • Quadriceps strengthening: Straight leg raises, ankle pumps
  • Walker → cane → unassisted: Progressive weight-bearing
NCLEX Trap: Knee replacement patients should NOT use pillows under the knee (promotes flexion contracture). Instead, keep leg extended with ankle support. Also, CPM is used for knees, NOT hips.

🦴 Spinal Surgery

Post-Laminectomy/Spinal Fusion Care

  • Positioning: Logroll (keep spine aligned) — NO twisting, flexing, or extending spine
  • Brace: TLSO (thoracolumbosacral orthosis) or cervical collar as ordered
  • Neurological checks: Motor, sensory, reflexes every 2–4 hours initially. Document baseline
  • Dural tear/CSF leak: Clear drainage from incision or nose (if cervical). Headache worse when upright. Keep flat, notify surgeon
Cauda Equina Syndrome — EMERGENCY: Saddle anesthesia, bowel/bladder dysfunction (urinary retention, incontinence), severe back pain with leg weakness. Requires emergent MRI and surgical decompression within 24–48 hours to prevent permanent paralysis.
NCLEX Trap: After spinal surgery, a patient with NEW onset urinary retention must be evaluated for cauda equina syndrome immediately — do NOT simply catheterize and wait. Also, patients with cervical spine surgery may have difficulty swallowing initially — advance diet slowly.

🦴 Hip Fractures

Types & Surgical Management

  • Femoral neck fractures: Intracapsular. Risk of avascular necrosis (disrupts retinacular blood supply). Treated with hemiarthroplasty or THA
  • Intertrochanteric fractures: Extracapsular. Good blood supply. Treated with sliding hip screw or intramedullary nail
  • Subtrochanteric fractures: Below lesser trochanter. Intramedullary nail fixation

Clinical Presentation

  • Shortened, externally rotated affected leg
  • Inability to bear weight
  • Hip pain, groin pain
  • Elderly patient after fall
Nursing Priority: Hip fracture surgery ideally within 24–48 hours to reduce mortality and complications. Preoperative: skin traction or Buck's traction for comfort. DVT prophylaxis. Pain management (regional anesthesia preferred — femoral nerve block). Postoperative: early mobilization (day of surgery or next day) is KEY to preventing complications.

🦴 Fat Embolism Syndrome (FES)

Pathophysiology & Presentation

Fat globules from marrow enter circulation → pulmonary, cerebral, and cutaneous manifestations. Usually occurs 24–72 hours after long bone fracture or intramedullary nailing.

Classic triad:

  • Respiratory: Dyspnea, tachypnea, hypoxemia, ARDS-like picture, petechial rash on chest/axilla
  • Neurological: Confusion, restlessness, seizures, coma
  • Cutaneous: Petechial rash (chest, neck, axilla, conjunctiva)
FES Nursing Priority: High-flow oxygen, intubation/ventilation if severe. IV fluids. Corticosteroids controversial. Prevention: early immobilization/fracture fixation. Monitor SpO2, mental status, respiratory rate closely.
NCLEX Trap: Fat embolism is different from DVT/PE. FES occurs 24–72 hours post-long bone fracture with the classic triad. DVT/PE can occur later and presents differently. Petechial rash is specific to FES.
