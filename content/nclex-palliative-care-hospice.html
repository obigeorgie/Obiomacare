Palliative Care & Hospice

Symptom management, end-of-life care, grief, ethical considerations, and nursing priorities for the NCLEX.

Palliative Care vs. Hospice

FeaturePalliative CareHospice
TimingAny stage of serious illness (can be curative)End of life (prognosis ≤6 months)
GoalImprove quality of life, relieve symptomsComfort, dignity, quality of remaining life
TreatmentAlongside curative treatmentCurative treatment discontinued
LocationHospital, clinic, homeHome, hospice facility, nursing home
TeamInterdisciplinaryInterdisciplinary + bereavement support
PrognosisNo prognosis requirementPhysician certifies ≤6 months if disease follows natural course
Mnemonic: "Palliative = Parallel, Hospice = Home to heaven" — Palliative care runs parallel with curative care. Hospice focuses on comfort as patient approaches end of life.

Symptom Management at End of Life

Pain Management

  • World Health Organization Analgesic Ladder:
    1. Non-opioids (acetaminophen, NSAIDs) ± adjuvants
    2. Weak opioids (tramadol, codeine) ± non-opioids
    3. Strong opioids (morphine, oxycodone, fentanyl, hydromorphone) ± non-opioids ± adjuvants
  • Morphine (gold standard): No ceiling dose. Titrate to effect. Common side effects: constipation (prophylactic laxatives), sedation (tolerance develops), nausea (usually resolves in 3–5 days), pruritus, urinary retention
  • Breakthrough pain: Give 10–20% of total daily opioid dose as rescue medication
  • Adjuvant analgesics: Gabapentin/pregabalin (neuropathic), steroids (bone pain, inflammation), antidepressants (chronic pain), bisphosphonates (bone metastases)
NCLEX Trap: Respiratory depression from opioids is rare in patients with chronic pain/tolerance. Do NOT withhold opioids from dying patients fearing respiratory depression — unrelieved pain is more harmful. Also, myoclonus and hyperalgesia at high opioid doses indicate opioid-induced neurotoxicity — rotate opioids rather than increase dose.

Dyspnea (Shortness of Breath)

  • Non-pharmacologic: Fan to face (stimulates trigeminal nerve), upright positioning, relaxation, anxiety reduction
  • Pharmacologic: Low-dose opioids (morphine 2.5–5 mg) reduce respiratory drive and perception of dyspnea. Oxygen if hypoxemic (may not help if not hypoxemic — fan often more effective)
  • Anxiolytics: Lorazepam for anxiety-related dyspnea
  • Secretions: Glycopyrrolate or atropine (dries secretions — "death rattle"), scopolamine patch

Other Common Symptoms

  • Constipation: Stimulant laxatives (senna) + osmotic (polyethylene glycol) + rectal interventions if needed. Prevent with opioids — start prophylactic laxatives same day as opioids
  • Anxiety/Agitation: Non-pharmacologic first (presence, reassurance, music). Lorazepam, midazolam if needed
  • Delirium: Treat underlying cause (infection, medications, dehydration). Haloperidol low dose. Avoid restraints. Provide familiar objects, clock, calendar
  • Fatigue: Energy conservation, treat anemia/depression, gentle exercise, methylphenidate in select cases
  • Cachexia: Anorexia at end of life is normal — do NOT force feed. Offer small amounts of preferred foods. Family education crucial

End-of-Life Care

Signs of Approaching Death (Hours to Days)

  • Decreased level of consciousness/somnolence
  • Decreased intake of food/fluids (normal, do NOT force)
  • Changes in breathing: Cheyne-Stokes respirations, apnea periods, shallow breathing
  • Mottling and coolness of extremities
  • Decreased urine output
  • Restlessness or terminal agitation
  • "Death rattle" — noisy breathing from pooled secretions (not distressing to patient, but distressing to family)
Nursing Priority at End of Life: Comfort measures only. Mouth care every 2 hours (toothbrush, moist swabs, lip balm — no lemon-glycerin swabs, they dry mucosa). Reposition every 2 hours. Gentle hygiene. Minimize interventions. Provide emotional support to patient and family. Allow natural death.

Grief & Bereavement

Types of Grief

  • Anticipatory grief: Grief before death occurs (patient and family grieve expected loss)
  • Complicated/prolonged grief: Persistent intense grief >6–12 months, interferes with functioning. Requires specialized support
  • Disenfranchised grief: Not socially recognized (extramarital partner, same-sex relationship in unsupportive environment, abortion, pet loss)

Kübler-Ross Stages of Grief

Not linear — people move back and forth:

  1. Denial: "Not me." Protective mechanism. Do NOT force acceptance.
  2. Anger: "Why me?" Direct anger at staff, family, God. Do NOT take personally. Allow expression.
  3. Bargaining: "If only..." Attempting to negotiate for more time.
  4. Depression: Sadness, withdrawal. Distinguish from clinical depression (may need treatment).
  5. Acceptance: Coming to terms. Peaceful, not necessarily happy.
NCLEX Trap: Do NOT tell patients/families they "should be" at a certain stage of grief. Grief is individual. Also, children grieve differently than adults — may show regression, behavioral changes, or delayed grief.

Ethical Considerations

Advance Directives

  • Living will: Document stating wishes for medical treatment if unable to communicate
  • Durable Power of Attorney for Healthcare (Healthcare Proxy): Designates surrogate decision-maker
  • Do Not Resuscitate (DNR)/Do Not Intubate (DNI): Specific orders about resuscitation. Does NOT mean "do not treat"
  • POLST/MOLST: Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment — portable medical orders
Ethical Principles: Autonomy (self-determination), Beneficence (do good), Non-maleficence (do no harm), Justice (fairness). When conflicts arise, patient autonomy generally takes precedence if patient has decision-making capacity.

Clinical Judgment Case Study

Palliative Care End-of-Life

Case: Terminal Sedation Decision

A 68-year-old with terminal lung cancer has uncontrolled pain despite maximum doses of morphine and adjuvant medications. He is awake but suffering. The family asks if "something more" can be done.

What is the nurse's best response?

  1. Explain that terminal sedation is euthanasia and cannot be offered
  2. Discuss palliative sedation with the physician and family
  3. Increase the morphine dose until the patient is unconscious
  4. Suggest the family consider hospice if not already enrolled
Answer: 2 — Discuss palliative sedation.

Palliative (terminal) sedation is ethically and legally distinct from euthanasia — the intent is to relieve refractory suffering, not to hasten death. It is appropriate when pain/suffering is unmanageable with standard measures. An ethics consultation may help. The doctrine of double effect applies: morphine may shorten life as an unintended side effect, but the primary intent is comfort. Hospice should already be involved but doesn't solve this immediate crisis.
