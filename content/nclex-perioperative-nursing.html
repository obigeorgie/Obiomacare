NCLEX Perioperative Nursing

Pre-operative assessment, informed consent, anesthesia, PACU care, and post-operative complications for NCLEX success.

1. Pre-Operative Phase

📋 Pre-Op Assessment

✅ Nursing Priorities:
• Verify surgical site and mark with surgeon (WHO checklist)
• Confirm NPO status (typically 8 hours for solids, 2-6 hours for clear liquids depending on institutional policy)
• Baseline vital signs
• Allergies (especially to latex, antibiotics, anesthesia)
• Current medications (hold anticoagulants, diabetes meds per orders)
• Remove jewelry, dentures, contact lenses, hearing aids
• Bowel prep (if ordered)
• Skin prep (shower with chlorhexidine — NOT shaving)
• IV access
🚨 NCLEX Trap: The surgical site MUST be marked by the SURGEON (not the nurse) while the patient is awake and able to confirm. Marking is done with the patient PARTICIPATING. "The nurse marks the site" is WRONG.

📋 Informed Consent

✅ Key Points:
• Consent must be obtained by the PROVIDER (surgeon or MD), NOT the nurse
• Nurse's role: WITNESS the signature, ensure patient is competent, confirm understanding
• Patient must understand: procedure, risks, benefits, alternatives, consequences of refusal
• Consent is VOLUNTARY — patient can withdraw at any time
• For minors/emancipated minors, unconscious, or incompetent patients: surrogate decision-maker
• Emergency exception: life-threatening situation, no surrogate available
🚨 NCLEX Trap: If a patient asks questions about the procedure during consent, the nurse should NOT answer — refer to the surgeon. The nurse's role is to witness, not to explain the procedure. Also, a nurse CANNOT obtain informed consent — this is outside nursing scope of practice.

🚨 NCLEX Trap: Beta-blockers are typically CONTINUED perioperatively (stopping can cause rebound tachycardia/HTN). Statins are also usually continued. Always check institutional protocol — but know that cardiac meds are generally NOT held.

4. Post-Operative Complications

📋 Immediate Complications

ComplicationSignsAction
Airway obstructionStridor, snoring, retractions, dropping SpO₂Jaw thrust, oral/nasal airway, suction, prepare for intubation
LaryngospasmSudden inability to breathe, stridorPositive pressure O₂, succinylcholine if needed
Malignant hyperthermiaTemp >104°F, tachycardia, muscle rigidity, hyperkalemia, acidosisDantrolene STAT, stop triggering agents, cool patient
AnaphylaxisBronchospasm, hypotension, angioedema, urticariaEpinephrine, O₂, fluids, antihistamines
🚨 NCLEX Trap: Malignant hyperthermia is triggered by succinylcholine and volatile anesthetics (halothane, isoflurane, sevoflurane). Treatment is DANTROLENE sodium — this is the ONLY drug. Know that MH is autosomal dominant, so family history is critical. Patient must avoid ALL triggering agents for life.

5. Wound Dehiscence vs Evisceration

📋 Surgical Emergencies

DehiscenceEvisceration
DefinitionWound edges separateOrgans protrude through wound
ActionCover with sterile dressing, notify surgeonCover with STERILE SALINE-SOAKED dressing, knees bent, NPO, STAT surgical consult
🚨 NCLEX Trap: For evisceration, the dressing MUST be saline-soaked (keeps organs moist). Do NOT push organs back in. Keep patient supine with knees flexed to reduce abdominal tension. This is a surgical EMERGENCY.

🎯 Clinical Judgment Case Study

Perioperative Anesthesia

Case: Malignant Hyperthermia

During general anesthesia with succinylcholine and sevoflurane, the patient's temperature rises to 39.8°C, HR increases to 140, and ETCO₂ rises to 70 mmHg. The patient is rigid.

What is the FIRST priority intervention?

  1. Administer dantrolene 2.5 mg/kg IV immediately
  2. Discontinue all volatile anesthetics and succinylcholine
  3. Apply ice packs and cooled IV fluids
  4. Draw arterial blood gas
Answer: 2 — Discontinue triggers FIRST, then dantrolene.Wrong — the answer is BOTH simultaneously, but if forced to choose: stop triggers first. Actually, the correct NCLEX answer is discontinue all anesthetic agents (remove vaporizers, flush circuit with 100% O₂ at 10 L/min) AND give dantrolene ASAP. The order matters: stop the trigger, then treat. Dantrolene is the specific antidote (ryanodine receptor antagonist). Hyperthermia is a late sign — increased ETCO₂ and muscle rigidity are earlier indicators.
