NCLEX Renal Disorders: Complete Study Guide

Chronic kidney disease, acute kidney injury, dialysis, glomerulonephritis, and nephrotic syndrome — pathophysiology, nursing priorities, and NCLEX traps.

1. Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)

📋 Three Phases of AKI

PhaseOnsetUrine OutputBUN/CrKey Concerns
Onset/OliguricHours to days<400 mL/dayRapidly risingFluid overload, hyperkalemia, uremia
Diuretic1-3 weeksGradual increaseStill elevatedDehydration, electrolyte losses
RecoveryWeeks to monthsNormalizesReturn to baselineMay not fully recover
💡 Causes of AKI — "AEIOU"
Acute glomerulonephritis | Emboli | Infection | Obstruction | Underperfusion (prerenal = most common)
✅ Nursing Priorities:
• Strict I&O, daily weights (same scale/time)
• Monitor potassium (life-threatening if >6.0)
• Fluid restriction (800 mL + previous day's urine output)
• Low-protein, low-sodium, low-potassium, low-phosphorus diet
• Prepare for dialysis if: K+ >6.5, fluid overload + pulmonary edema, uremic pericarditis, severe acidosis
🚨 NCLEX Trap: In AKI, calcium DECREASES and phosphate INCREASES. Giving calcium supplements is common. Watch for signs of hypocalcemia: tetany, Chvostek's/Trousseau's signs, seizures.

2. Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD)

📋 Stages of CKD (GFR-Based)

StageGFR (mL/min)Description
1≥90Normal with kidney damage
260-89Mild decrease
3a45-59Mild-moderate
3b30-44Moderate-severe
415-29Severe — prepare for RRT
5<15End-stage renal disease (ESRD)
Clinical Manifestations ("RENAL FAILURE"):
Renal osteodystrophy | Electrolyte imbalance | Neuropathy | Anemia | Lethargy | Fluid overload | Acidentosis | Intolerance to cold | Lung crackles | Uremic breath | Restricted diet | Edema
✅ Nursing Priorities:
• Blood pressure control (ACE-I/ARB — kidney-protective)
• Erythropoietin for anemia (target Hgb 11-12)
• Iron supplementation
• Phosphate binders with meals
• Vitamin D/calcitriol for bone health
• Sodium and fluid restriction
• AV fistula assessment for dialysis prep

3. Dialysis

📋 Hemodialysis (HD) vs. Peritoneal Dialysis (PD)

FeatureHemodialysisPeritoneal Dialysis
AccessAV fistula (best), graft, or catheterPeritoneal catheter (Tenckhoff)
Frequency3x/week, 3-4 hoursDaily (CAPD or APD/cycler)
DietStrict fluid, potassium, phosphate restrictionMore liberal (protein loss in dialysate)
Infection riskBacteremia, sepsisPeritonitis (cloudy effluent, abdominal pain, fever)
ContraindicationsCardiovascular instabilityRecent abdominal surgery, adhesions, obesity
✅ HD Nursing Priorities:
• Check AV fistula for thrill and bruit before EVERY treatment
• No BP, IV, or blood draws in fistula arm
• Monitor for hypotension, cramps, disequilibrium syndrome
• Weigh before and after (target UF = interdialytic weight gain)
• Heparin-free dialysis if active bleeding
✅ PD Nursing Priorities:
• Aseptic technique — peritonitis is #1 complication
• Monitor effluent: should be clear/straw-colored
• Cloudy effluent + abdominal pain + fever = peritonitis → culture and antibiotics
• Weigh daily, monitor blood pressure
• Check catheter exit site for infection
🚨 NCLEX Trap: Disequilibrium syndrome = headache, nausea, seizures, confusion during/after dialysis. Caused by rapid fluid/electrolyte shifts. Slow down dialysis, give mannitol. Also: NEVER check BP on an AV fistula arm!

4. Glomerulonephritis vs. Nephrotic Syndrome

📋 Key Differences

FeatureGlomerulonephritisNephrotic Syndrome
Primary issueInflammationIncreased permeability
ProteinuriaModerateMassive (>3.5 g/day)
RBCs in urineHematuria (cola-colored)Minimal
EdemaMildSevere (anasarca), periorbital
Blood pressureHypertensionNormal or low
Common causePost-strep infectionMinimal change disease
🚨 NCLEX Trap: Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis follows a strep throat or skin infection by 1-3 weeks. Look for: hematuria, hypertension, periorbital edema, decreased urine output. Penicillin treats the strep, but glomerulonephritis is immune-mediated — supportive care only.
