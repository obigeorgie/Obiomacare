NCLEX Respiratory Disorders: Complete Study Guide

COPD, asthma, pneumonia, tuberculosis, and pulmonary embolism — pathophysiology, nursing priorities, and NCLEX traps.

1. COPD (Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease)

Chronic Bronchitis vs. Emphysema

FeatureChronic Bronchitis ("Blue Bloater")Emphysema ("Pink Puffer")
Primary issueExcessive mucus productionAlveolar wall destruction
AppearanceCyanotic, edematousThin, barrel-chested
PaCO₂Elevated (retained)Normal or low
ComplicationCor pulmonaleRespiratory failure
Mnemonic — "COPD"
Chronic cough | O₂ carefully (keep 1-2L, avoid suppressing hypoxic drive) | Pursed-lip breathing | Diet high-calorie, low-carb
Nursing Priorities:
• Low-flow O₂ (1-2L NC) — NEVER high flow in chronic CO₂ retainers!
• Pursed-lip and diaphragmatic breathing
• High-Fowler's or tripod position
• Bronchodilators first, then mucolytics, then chest PT
• Monitor ABGs — watch for respiratory acidosis
• Smoking cessation #1 intervention
NCLEX Trap: Giving high-flow O₂ to a COPD patient can cause apnea. These patients rely on hypoxic drive. Always use low-flow O₂ (1-2L) unless acute PE or other exception.

2. Asthma

Acute Asthma Exacerbation

Symptoms: Wheezing, dyspnea, chest tightness, tachypnea, use of accessory muscles, anxiety

Patho: Bronchospasm + inflammation + mucus plugging → airway obstruction → ventilation-perfusion mismatch.

Nursing Priorities:
• SABA (albuterol) — first line, rapid relief
• Corticosteroids (systemic if severe)
• O₂ to keep SpO₂ > 92%
• Peak expiratory flow monitoring
• IV fluids if dehydrated
• Epinephrine or magnesium sulfate for severe cases
NCLEX Trap: A "silent chest" (no wheezing) in asthma is a LATE, DANGEROUS sign — means no air movement. Also, anxiety can precede respiratory failure — don't just reassure, reassess!

3. Pneumonia

Nursing Priorities:
• Antibiotics within 4-6 hours of admission (CMS core measure)
• O₂ therapy, respiratory monitoring
• Incentive spirometry q1h while awake
• Turn, cough, deep breathe
• Hydration (unless contraindicated)
• Pneumonia vaccine for high-risk patients before discharge
CURB-65 Severity Criteria:
Confusion | Urea >19 | Respiratory rate ≥30 | BP <90/60 | Age ≥65 — Score ≥2 = admit

4. Pulmonary Embolism (PE)

Acute PE

Symptoms — "PLEURITIC"
Pleuritic chest pain | Low SpO₂ | Elevated HR | Unexplained dyspnea | Restlessness | Immobilized (recent surgery, travel) | Tachypnea | Increased D-dimer | Calf pain (DVT)

Patho: Thrombus (usually from DVT) lodges in pulmonary vasculature → V/Q mismatch → hypoxemia → right heart strain.

Nursing Priorities:
• High-flow O₂ (non-rebreather if needed)
• IV access, labs (D-dimer, ABG, troponin, BNP)
• Anticoagulation (heparin drip or LMWH)
• Absolute bed rest — NO leg massage
• Prepare for thrombolytics or embolectomy if massive PE
• Monitor for hemodynamic instability
NCLEX Trap: Massive PE presents with hypotension, syncope, or cardiac arrest. Don't wait for imaging — if suspected with hemodynamic compromise, treat immediately. And NEVER massage a leg with suspected DVT!

5. Tuberculosis (TB)

Active TB vs. Latent TB

FeatureLatent TBActive TB
SymptomsNoneCough >3 weeks, hemoptysis, night sweats, weight loss, fever
ContagiousNoYes (airborne precautions)
Chest X-rayNormal or calcified granulomaCavitary lesions, upper lobe infiltrates
TreatmentINH or rifapentine (3-4 months)RIPE: Rifampin, INH, Pyrazinamide, Ethambutol (2 months), then RI (4 months)
Nursing Priorities:
• AIRBORNE precautions (N95, negative pressure room)
• Sputum AFB x3 for diagnosis
• DOT (Directly Observed Therapy) for adherence
• Monitor liver function (hepatotoxicity from RIPE)
• Educate: Rifampin turns body fluids orange-red
NCLEX Trap: TB is AIRBORNE, not droplet. Use N95, not surgical mask. A patient is no longer contagious after 2-3 weeks of effective therapy and 3 negative sputum AFB smears.

NGN Case Study: COPD Exacerbation

Client Scenario

Mr. Henderson, 68-year-old male, arrives at the ED with severe shortness of breath. He has a 40 pack-year smoking history and was diagnosed with COPD 5 years ago. He reports increased sputum production (yellow-green, thick) and using his rescue inhaler every 2 hours for the past 3 days. He is on home oxygen at 2L NC.

Vital Signs Physical Assessment ABG

Vital Signs: BP 154/92, HR 110, RR 28, Temp 37.8°C, SpO₂ 82% on 2L NC

Physical Assessment: Tripod positioning, using accessory muscles. Prolonged expiration with wheezing. Barrel chest. Unable to speak in full sentences. JVD present. Peripheral edema 2+ bilaterally. Distant heart sounds.

ABG: pH 7.28, PaCO₂ 68, PaO₂ 52, HCO₃ 32, SaO₂ 82%

Question 1 — Recognize Cues: Based on the ABG, what is the primary acid-base disturbance?

  • A. Respiratory acidosis with metabolic alkalosis
  • B. Respiratory acidosis with metabolic compensation
  • C. Metabolic acidosis with respiratory compensation
  • D. Respiratory alkalosis with metabolic compensation

Correct: B. Respiratory acidosis with metabolic compensation

pH 7.28 (acidotic), PaCO₂ 68 (elevated → respiratory acidosis), HCO₃ 32 (elevated → metabolic compensation). In COPD with chronic CO₂ retention, the kidneys compensate by retaining bicarbonate. This is a compensated respiratory acidosis. The patient is a "CO₂ retainer" — their baseline may be pH 7.35, PaCO₂ 55, HCO₃ 30. The acute change is the increased PaCO₂ from 55 to 68.

Question 2 — Analyze Cues: The nurse places the patient on 4L NC. His SpO₂ improves to 90% but he becomes drowsy and his RR drops to 18. What is the most likely cause?

  • A. Sedative effect of hypoxia correction
  • B. CO₂ narcosis from excessive oxygen
  • C. Impending respiratory arrest
  • D. Normal response to reduced work of breathing

Correct: B. CO₂ narcosis from excessive oxygen

In CO₂ retainers, the hypoxic drive is the primary stimulus for breathing (not CO₂). High-flow oxygen removes this hypoxic drive, causing hypoventilation and CO₂ accumulation (CO₂ narcosis). The drowsiness and decreased RR are signs of this. For COPD patients, oxygen should be titrated to 88-92% SpO₂ (not 94-98%). This is one of the most tested concepts on NCLEX.

Question 3 — Prioritize Hypotheses: The provider orders albuterol-ipratropium nebulizer, methylprednisolone 125mg IV, and doxycycline 100mg PO. The patient's SpO₂ is 86% on 2L NC. What is the nurse's priority action?

  • A. Administer the bronchodilator nebulizer first
  • B. Increase oxygen to maintain SpO₂ 88-92%
  • C. Give methylprednisolone to reduce inflammation
  • D. Start doxycycline for the infection

Correct: B. Increase oxygen to maintain SpO₂ 88-92%

While bronchodilators are important, the immediate priority is adequate oxygenation. SpO₂ of 86% is too low. Titrate O₂ to achieve 88-92% (the target for COPD patients). Once oxygenation is adequate, administer bronchodilators. Steroids and antibiotics are important but not the most time-sensitive intervention. The ABCDE approach applies: Airway and Breathing before medications.

Question 4 — Generate Solutions: The patient improves and is transferred to the medical floor. Which discharge instruction is highest priority?

  • A. "Use your albuterol inhaler before your steroid inhaler"
  • B. "Increase your home oxygen to 4L during exacerbations"
  • C. "Get the pneumococcal and annual flu vaccines"
  • D. "Stop smoking immediately"

Correct: D. "Stop smoking immediately"

Smoking cessation is the single most important intervention to slow COPD progression and reduce exacerbations. No other intervention has as much impact on long-term outcomes. While vaccines (C) are important and inhaler technique (A) matters, smoking cessation is paramount. Home oxygen should NEVER be adjusted without provider direction — increasing flow without monitoring can cause CO₂ narcosis.

Question 5 — Take Action: On day 2, the patient reports he "can't afford" his new inhalers. Which nursing action is most appropriate?

  • A. Tell him to use his rescue inhaler only when absolutely necessary
  • B. Contact social work and explore patient assistance programs
  • C. Simplify his regimen to the cheapest available medications
  • D. Provide samples from the hospital pharmacy

Correct: B. Contact social work and explore patient assistance programs

Medication non-adherence due to cost is a major barrier. The nurse should involve social work, explore pharmaceutical assistance programs, check for generic alternatives, and connect the patient with resources. Telling him to ration his inhaler (A) is dangerous. Simplifying without provider input (C) is outside nursing scope. Samples (D) are a temporary fix, not a sustainable solution.

Question 6 — Evaluate Outcomes: At 6-month follow-up, the patient reports using his rescue inhaler 2-3 times per week and has had no exacerbations. Which outcome indicates the treatment plan is effective?

  • A. Rescue inhaler use decreased from daily to 2-3 times per week
  • B. The patient has gained 10 pounds since discharge
  • C. The patient reports feeling "tired all the time"
  • D. The patient uses his home oxygen 24 hours a day

Correct: A. Rescue inhaler use decreased from daily to 2-3 times per week

Reduced rescue inhaler use indicates better disease control and fewer bronchospastic episodes. No exacerbations is also a positive outcome. Weight gain (B) could indicate fluid retention or steroids. Fatigue (C) is concerning and needs evaluation. Using O₂ 24/7 (D) may indicate disease progression — O₂ should be used as prescribed, not continuously unless ordered.

NGN Unfolding Case Study: COPD Exacerbation

Phase 1: Assessment Phase 2: Intervention Phase 3: Deterioration

Phase 1: Recognize Cues

A 68-year-old with COPD (FEV₁ 45% predicted) is admitted with increased dyspnea. He reports using his rescue inhaler 6 times in the past 24 hours.

Vital Signs: Temp 37.8°C, HR 110, BP 148/88, RR 28, SpO₂ 84% on 2L NC

ABG on 2L: pH 7.32, PaCO₂ 58, PaO₂ 58, HCO₃⁻ 30

Which finding requires the MOST IMMEDIATE action?

  1. Respiratory rate of 28 with accessory muscle use
  2. SpO₂ 84% despite supplemental O₂
  3. ABG showing respiratory acidosis with compensation
  4. Increased rescue inhaler use indicating poor control

Correct: B — SpO₂ 84% despite O₂.

While all findings are abnormal, hypoxemia unresponsive to supplemental O₂ is life-threatening. In COPD, target SpO₂ is 88-92% (not 94%+ like other patients). SpO₂ 84% indicates severe hypoxemia that could lead to organ failure. The nurse must increase O₂ and notify the provider immediately. ABG findings are expected in acute-on-chronic COPD and guide treatment but aren't as immediately threatening as refractory hypoxemia.

Phase 2: Generate Solutions

The provider orders: increase O₂ to 4L NC, albuterol-ipratropium nebulizer q4h, methylprednisolone 125 mg IV daily, and sputum culture.

One hour later, the patient's SpO₂ is 90% on 4L, but he becomes drowsy and difficult to arouse.

New ABG on 4L: pH 7.28, PaCO₂ 68, PaO₂ 62, HCO₃⁻ 32

What is the nurse's priority action?

  1. Continue current O₂ at 4L — drowsiness is expected with COPD
  2. Decrease O₂ to 2L to prevent CO₂ retention
  3. Prepare for non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (BiPAP)
  4. Increase O₂ to 6L to improve oxygenation

Correct: C — Prepare for BiPAP.

The patient has acute-on-chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure (PaCO₂ 68 with worsening acidosis) with altered mental status. This is an indication for NIV (BiPAP). Decreasing O₂ would worsen hypoxemia. Increasing O₂ would worsen CO₂ retention. The correct action is preparing for BiPAP while notifying the provider. If NIV fails or patient is too altered, intubation may be needed.

Phase 3: Evaluate & Take Action

The patient is started on BiPAP (IPAP 12, EPAP 5, FiO₂ 40%). After 2 hours:

Vital Signs: HR 96, BP 132/78, RR 20, SpO₂ 92%

ABG: pH 7.36, PaCO₂ 52, PaO₂ 68, HCO₃⁻ 30

The patient suddenly pulls off the BiPAP mask and says, "I can't breathe with this thing on!" His SpO₂ drops to 86%.

What is the nurse's FIRST action?

  1. Reassure the patient and attempt to reapply the mask
  2. Switch to high-flow nasal cannula at 40 L/min
  3. Call the provider for intubation orders
  4. Place a nasopharyngeal airway and bag-mask ventilate

Correct: A — Reassure and attempt to reapply the mask.

BiPAP is the patient's lifeline. The most common reason for intolerance is anxiety and claustrophobia, not true respiratory failure. The nurse should calmly explain the purpose, coach breathing, and attempt reapplication. If the patient truly cannot tolerate it despite coaching, then escalate to high-flow NC or notify the provider. But the first step is always to address the psychological barrier — many patients tolerate BiPAP once they understand it helps them breathe.
