Stroke & Seizures Deep Dive

Ischemic vs hemorrhagic stroke, TIA, seizure types, status epilepticus, and nursing management for the NCLEX.

🧠 Stroke (Cerebrovascular Accident)

Ischemic Stroke (85% of strokes)

Pathophysiology: Occlusion of cerebral artery → interruption of blood flow → ischemia → infarction

Causes: Thrombosis (atherosclerosis), embolism (AFib, carotid plaque, DVT with PFO), lacunar (small vessel disease)

Manifestations: Depends on affected vessel/territory
  • MCA (most common): Contralateral hemiparesis/hemiplegia (face/arm > leg), aphasia (dominant hemisphere), neglect (non-dominant), homonymous hemianopia
  • ACA: Contralateral leg weakness > arm, abulia, urinary incontinence
  • PCA: Visual field defects, alexia, memory deficits
  • Vertebrobasilar: Cranial nerve deficits, ataxia, vertigo, dysphagia, locked-in syndrome (basilar artery)
Acute Management (Time-Critical):
  1. Activate stroke protocol — "Time is brain"
  2. CT scan non-contrast: Rule out hemorrhage (must be done BEFORE thrombolytics)
  3. Thrombolytics (tPA/alteplase): IV if within 4.5 hours of symptom onset, no contraindications
  4. Mechanical thrombectomy: Large vessel occlusion within 6-24 hours (window extended with imaging selection)
  5. Aspirin: Within 24-48 hours if not receiving thrombolytics
  6. Blood pressure: Allow permissive hypertension (up to 220/120) in acute ischemic stroke to maintain perfusion. Lower ONLY if receiving thrombolytics (goal <185/110) or if hypertensive emergency.
NCLEX Trap: Do NOT give aspirin within 24 hours of thrombolytics (hemorrhage risk). Do NOT lower BP aggressively in acute ischemic stroke (reduces perfusion to penumbra). BP goals: If no tPA → allow up to 220/120. If tPA → <185/110 before, <180/105 after.

Hemorrhagic Stroke (15% of strokes)

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH): Bleeding into brain parenchyma. Causes: HTN (most common), amyloid angiopathy, coagulopathy, vascular malformations, tumor.

Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH): Bleeding into subarachnoid space. Most common cause: ruptured aneurysm. "Thunderclap headache" — worst headache of life.

Manifestations: Sudden severe headache ("thunderclap" for SAH), nausea/vomiting, decreased LOC, seizures, focal deficits, meningeal signs (neck stiffness, photophobia), coma
Management:
  • Reverse anticoagulation: PCC, vitamin K (warfarin), idarucizumab (dabigatran), andexanet alfa (Xa inhibitors)
  • BP control: Goal <140-160 systolic (reduces hematoma expansion)
  • ICP management: Head of bed 30°, osmotherapy (mannitol, hypertonic saline), possible ventriculostomy for hydrocephalus
  • Seizure prophylaxis: If ICH with cortical involvement
  • SAH specific: Nimodipine (prevents vasospasm), early aneurysm clipping/coiling, triple-H therapy (hypertension, hypervolemia, hemodilution)
NCLEX Trap: Nimodipine is given for SAH to prevent vasospasm — NOT for BP control (can cause hypotension, give cautiously). Monitor for vasospasm (worsening neuro 4-14 days after SAH — "symptomatic vasospasm").

Post-Stroke Nursing Care

First 24 Hours:
  • Neuro checks q1-2h (GCS, pupillary response, motor function, speech)
  • BP monitoring (permissive hypertension unless tPA given)
  • Strict I&O, avoid hypotonic fluids (cerebral edema risk)
  • NIHSS scoring
  • Swallow screening before ANY oral intake (aspiration risk)
  • DVT prophylaxis (after 24-48h if no hemorrhage)
Prevent Complications:
  • Aspiration precautions — NPO until swallow eval, elevate HOB, thickened liquids if needed
  • Pressure injury prevention — frequent repositioning, special mattress
  • Contracture prevention — ROM exercises, splinting
  • Communication support — speech therapy, communication boards
  • Emotional support — depression common post-stroke

⚡ Seizures

Classification

Focal (Partial) Seizures:

  • Focal aware (simple partial): Consciousness preserved. Motor, sensory, autonomic, or psychic symptoms.
  • Focal impaired awareness (complex partial): Altered consciousness. Automatisms (lip smacking, picking at clothes). Post-ictal confusion.
  • Focal to bilateral tonic-clonic: Starts focal, spreads to both hemispheres.

Generalized Seizures:

  • Tonic-clonic (grand mal): Tonic phase (stiffening) → clonic phase (jerking). Post-ictal confusion, fatigue, headache, Todd's paralysis.
  • Absence (petit mal): Brief loss of consciousness (seconds), staring, no post-ictal confusion. Childhood onset.
  • Atonic: Sudden loss of muscle tone → "drop attacks"
  • Myoclonic: Brief jerks

Status Epilepticus

Definition: Seizure >5 minutes OR recurrent seizures without recovery of consciousness between

Medical emergency — neuronal damage after 30 minutes, mortality increases with duration

Management Protocol:
  1. ABCs: Airway, breathing, circulation. Oxygen, suction, position on side. Do NOT force objects into mouth.
  2. Lorazepam 4 mg IV (repeat x1 if needed) — first line
  3. Second-line: Fosphenytoin, valproic acid, or levetiracetam IV
  4. Refractory: Midazolam or propofol infusion, intubation
  5. Identify cause: Glucose (give thiamine BEFORE glucose if malnourished), electrolytes, ABG, tox screen, head CT, antiepileptic levels
NCLEX Trap: Do NOT restrain during seizure (muscle injury). Do NOT put anything in mouth (broken teeth, aspiration). Time the seizure. Protect from injury (clear area, pad headboard). After seizure: recovery position, monitor airway, assess for injury, reassure patient.

Common Antiepileptic Drugs (AEDs)

Drug Key Side Effects Key Monitoring
Phenytoin Gingival hyperplasia, hirsutism, ataxia, SJS/TEN risk Drug levels, therapeutic 10-20 mcg/mL
Carbamazepine Hyponatremia, SJS/TEN, hepatotoxicity, bone marrow suppression CBC, LFTs, Na⁺, levels
Valproic acid Weight gain, tremor, hair loss, hepatotoxicity, pancreatitis, teratogenic LFTs, ammonia, platelets
Levetiracetam Behavioral changes, irritability, somnolence Renal dosing
Lamotrigine Rash (SJS/TEN — slow titration), dizziness Titrate slowly
