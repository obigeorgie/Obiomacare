Trauma Nursing for NCLEX: Primary & Secondary Survey

Hemorrhage control, spinal precautions, shock management, and multi-system trauma. The emergency concepts the NCLEX tests — with clinical scenarios from the ER.

By Nnamdi Okorafor, RN · 15-year ER nurse · Updated August 2026 · Obioma

Why trauma matters on the NCLEX: Trauma scenarios test your ability to prioritize, recognize life threats, and act under pressure. The NGN case studies often present a multi-system trauma patient and ask you to identify priorities, recognize deterioration, and select interventions.

The Primary Survey: ABCDE

The primary survey is a rapid, systematic assessment to identify and treat life-threatening conditions. Don't move to the next step until the current one is addressed.

A — Airway with C-Spine Control

  • Assess patency: Is the patient talking? Gasping? Silent?
  • Look for obstruction: blood, vomit, foreign body, tongue
  • Listen for stridor, gurgling, snoring
  • Open airway: jaw thrust (if suspected C-spine injury) or head-tilt chin-lift
  • Suction if needed
  • Insert oropharyngeal airway (unconscious) or nasopharyngeal airway (conscious)
  • If airway compromised → intubate

Key: Always assume C-spine injury in trauma until cleared. Maintain inline stabilization during airway maneuvers.

B — Breathing & Ventilation

  • Inspect: chest rise, respiratory rate, paradoxical movement, open wounds
  • Palpate: tracheal deviation, crepitus, flail segments
  • Percuss: hyperresonance (pneumothorax) or dullness (hemothorax)
  • Auscultate: breath sounds present? Equal?
  • Apply O₂: 15L non-rebreather if compromised

Life threats: Tension pneumothorax, open pneumothorax, massive hemothorax, flail chest

C — Circulation with Hemorrhage Control

  • Check pulse: rate, quality, rhythm
  • Check skin: color, temperature, moisture
  • Check capillary refill (<2 seconds normal)
  • Control external bleeding: direct pressure → tourniquet (if limb) → hemostatic dressing
  • Establish 2 large-bore IVs (14g or 16g)
  • Draw labs: type & cross, CBC, coags, chemistries, lactate, ABG
  • Start warmed crystalloid (LR or NS)

D — Disability (Neurologic)

  • Level of consciousness: AVPU (Alert, Voice, Pain, Unresponsive) or GCS
  • Pupil size and reaction
  • Movement: purposeful? Withdrawal? Posturing?
  • Check glucose (hypoglycemia can mimic brain injury)

E — Exposure & Environmental Control

  • Completely undress patient (cut clothes if needed)
  • Inspect for injuries: front, back, sides, under hair, axilla, groin
  • Log roll with C-spine precautions to inspect back
  • Prevent hypothermia: warm blankets, warmed fluids, increase room temp

Hypothermia is a trauma triad of death (with acidosis and coagulopathy). Keep them warm.

The Secondary Survey: Head-to-Toe

After the primary survey is complete and life threats are addressed, perform a comprehensive head-to-toe exam. This includes:

  • History: SAMPLE (Signs/Symptoms, Allergies, Medications, Past history, Last meal, Events)
  • Head: Scalp lacerations, skull fractures (Battle sign, raccoon eyes), facial fractures
  • Neck: Tracheal deviation, jugular venous distension, c-spine tenderness
  • Chest: Breath sounds, heart sounds, chest wall integrity
  • Abdomen: Distension, tenderness, rigidity, seatbelt sign
  • Pelvis: Stability, tenderness, open fractures
  • Extremities: Pulses, deformity, compartment syndrome signs
  • Neurologic: Reassess GCS, focal deficits, spinal level

NCLEX Tip — Neurogenic Shock vs. Spinal Shock:
Neurogenic shock: Hemodynamic problem — hypotension, bradycardia, vasodilation. From disruption of sympathetic outflow (T6+).
Spinal shock: Neurologic problem — flaccid paralysis, areflexia, loss of sensation below injury. From spinal cord injury at any level.
A patient can have BOTH.

Immediate Hemorrhage Control

  1. Direct pressure — hold firm pressure for at least 10 minutes
  2. Tourniquet — if direct pressure fails and it's an extremity. Place 2-3 inches above wound, tighten until bleeding stops. Mark time. Don't remove in field.
  3. Hemostatic dressing — pack into wound if tourniquet not applicable
  4. Pressure dressing — bulky dressing with elastic wrap

Massive Transfusion Protocol (MTP)

Activated when patient requires >10 units PRBCs in 24 hours or >4 units in 1 hour:

  • Ratio: 1:1:1 (PRBC : FFP : Platelets) — mimics whole blood
  • Give crystalloid sparingly once blood products available
  • Warm all products (hypothermia worsens coagulopathy)
  • Monitor calcium (citrate in blood products binds calcium)
  • Goal: systolic BP 80-90 mmHg (permissive hypotension) until hemorrhage controlled

Signs of Increased Intracranial Pressure (ICP)

  • Early: Headache, nausea, vomiting, altered LOC, restlessness
  • Late: Cushing's triad (hypertension, bradycardia, irregular respirations), pupillary changes, decorticate/decerebrate posturing

NCLEX Tip — Head Injury Priority: For any head trauma patient, monitor for unequal pupils, worsening GCS, and vomiting. These are signs of expanding intracranial hematoma. Head CT is the diagnostic test of choice.

Concussion Management

  • Neuro checks every 2 hours for first 24 hours
  • No sports or strenuous activity until symptom-free
  • Return to play protocol: 6 steps, 24 hours between each
  • Second impact syndrome: rare but fatal — never return too early

Spinal Injuries

Spinal Precautions

  • Log roll as a unit (head, shoulders, hips, legs move together)
  • Maintain inline stabilization until C-spine cleared clinically AND radiographically
  • Hard cervical collar
  • Backboard for transport (remove ASAP to prevent pressure injuries)

Autonomic Dysreflexia (T6 and Above)

  • Trigger: Noxious stimulus below injury (full bladder, constipation, tight clothing, pressure injury)
  • Signs: Severe hypertension (up to 300/200), bradycardia, headache, flushing above injury, pale/cool below injury
  • This is a medical emergency
  • Action: Sit patient up, loosen clothing, check bladder (catheterize if full), check for impaction, monitor BP, antihypertensives PRN

Chest Trauma

Tension Pneumothorax

  • Mechanism: Air enters pleural space but can't escape → pressure builds
  • Signs: Severe respiratory distress, tracheal deviation away from affected side, JVD, absent breath sounds, hypotension, tachycardia
  • Action: Needle decompression immediately (2nd intercostal space, midclavicular line) → chest tube
  • Don't wait for X-ray. This is a clinical diagnosis.

Hemothorax

  • Blood in pleural space (often from rib fracture lacerating intercostal artery or lung)
  • Dullness to percussion, decreased breath sounds
  • Chest tube drainage: >1500mL immediately or >200mL/hr for 2-4 hours = thoracotomy

Cardiac Tamponade (Beck's Triad)

  • Hypotension
  • JVD
  • Muffled heart sounds
  • Also: pulsus paradoxus (drop in SBP >10 mmHg on inspiration)
  • Action: Pericardiocentesis (emergency needle aspiration) or thoracotomy

Abdominal Trauma

Blunt vs. Penetrating

  • Blunt: Seatbelt sign, handlebar injury, fall — can cause solid organ injury (liver, spleen, kidney)
  • Penetrating: Gunshot, stab — can injure any structure. If anterior abdomen or back between nipples and pubis → assume intraperitoneal injury

Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (FAST)

Bedside ultrasound looking for free fluid (blood) in:

  1. Pericardium
  2. Right upper quadrant (Morison's pouch — liver/kidney)
  3. Left upper quadrant (spleen/kidney)
  4. Suprapubic (bladder)

Positive FAST + unstable patient = go to OR immediately.

Seatbelt Sign

  • Ecchymosis across abdomen from lap belt
  • High association with bowel injury and lumbar spine fracture
  • CT abdomen even if stable

🧩 NGN Case Study: Multi-System Trauma

Client Scenario

James, 28-year-old male, is brought to the ED by ambulance after a high-speed motorcycle crash. He was wearing a helmet. He was thrown approximately 20 feet. EMS reports he was alert at the scene but became increasingly confused during transport. He has a large scalp laceration with active bleeding, and his left leg appears deformed.

Vital Signs Physical Assessment Labs Provider Orders

Vital Signs: BP 92/58, HR 128, RR 28, SpO₂ 89% on room air, Temp 35.8°C (96.4°F)

Physical Assessment: Alert but confused (GCS 13: E4 V4 M5). 5cm scalp laceration oozing blood. Left thigh with obvious deformity and swelling. Equal breath sounds bilaterally but diminished at bases. Abdomen soft, non-distended, no seatbelt sign. Pelvis stable. Moves all extremities.

Labs: Hgb 9.2 g/dL, Hct 27%, Type & cross sent, Lactate 4.8 mmol/L, ABG: pH 7.28, PaCO₂ 32, HCO₃ 18

Provider Orders: 2 large-bore IVs, warmed LR bolus, CT head/c-spine/abdomen/pelvis, X-ray left femur, tetanus, consult orthopedics

Question 1 — Recognize Cues: Based on the vital signs and assessment, which finding requires the nurse's immediate intervention before proceeding with the secondary survey?

  • A. Scalp laceration with active bleeding
  • B. SpO₂ 89% on room air
  • C. Left femur deformity
  • D. Lactate 4.8 mmol/L

Correct: B. SpO₂ 89% on room air

The primary survey follows ABCDE. The patient's oxygen saturation is critically low at 89%. Airway is patent (talking), but breathing is compromised. This must be addressed immediately with supplemental oxygen (15L non-rebreather) before proceeding. While the scalp laceration and femur fracture are important, they are not immediately life-threatening in the same way as hypoxemia.

Question 2 — Analyze Cues: The nurse applies supplemental oxygen and the patient's SpO₂ improves to 96%. The nurse notes the patient's blood pressure has dropped to 84/50 despite 2L of warmed LR. Which is the most likely cause of his deteriorating hemodynamics?

  • A. Neurogenic shock from spinal cord injury
  • B. Hypovolemic shock from internal/external bleeding
  • C. Cardiogenic shock from myocardial contusion
  • D. Septic shock from wound contamination

Correct: B. Hypovolemic shock from internal/external bleeding

The patient has a scalp laceration (external bleeding) and a deformed femur (can lose 1-2L into the thigh). His tachycardia (128), hypotension (84/50), cool temperature (35.8°C), and low Hgb (9.2) all point to hypovolemic shock from hemorrhage. Neurogenic shock would present with warm, dry skin and bradycardia — this patient is tachycardic. Cardiogenic shock is less likely with a soft, non-distended abdomen and stable pelvis (though not ruled out yet).

Question 3 — Prioritize Hypotheses: The trauma team is preparing the patient for CT scan. The nurse should prioritize which intervention before transport?

  • A. Splint the left femur
  • B. Insert a urinary catheter
  • C. Ensure two large-bore IVs are patent with blood products available
  • D. Administer pain medication

Correct: C. Ensure two large-bore IVs are patent with blood products available

The patient is hemodynamically unstable (BP 84/50, HR 128) and has signs of ongoing hemorrhage. Before transporting to CT, the nurse must ensure adequate IV access and that blood products are available/infusing. Patients can deteriorate rapidly during transport. Splinting the femur is important but secondary to hemorrhage control. Pain medication could mask neuro changes and drop BP further.

Question 4 — Generate Solutions: The CT reveals a small epidural hematoma and a displaced mid-shaft femur fracture. The patient becomes increasingly agitated and his pupils are now unequal (R 5mm, L 3mm). What is the nurse's priority action?

  • A. Prepare for immediate OR for femur fixation
  • B. Notify provider of change in neuro status
  • C. Administer sedation to control agitation
  • D. Recheck blood glucose

Correct: B. Notify provider of change in neuro status

Unequal pupils with increasing agitation in a head trauma patient indicates expanding intracranial hematoma with rising ICP. This is a neurosurgical emergency requiring immediate intervention (likely decompressive craniotomy). While preparing for OR is important, the nurse must first communicate the change to the provider. Sedation could mask neuro deterioration. Glucose check is important but not the priority with unequal pupils.

Question 5 — Take Action: The patient is scheduled for emergent craniotomy and ORIF of the femur. Which of the following should the nurse include in the pre-operative checklist? Select all that apply.

  • A. Type and crossmatch for 6 units PRBCs
  • B. Verify informed consent for both procedures
  • C. Remove the cervical collar for skin prep
  • D. Ensure Foley catheter is inserted
  • E. Administer prophylactic antibiotics within 60 minutes of incision

Correct: A, B, D, E

A: Yes — patient is already anemic (Hgb 9.2) with ongoing blood loss. Blood must be available.
B: Yes — informed consent is required. In emergent trauma, implied consent or emergency consent protocols apply if patient cannot consent and no surrogate is available.
C: No — C-spine precautions must be maintained until cleared radiographically. The collar is NOT removed for skin prep.
D: Yes — Foley is needed for accurate I&O monitoring, especially with hemodynamic instability and intraoperative fluids.
E: Yes — Surgical Care Improvement Project (SCIP) requires prophylactic antibiotics within 60 minutes before incision.

Question 6 — Evaluate Outcomes: Post-operatively, the patient is in the ICU. His ICP monitor reads 22 mmHg. Which nursing intervention is most appropriate?

  • A. Position head of bed at 15°
  • B. Administer mannitol 0.25 g/kg IV
  • C. Suction the endotracheal tube vigorously
  • D. Cluster all care activities to minimize stimulation

Correct: B. Administer mannitol 0.25 g/kg IV

Normal ICP is <15 mmHg. An ICP of 22 mmHg is elevated and requires intervention. Mannitol is an osmotic diuretic that reduces cerebral edema. Head of bed should be at 30° (not 15°) to promote venous drainage. Vigorous suctioning increases ICP — pre-oxygenate, suction briefly. Clustering care is incorrect — space out activities to minimize ICP spikes.
