NCLEX Wound Care & Pressure Injuries

Pressure injury staging, wound healing phases, dressing selection, prevention strategies, and NCLEX priority-setting.

1. Pressure Injury Staging (NPIAP 2016)

📋 Pressure Injury Stages

StageDescriptionKey Features
Stage 1Intact skin with non-blanchable erythemaSkin intact, red that doesn't blanch, may be painful/firm/warm
Stage 2Partial-thickness skin lossShallow open ulcer, pink/red wound bed, NO slough, may be blister
Stage 3Full-thickness skin lossSubcutaneous fat visible, may have slough/undermining, NO bone/tendon/muscle
Stage 4Full-thickness tissue lossBone, tendon, or muscle exposed, often with undermining/tunneling
UnstageableFull-thickness, base covered by slough/escharCannot determine depth until debrided
Deep Tissue Pressure Injury (DTPI)Intact or non-intact skin with deep tissue injuryPurple/maroon localized area, blood-filled blister, painful/boggy/firm
🚨 NCLEX Trap: Stage 1 pressure injuries have INTACT skin — if the skin is broken, it's at least Stage 2. DTPI can look like a bruise but is actually deep tissue damage. Once DTPI opens, it often stages as 3 or 4. NEVER massage reddened areas — can cause deeper tissue damage!

2. Braden Scale for Predicting Pressure Sore Risk

📋 Braden Scale Components (Score 6-23)

FactorScore RangeWhat It Assesses
Sensory perception1-4Ability to respond to pressure-related discomfort
Moisture1-4Degree to which skin is exposed to moisture
Activity1-4Physical activity level
Mobility1-4Ability to change and control body position
Nutrition1-4Usual food intake pattern
Friction/Shear1-3Amount of resistance to movement
💡 Risk Levels:
19-23 = Not at risk | 15-18 = Mild risk | 13-14 = Moderate risk | 10-12 = High risk | ≤9 = Very high risk
✅ Prevention Strategies (for ALL at-risk patients):
• Reposition q2h (side-lying 30° — not 90° to reduce trochanter pressure)
• Pressure-reducing mattress/overlays
• Keep head of bed ≤30° (reduces sacral shear)
• Moisture barrier creams for incontinence
• Adequate nutrition (protein, vitamin C, zinc)
• Heel protectors/offloading devices
• Skin inspection at every turn

3. Wound Healing Phases

📋 Three Phases of Wound Healing

PhaseDurationCharacteristicsNursing Focus
InflammatoryDays 1-4Hemostasis, vasodilation, neutrophils, edema, erythema, warmthProtect wound, manage drainage, prevent infection
ProliferativeDays 5-20Granulation tissue, angiogenesis, collagen deposition, wound contraction, epithelializationMoist wound environment, adequate nutrition, protect granulation tissue (beefy red = healthy)
Maturation (Remodeling)Weeks to yearsCollagen reorganizes, scar strengthens (up to 80% original strength), scar flattens/fadesScar massage, sun protection, activity as tolerated
🚨 NCLEX Trap: Wounds heal best in a MOIST environment — not dry. Moisture promotes epithelial migration. However, too much moisture causes maceration. The goal is "moist, not wet." Also: granulation tissue should be beefy red — if it's pale, it may need more support; if it's dark/boggy, may be infected.

4. Wound Dressing Selection

📋 Dressing Types and Indications

DressingBest ForNotes
Transparent filmStage 1, autolytic debridement, IV site protectionImpermeable to fluids/bacteria, allows O₂ exchange
HydrocolloidStage 2, shallow Stage 3, autolytic debridementForms gel with exudate; don't use on infected wounds
HydrogelDry wounds, painful wounds, autolytic debridementDonates moisture; soothing
AlginateModerate to heavy exudate, Stage 3/4Derived from seaweed; forms gel; change daily
FoamModerate exudate, cushioningAbsorbent, non-adherent, can stay several days
Silver-impregnatedInfected or at-risk woundsAntimicrobial; don't use long-term (can stain)
Negative Pressure Wound Therapy (NPWT)Stage 3/4, surgical wounds, dehisced woundsPromotes granulation, reduces edema, removes exudate
✅ General Dressing Principles:
• Match absorbency to exudate amount
• Protect periwound skin (barrier cream, no tape on fragile skin)
• Consider pain during dressing changes
• Sterile technique for Stage 3/4 and surgical wounds
• Clean technique acceptable for chronic Stage 2 in stable patients

5. Common Wound Complications

📋 Wound Complications

ComplicationSignsIntervention
InfectionIncreased pain, erythema, warmth, purulent drainage, foul odor, fever, delayed healingWound culture, antibiotics, debridement, antimicrobial dressings
DehiscenceWound edges separate, fascia intactSupport with binder, NPO, prepare for possible closure
EviscerationWound opens with organs protrudingMEDICAL EMERGENCY — cover with saline-moist sterile dressing, NPO, surgery STAT
FistulaAbnormal passage between organs/skinProtect skin, manage drainage, nutritional support, possible surgery
NecrosisBlack/brown eschar, non-viable tissueDebridement (surgical, enzymatic, autolytic, mechanical)
🚨 NCLEX Trap: If evisceration occurs, NEVER push organs back in. Cover with sterile saline-soaked dressing (keeps tissue moist), keep patient NPO and supine with knees flexed, call surgeon immediately. For dehiscence, a binder may help but don't apply if evisceration is suspected.

🎯 Clinical Judgment Case Study

Wounds Pressure Injury

Case: Stage 4 Pressure Injury

An 85-year-old with dementia and incontinence has a 4cm x 3cm full-thickness wound over the sacrum with exposed bone, slough, and moderate exudate. The patient is incontinent of stool 3-4 times daily.

What is the priority wound care intervention?

  1. Apply a hydrocolloid dressing to maintain moist environment
  2. Pack the wound with gauze and change daily
  3. Apply a foam dressing with barrier cream and manage incontinence
  4. Order a low-air-loss mattress and turn every 4 hours
Answer: 3 — Foam dressing + barrier cream + incontinence management.

Stage 4 sacral pressure injury with incontinence: fecal contamination is the enemy. Hydrocolloid is wrong (requires intact skin, doesn't manage heavy exudate). Wet-to-dry gauze is outdated and painful. Priority: protect periwound skin with barrier cream (zinc oxide or petroleum), use highly absorbent foam dressing, and aggressively manage incontinence (scheduled toileting, bowel program, fecal management system if needed). Low-air-loss mattress is important but secondary to wound protection.
