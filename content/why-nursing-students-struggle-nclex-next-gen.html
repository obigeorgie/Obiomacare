Why Nursing Students Struggle With NCLEX Next Gen

Published: 2026-07-24 Read Time: 6 minutes Category: NCLEX Prep | Clinical Judgment Keywords: nclex next gen, clinical judgment, nursing prioritization, nclex anxiety, nclex case study ---

It Started With a Tweet

"I have so much anxiety about NCLEX I'd rather just get it over with (sad)" That sentiment shows up constantly in nursing student communities. And it's not the only one. Talk to any group of nursing students and you'll hear the same concerns. The pattern is unmistakable: NCLEX anxiety is at an all-time high. But here's what surprised us: the anxiety isn't about knowing content. It's about something deeper. ---

The Shift Nobody Talks About

Remember the old NCLEX?
What drug class does metoprolol belong to?
That's a memorization question. You either know it or you don't. Now look at Next Gen NCLEX:
Your patient reports dizziness after taking their prescribed metoprolol. What's your FIRST action?
This isn't asking what you know. It's asking how you think. And that's exactly where nursing students are getting stuck. ---

What We Found From Real Students

We scraped 30+ real nursing student tweets and found five consistent pain points:

1. "I Feel Like I Don't Know Anything"

One student wrote: "Me studying for NCLEX: shit I dont know shitttttt I have to study a lot" Sound familiar? Here's the truth: You DO know things. You just haven't been trained to apply them. Nursing school teaches facts. NCLEX Next Gen tests clinical judgment. Those are different skills.

2. "Next Gen Case Studies Fry My Brain"

"Staring at a Next Gen NCLEX case study or a 40-page pharmacology module and feeling like your brain is completely fried" Case studies are overwhelming because students try to read everything. But you don't need to. The secret? Read the QUESTION first. It tells you exactly what to look for.

3. "My Anxiety Is Through the Roof"

We found tweets like:
  • "my anxiety is through the roof"
  • "I have anxiety as if I'm going take nclex tm tf I can't sleep"
  • "this shit got my anxiety wayyyyy upppppp"
    • This isn't weakness. It's your brain protecting you from failure. The problem? Anxiety hijacks the same region you need for clinical judgment. Solution: 5 deep breaths before every study session. Train your brain to associate NCLEX prep with calm, not panic.

    4. "I Memorized Everything But Still Fail Practice Tests"

    This is the #1 mistake we see. Students spend hours memorizing drug classes, lab values, and disease processes. Then they face a question like:
    Your post-op patient can't breathe. What's your FIRST action?
    And they freeze. Because this isn't a memorization question. It's a clinical judgment question.

    5. "SATA Questions Destroy My Confidence"

    Select All That Apply (SATA) questions are the nemesis of nursing students everywhere. The mistake? Trying to find ALL the right answers at once. The fix: Treat each option as a true/false question. Look at A alone. Then B. Then C. When in doubt? Pick the SAFEST option. NCLEX is a safety exam. ---

    The Clinical Judgment Framework

    The NCSBN (the people who write the NCLEX) developed something called the Clinical Judgment Measurement Model. It's not just theory—it's the exact framework they use to write questions. Here's the simplified version:

    Step 1: Recognize Cues

    Don't read the whole case study. Find the RED FLAGS:
  • Abnormal vital signs
  • Concerning patient behavior
  • Off lab values
  • Medication errors

    • Step 2: Analyze Cues

    Ask: "What does this MEAN for my patient?" Not: "What drug is this?" Instead: "Why is this patient tachycardic?"

    Step 3: Prioritize

    What's the WORST thing that could happen? Remember: Airway > Breathing > Circulation > Safety NCLEX prioritizes:
  • Acute over chronic
  • Expected vs. unexpected
  • Safety first, always

    • Step 4: Generate Solutions

    Look at your answer options:
  • Cross out 2 that are clearly wrong
  • Compare the remaining 2
  • Pick the SAFEST one

    • Step 5: Take Action

    Your chosen action should:
  • Address the most life-threatening issue
  • Be within your scope of practice
  • Be the FIRST thing you'd do
