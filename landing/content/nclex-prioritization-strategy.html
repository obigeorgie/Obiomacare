NCLEX Prioritization Strategy: The ABCDE Method

Prioritization questions are the most common on NCLEX. The ABCDE method gives you a foolproof framework for every scenario.

By Nnamdi Okorafor, RN · 15-year ER nurse · Updated July 2026 · Obioma

Here's the truth: NCLEX prioritization questions aren't testing your knowledge. They're testing whether you can identify what will kill the patient fastest. The ABCDE method is the same framework ER nurses use in mass casualty incidents. It works on the exam because it works in real life.

What Is the ABCDE Method?

The ABCDE method is a systematic approach to patient assessment and prioritization. It ensures you address the most life-threatening problems first. Here's what each letter stands for:

A — Airway

Is the patient's airway open and protected? Can they speak? Are they choking? Is there stridor, gurgling, or silence?

Airway always comes first. A patient can survive minutes without breathing, but only seconds without an airway. If the airway is compromised, nothing else matters until it's fixed.

B — Breathing

Are they breathing? How fast? How deep? What's their oxygen saturation? Are they using accessory muscles?

Look for: tachypnea (RR > 20), bradypnea (RR < 12), hypoxemia (SpO2 < 92%), cyanosis, nasal flaring, retractions.

C — Circulation

How's their perfusion? Check pulse, blood pressure, capillary refill, skin color/temperature, and mental status.

Look for: hypotension (SBP < 90), tachycardia (HR > 100), weak pulses, delayed capillary refill (> 3 sec), cool/clammy skin, altered mental status.

D — Disability (Neuro)

What's their level of consciousness? Are they alert and oriented? Can they move all extremities?

Use the AVPU scale: Alert, Verbal, Pain, Unresponsive. Any change in mental status is significant.

E — Exposure/Environment

Check temperature, look for rashes, wounds, bleeding, or signs of abuse. Remove clothing to assess fully.

Also consider: hypothermia, hyperthermia, environmental hazards, and psychosocial needs.

The ABCDE Decision Tree for NCLEX

When you see a prioritization question, run through this mental checklist:

  1. Is anyone's airway compromised? → If yes, that's your priority. No exceptions.
  2. Is anyone in respiratory distress? → If yes, and airway is clear, this is your priority.
  3. Is anyone showing signs of shock or poor perfusion? → If yes, and A/B are stable, this is your priority.
  4. Is anyone altered or unresponsive? → If yes, and A/B/C are stable, assess neuro.
  5. Everything else → Pain, psychosocial needs, education, documentation.

NCLEX Prioritization Examples

Example 1: Who do you see first?

You have 4 patients. Who do you assess first?

  • Room 101: Post-op appendectomy, pain 3/10, vitals stable
  • Room 102: COPD, O2 sat 89% on 2L NC, RR 28, anxious
  • Room 103: MI, chest pain 8/10, BP 142/88, HR 96
  • Room 104: New admit, fever 101.2, complains of chills

Answer: Room 102. O2 sat 89% + RR 28 = respiratory failure. Airway/breathing takes priority over chest pain (stable MI) and fever.

Example 2: Which action takes priority?

A post-op patient has: BP 88/52, HR 122, RR 24, urine output 15 mL/hr, incision dressing dry.

Which action should the nurse take first?

  • a) Change the dressing
  • b) Call the provider
  • c) Check for bleeding and elevate legs
  • d) Document the findings

Answer: C. These vitals suggest hemorrhagic shock. Before calling the provider, the nurse should assess for bleeding (the cause) and position the patient to improve perfusion. Independent nursing actions first.

Common NCLEX Prioritization Traps

Trap #1: "The patient in the most pain"

Pain is important, but it's not life-threatening. A patient with pain 10/10 but stable vitals can wait. A patient with pain 3/10 and SpO2 86% cannot.

Trap #2: "The newest admission"

New admissions need assessment, but if all your patients are stable, the new admit is your priority. If someone is crashing, the new admit waits.

Trap #3: "The patient who is demanding"

Demanding patients get attention in real life. On NCLEX, they don't get priority unless their ABCs are compromised.

ER nurse wisdom: In a true emergency, you don't have time to think through a algorithm. You react based on pattern recognition. That's why repetition matters. The more prioritization questions you practice, the more automatic your responses become.
