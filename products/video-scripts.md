## Video 1: "The NGN NCLEX is Testing Something Different" (8-10 min)

### Hook (0:00-0:30)
"Okay, real talk. I just got off a 12-hour ER shift, and I need to tell you something about the NCLEX that nobody's talking about. If you're studying for the Next Generation NCLEX, and you're memorizing drug cards and lab values — you're studying for the wrong test."

### Problem (0:30-1:30)
"The NGN isn't testing what you know. It's testing how you THINK. Let me show you what I mean.

Traditional NCLEX question: 'What is the normal range for potassium?' — You either know it or you don't.

NGN question: You have three patients. One has K+ 3.2, one has K+ 5.8, one has K+ 4.2 but is on dialysis. Who's your priority? 

That's not memorization. That's clinical judgment. And that's what I do every single shift."

### Solution Intro (1:30-2:30)
"After 10 years in ER and oncology, I've developed a framework that I use instinctively. And I've broken it down into something you can learn. It's called the Clinical Judgment Model, and it has four phases."

### Content — The Four Phases (2:30-6:00)

**Phase 1: Recognize Cues** (2:30-3:30)
"Cues are pieces of data that tell you something important. Vital signs, lab values, patient behavior, your gut feeling. But here's the key — not every abnormal value is urgent. A BP of 148/92 in a stable patient? Monitor it. Same BP in a post-op craniotomy patient with a sudden headache? Call the doctor NOW. Same number. Different action. Context is everything."

**Phase 2: Analyze** (3:30-4:30)
"Once you recognize cues, ask three questions: What's the underlying problem? What could go wrong next? And what do I need to know that I don't know? Experienced nurses think two steps ahead. That's what the NGN is testing."

**Phase 3: Prioritize** (4:30-5:15)
"When everything is urgent, who do you see first? Use the ABC framework as your foundation, but know that it's not the whole house. I'll show you the prioritization matrix that ER nurses actually use."

**Phase 4: Take Action + Evaluate** (5:15-6:00)
"Least invasive first. Then reassess. Did it work? What changed? This loop — recognize, analyze, prioritize, act, evaluate — this is clinical judgment. And this is what the NGN tests."

### Social Proof (6:00-7:00)
"I've used this framework in codes, in rapid responses, in quiet moments where something just felt wrong. It's not magic. It's a thinking pattern you can develop. And I've taught it to dozens of new grads who went from panicking to thinking clearly under pressure."

## Video 2: "Real ER Case: Chest Pain Walkthrough" (10-12 min)

### Hook (0:00-0:30)
"I'm going to walk you through a real case — one that almost got away from me. This is exactly how I think through a chest pain patient in the ER. And if you're studying for the NGN, this is the kind of scenario you'll see."

### The Case (0:30-1:00)
"3:47 AM. 54-year-old male. Says he has 'indigestion.' Took Tums. Didn't help. His wife brought him in."

### My Initial Assessment (1:00-2:30)
"Walking in, I notice three things before I even check vitals: He's diaphoretic. He's clutching his CHEST, not his stomach. And his wife says he 'never complains.' That's three red flags. This is NOT indigestion."

### Vital Signs & Actions (2:30-4:30)
"BP 168/94, HR 102, RR 22, SpO2 95%. Here's what I do — in order:

1. O2, continuous monitoring
2. 12-lead EKG
3. IV access x2, draw troponin
4. Aspirin 325mg
5. Nitroglycerin 0.4mg SL
6. Call MD — 'Possible STEMI, EKG pending'

But here's the nuance — his EKG shows ST elevation in the inferior leads. That means possible right ventricular involvement. If I give more nitroglycerin and he has RV involvement, his BP will crash. So I hold further nitro and get a right-sided EKG."

### The Outcome (4:30-5:30)
"Right-sided EKG clear. Troponin elevated. Cardiology activated. Door-to-balloon time: 62 minutes. He went home five days later."

### The Lesson (5:30-6:30)
"The patient kept saying 'it's just indigestion.' He almost convinced me. But his presentation — diaphoresis, chest clutching, never-complains history — overrode his self-diagnosis. Trust your assessment."

## Video 3: "How to Prioritize When Everything is Urgent" (8-10 min)

### Hook (0:00-0:30)
"3 AM. You're the only nurse with four patients. Two of them are calling. One is desatting. And the charge nurse just asked if you can take an admission. What do you do? This is the #1 question I get from new grads."

### The Framework (0:30-2:00)
"I use a tier system. Tier 1 is life-threatening — airway, breathing, circulation. Tier 2 is urgent — chest pain, neuro changes, sepsis. Tier 3 is important but not emergent. Tier 4 is routine."

### Live Scenario (2:00-5:00)
"Let me give you a real scenario from my med-surg days:

Room 1: Post-op appy, pain 8/10, refusing to ambulate
Room 2: CHF exacerbation, SpO2 91%, RR 24, crackles
Room 3: DKA, insulin drip, K+ 3.2
Room 4: Fall risk, mild dementia, trying to get out of bed

Who do you see first?"

[Walk through the analysis]

"Room 2 first — respiratory compromise. Then Room 3 — hypokalemia with insulin drip is cardiac risk. Then Room 1 — pain control and mobilization. Room 4 last — delegate to tech for sitter."

### Common Mistakes (5:00-6:00)
"New grads often see the patient who's complaining loudest first. Or the one they like. Or they try to do everything at once and do nothing well. The key is systematic prioritization."

### Practice Tip (6:00-7:00)
"Practice this every shift. Even when you're not overwhelmed. Ask yourself: If I had to drop everything right now, who would I see first? That mental exercise builds the pattern."

## Video 4: "SBAR: How to Talk to Doctors Like a Senior Nurse" (6-8 min)

### Hook (0:00-0:30)
"I'm going to teach you the SBAR method that actually works at 3 AM. Not the academic version. The real version that gets doctors to act fast and respect you."

### The Problem (0:30-1:30)
"Most new grads either: Give too much information and the doctor tunes out, or give too little and the doctor has to ask 20 questions. Both waste time. Both frustrate everyone."

### Standard SBAR (1:30-2:30)
"For routine stuff: Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation. Keep it structured. But for urgent stuff — lead with what you need."

### Urgent SBAR (2:30-4:00)
"Example: 'I need you to see room 8 NOW. Post-op hip, was stable, now BP 88/52, HR 118, temp 102.5, confused, incision purulent. I suspect sepsis. Need antibiotics and possible ICU.'

Notice: I led with the request. Then background. Then assessment. Then recommendation. Don't bury the lede when someone's crashing."

### Practice Example (4:00-5:30)
"Let's practice. You're calling about a patient with chest pain. What's your urgent SBAR?"

### The Confidence Piece (5:30-6:30)

## Video 5: "First-Year Survival: What I Wish I'd Known" (8-10 min)

"Your first year as a nurse will be the hardest year of your career. You'll cry. You'll doubt yourself. You'll wonder if you made a huge mistake. I did all of those things. Here's what I wish someone had told me."

"Week 1: You're drinking from a fire hose. Everything is new. You don't know where anything is. That's normal.

Month 1: You feel incompetent. You're slow. You forget things. That's normal too.

Month 3: You hit a plateau. You think you should be better by now. You should. But you're not. And that's ALSO normal."

### Time Management (2:00-3:30)
"The time block method. First hour: report, orders, quick rounds. Next two hours: meds and assessments. Middle of shift: procedures and education. End of shift: documentation and report. Cluster care. Delegate. And take your breaks — you're legally entitled."

### Building Intuition (3:30-4:30)
"Experienced nurses seem to 'just know.' It's not magic. It's pattern recognition. Keep a journal. Write down interesting cases, mistakes, what you learned. Review it monthly. You'll see your growth."

### Handling Burnout (4:30-5:30)
"The signs: dreading work, emotional exhaustion, cynicism. Prevention: sleep 7-8 hours, exercise, eat real food, talk to someone, take your PTO. And know when to leave a job. Your license and well-being matter more than loyalty to a bad employer."

### The One Thing (5:30-6:30)
"If I could tell every new grad one thing: Trust your gut. If a patient 'just doesn't look right,' investigate. That vague feeling has saved lives."

### Final Message (6:30-7:30)

## Recording Notes

### Setup
- Quiet room or closet (for sound absorption)
- Ring light or window light (face-level)
- Phone on tripod (landscape for YouTube, portrait for TikTok/IG)
- Lapel mic or phone's built-in (close to mouth)

### Style
- Speak like you're talking to a friend
- Use your real voice — don't be "professional"
- Show emotion — frustration, urgency, empathy
- Use hand gestures (even if camera doesn't show them — it affects your voice)

### Editing
- Jump cuts every 3-5 seconds (keep energy high)
- Text overlays for key phrases
- B-roll: hospital footage (generic), writing on whiteboard, patient scenarios (no HIPAA)
- Music: low background, uplifting

### Thumbnail Formula
- Your face (reaction/expression)
- Big text: "NGN NCLEX" / "PRIORITIZATION" / "SBAR"
- Red arrow or circle pointing to key element
- Bright colors (red, yellow, blue)
