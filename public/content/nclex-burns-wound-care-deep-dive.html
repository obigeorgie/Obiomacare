Burn classifications, fluid resuscitation, wound healing stages, infection prevention, and nursing priorities for the NCLEX.

Burn Classification by Extent

Rule of Nines (Adults)

  • Head and neck: 9%
  • Each arm: 9%
  • Each leg: 18%
  • Anterior trunk: 18%
  • Posterior trunk: 18%
  • Perineum: 1%

Palm method: Patient's palm (including fingers) = ~1% TBSA

Mnemonic: "9, 9, 9, 18, 18, 18, 1" — Head 9%, arms 9% each (2), legs 18% each (2), trunk front/back 18% each (2), perineum 1%

Fluid Resuscitation (Parkland Formula)

Parkland Formula (First 24 Hours)

Fluid = 4 mL × kg body weight × % TBSA burned

  • First 8 hours: Give 1/2 of total fluid (from time of injury, NOT from hospital arrival)
  • Next 16 hours: Give remaining 1/2

Example: 70 kg patient with 40% TBSA burn = 4 × 70 × 40 = 11,200 mL total. Give 5,600 mL in first 8 hours, 5,600 mL in next 16 hours.

NCLEX Trap: Parkland formula timing starts from TIME OF BURN, not time of arrival. If patient arrives 2 hours post-burn, give the first half over the remaining 6 hours. Also, use LACTATED RINGER'S (not normal saline) — provides lactate buffer and prevents hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis.

Monitoring Fluid Resuscitation

  • Urine output: Target 0.5–1.0 mL/kg/hr (adults), 1.0 mL/kg/hr (children)
  • Vital signs: MAP >65 mmHg, HR <120
  • Mental status: Alert, oriented
  • Peripheral perfusion: Warm extremities, capillary refill <2 seconds
  • Lab: Hematocrit, lactate, base deficit

Burn Emergencies & Complications

Inhalation Injury

Suspect if: Burns in enclosed space, facial burns, singed nasal hairs, soot in mouth/nares, hoarseness, stridor, carbonaceous sputum

Management: 100% humidified oxygen, early intubation if airway edema suspected, carboxyhemoglobin level, bronchoscopy

Carbon monoxide poisoning: COHb >15% significant. Pulse oximetry is INACCURATE (reads falsely high). Treatment: 100% O2 (half-life of COHb ~74 min on 100% O2 vs. 320 min on room air)

Circumferential Burns: Can cause compartment syndrome from eschar constriction. Escharotomy (surgical incision through eschar) needed if compromised circulation or ventilation. Signs: decreased pulses, cool extremities, increased pain, difficulty breathing (chest/eschar).

Electrical Burns

  • Entry and exit wounds — internal damage often MORE severe than skin suggests
  • Cardiac dysrhythmias (VF, asystole) — continuous cardiac monitoring for 24–48 hours
  • Rhabdomyolysis → myoglobinuria → acute kidney injury
  • Compartment syndrome from deep tissue necrosis
  • Cataracts (delayed complication)
Nursing Priority: Aggressive fluid resuscitation (may exceed Parkland). Urine alkalinization (target urine pH >6.5) to prevent myoglobin precipitation in kidneys. Monitor CK, myoglobin, renal function.

Chemical Burns

  • Immediate, prolonged irrigation with water for 20–30+ minutes (exceptions: dry powder — brush off first; phenol — polyethylene glycol)
  • Do NOT attempt to neutralize chemicals (exothermic reaction)
  • Alkali burns (lye, cement) penetrate deeper and longer than acid burns
  • Hydrofluoric acid: calcium gluconate gel or intra-arterial infusion

Wound Healing

Stages of Wound Healing

  • Hemostasis (immediate): Vasoconstriction, platelet plug, clotting cascade
  • Inflammatory (0–3 days): Vasodilation, increased permeability, neutrophils then macrophages. Signs: redness, heat, swelling, pain
  • Proliferative (3–21 days): Granulation tissue, angiogenesis, collagen synthesis, epithelialization, wound contraction
  • Maturation/Remodeling (21 days–2 years): Collagen reorganization, scar strengthening. Scar reaches ~80% of original strength
Mnemonic: "HIP-M" — Hemostasis, Inflammatory, Proliferative, Maturation

Factors Impairing Wound Healing

  • Hypoxia, poor perfusion (peripheral vascular disease)
  • Diabetes (poor glycemic control)
  • Malnutrition (low protein, vitamin C, zinc)
  • Obesity, smoking, immunosuppression
  • Infection, foreign bodies, necrotic tissue
  • Corticosteroids, chemotherapy

NGN Case Study: Major Burn Injury

Client Scenario

Mr. Thompson, 42-year-old male, was brought to the ED after a house fire. He was trapped in a smoke-filled room for approximately 10 minutes before rescue. He has burns to his face, neck, bilateral upper extremities, and anterior chest.

Vital Signs Physical Assessment Burn Estimation

Vital Signs: BP 88/54, HR 128, RR 28, Temp 36.2°C, SpO₂ 89% on room air

Physical Assessment: Soot around mouth and nares. Singed nasal hairs. Stridorous breath sounds. Burns are leathery, white, and painless to pinprick over chest and arms. Facial burns are erythematous with blisters. Patient is alert but anxious.

Burn Estimation (Rule of Nines): Face 4.5%, anterior chest 9%, bilateral upper extremities 18% = 31.5% TBSA

Question 1 — Recognize Cues: What is the priority intervention?

  • A. Calculate fluid resuscitation using Parkland formula
  • B. Assess airway for inhalation injury and prepare for intubation
  • C. Debride the burn wounds and apply silver sulfadiazine
  • D. Insert a Foley catheter to monitor urine output

Correct: B. Assess airway for inhalation injury and prepare for intubation

The patient has classic signs of inhalation injury: soot around mouth, singed nasal hairs, stridor, and facial burns. Airway edema from thermal injury can progress rapidly and cause complete obstruction. Intubation should be performed early before edema makes it impossible. This takes absolute priority over fluid resuscitation, wound care, or catheter insertion. "Airway first" is the rule in burn patients with inhalation injury.

Question 2 — Analyze Cues: The patient is intubated. Which IV fluid order is most appropriate for the first 24 hours?

  • A. D5½NS at 125 mL/hr
  • B. Lactated Ringer's using Parkland formula: 4 mL × 31.5 kg × 31.5% = 3,969 mL total; half in first 8 hours
  • C. Normal saline at 250 mL/hr
  • D. Albumin 25% infusion to replace protein losses

Correct: B. Lactated Ringer's using Parkland formula

Parkland formula: 4 mL × kg × %TBSA. Assuming 70kg: 4 × 70 × 31.5 = 8,820 mL total in 24 hours. Half (4,410 mL) in first 8 hours from time of injury, remainder over next 16 hours. LR is preferred over NS because it contains lactate (converted to bicarbonate) which helps buffer metabolic acidosis from shock. D5½NS is hypotonic and inappropriate for burn resuscitation. Albumin is used in some protocols but not as first-line.

Question 3 — Prioritize Hypotheses: At 8 hours post-injury, the patient has received 4,400 mL of LR. His urine output is 15 mL/hr and he has +1 pedal edema. BP is 94/60, HR 110. What is the nurse's priority action?

  • A. Increase the fluid rate — urine output is inadequate
  • B. Continue current rate — within acceptable parameters
  • C. Decrease the fluid rate — signs of fluid overload
  • D. Administer furosemide 20mg IV for fluid overload

Correct: A. Increase the fluid rate — urine output is inadequate

Goal urine output for burn resuscitation is 0.5-1.0 mL/kg/hr (30-50 mL/hr for adults). 15 mL/hr is inadequate despite receiving half the Parkland volume. The patient may need more fluid than the formula predicts. Edema in burn patients is expected due to capillary leak — it's not a sign to decrease fluids when urine output is low. Furosemide is contraindicated during the resuscitation phase. Titrate fluids to achieve urine output goals.

Question 4 — Generate Solutions: On day 3, the patient's temperature spikes to 39.5°C. WBC is 18,000. The wound culture grows Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Which statement about burn wound infection is correct?

  • A. Topical silver sulfadiazine is effective against Pseudomonas
  • B. Systemic antibiotics should be started immediately for all positive cultures
  • C. Fever and elevated WBC are normal in the first week post-burn
  • D. Excision and grafting should be delayed until infection clears

Correct: A. Topical silver sulfadiazine is effective against Pseudomonas

Silver sulfadiazine (Silvadene) has broad-spectrum activity including gram-negative organisms like Pseudomonas. It's the standard topical antimicrobial for burns. However, systemic antibiotics are reserved for confirmed invasive infection (not colonization) because prophylactic antibiotics increase resistance. Fever and leukocytosis can be hypermetabolic response, but with positive cultures and clinical signs, infection is likely. Early excision and grafting (within 72 hours) is preferred — it removes the nidus of infection.

Question 5 — Take Action: The patient is scheduled for excision and grafting of the chest burns. Which pre-operative nursing action is highest priority?

  • A. Ensure informed consent is signed
  • B. Verify Hgb is ≥10 g/dL and platelets ≥50,000
  • C. Shave all hair from the burn wound and donor site
  • D. Administer prophylactic broad-spectrum antibiotics

Correct: B. Verify Hgb is ≥10 g/dL and platelets ≥50,000

Excision and grafting can cause significant blood loss. Pre-op labs must confirm the patient can tolerate surgery — Hgb ≥10 and platelets ≥50,000 are typical requirements. If Hgb is low, transfuse before surgery. Consent is important but obtained by the surgeon. Shaving should be done carefully (electric razor only — no blades near burn). Prophylactic antibiotics are not routine — topical antimicrobials are preferred to prevent resistance.

Question 6 — Evaluate Outcomes: Post-grafting, the donor site (left thigh) has a bulky dressing. The patient reports severe itching at the graft site. Which nursing intervention is most appropriate?

  • A. Apply cool compresses and administer diphenhydramine
  • B. Tell the patient itching means healing is occurring
  • C. Remove the dressing to inspect the graft
  • D. Apply hydrocortisone cream to the graft site

Correct: A. Apply cool compresses and administer diphenhydramine

Itching is common during burn healing due to histamine release and nerve regeneration. Cool compresses provide relief without disturbing the graft. Diphenhydramine (Benadryl) is an antihistamine that reduces itching. Hydrocortisone should NOT be applied to a fresh graft (impairs healing). Removing the dressing could dislodge the graft. While itching does indicate healing, the patient needs actual relief, not just reassurance.
