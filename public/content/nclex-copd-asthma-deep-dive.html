COPD & Asthma Deep Dive

Pathophysiology, exacerbations, medications, oxygen therapy, and nursing management for COPD and asthma on the NCLEX.

🌬️ COPD (Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease)

Types & Pathophysiology

Emphysema: Destruction of alveolar walls → loss of elastic recoil → air trapping. Pink puffer (thin, barrel chest, pursed-lip breathing, non-productive cough).

Chronic Bronchitis: Hypersecretion of mucus, inflamed airways. Blue bloater (overweight, cyanotic, productive cough, edema, signs of cor pulmonale).

Mnemonic: "Blue Bloater, Pink Puffer" — Blue bloater = bronchitis (cyanotic, edema). Pink puffer = emphysema (not cyanotic, thin, pursed-lip breathing).

COPD Exacerbation

Triggers: Infection (most common), air pollution, weather changes, non-adherence to therapy
Manifestations: Increased dyspnea, increased cough/sputum, sputum purulence, wheezing, tachypnea, use of accessory muscles, confusion (hypercapnia)
Management:
  1. Bronchodilators: SABA (albuterol) + SAMA (ipratropium) nebulizers
  2. Corticosteroids: Systemic (prednisone 40 mg x 5 days)
  3. Antibiotics: If 2+ Cardinal symptoms (increased dyspnea, sputum volume, purulence) OR ventilator support needed
  4. Oxygen: Target SpO₂ 88-92% (NOT 94-100% — risk of CO₂ retention and respiratory acidosis)
  5. Non-invasive ventilation (BiPAP): For acute respiratory acidosis (pH ≤7.35 + PaCO₂ >45)
  6. Mechanical ventilation: If NIV fails or contraindicated
NCLEX Trap: In COPD, give LOW-FLOW oxygen (2 L NC) to target 88-92%. High-flow O₂ suppresses hypoxic drive (these patients rely on hypoxemia to stimulate breathing). Monitor ABGs. Watch for signs of CO₂ narcosis (somnolence, confusion, headache).

COPD Medications

Class Examples Notes
SABA Albuterol, levalbuterol Rescue inhaler. Tremor, tachycardia.
SAMA Ipratropium Dry mouth, urinary retention. First-line for COPD.
LABA Salmeterol, formoterol NOT monotherapy — must combine with ICS
LAMA Tiotropium, umeclidinium First-line maintenance for COPD
ICS Fluticasone, budesonide For frequent exacerbations. Rinse mouth after use.
PDE-4 inhibitor Roflumilast Severe COPD with chronic bronchitis. Weight loss, diarrhea.

🫁 Asthma

Pathophysiology

Chronic inflammatory disorder of airways characterized by:

  • Bronchospasm (smooth muscle contraction)
  • Mucosal edema
  • Increased mucus production
  • Airway hyperresponsiveness

Reversible (unlike COPD) with treatment

Asthma Classification

Severity Daytime Symptoms Nighttime Symptoms Lung Function
Intermittent ≤2 days/week ≤2x/month FEV₁ ≥80%
Persistent (Mild) >2 days/week 3-4x/month FEV₁ ≥80%
Persistent (Moderate) Daily >1x/week FEV₁ 60-80%
Persistent (Severe) Throughout the day Frequent FEV₁ <60%

Acute Asthma Exacerbation

Severe exacerbation signs:
  • Unable to speak in full sentences
  • Accessory muscle use, tripod position
  • Respiratory rate >30
  • Heart rate >120
  • PEF <50% predicted
  • Pulse oximetry <90%
  • Silent chest (ominous — severe airway obstruction)
  • Altered consciousness
Management:
  1. Oxygen: Target SpO₂ 94-98% (asthma patients do NOT have chronic hypercapnia)
  2. SABA: Albuterol nebulizer or MDI with spacer (every 20 min x 3, then reassess)
  3. Ipratropium bromide: Add to SABA (reduces hospitalization)
  4. Systemic steroids: Prednisone/prednisolone within 1 hour (oral = IV for most patients)
  5. Magnesium sulfate: 2 g IV over 20 min (if severe, not responding to initial therapy)
  6. BiPAP/Intubation: If impending respiratory failure
NCLEX Trap: In asthma, give HIGH-FLOW oxygen (target 94-98%) — these patients are NOT CO₂ retainers. Do NOT give sedatives (mask respiratory failure). Peak flow <50% = severe. Intubate if altered mental status, exhaustion, or refractory hypoxemia.

Status Asthmaticus

Definition: Severe asthma that persists despite standard treatment (SABA + corticosteroids)

Management:
  • Continuous nebulized albuterol
  • High-dose systemic steroids
  • Magnesium sulfate
  • Heliox (reduces work of breathing)
  • Mechanical ventilation if needed (permissive hypercapnia strategy)
  • Ketamine (bronchodilator + sedation for intubation)

🩺 Long-Term Management

Asthma Action Plan (Green/Yellow/Red Zones)

  • Green (Doing Well): PEF 80-100% predicted. Continue daily controller.
  • Yellow (Caution): PEF 50-80%. Increase SABA, may increase ICS, contact provider.
  • Red (Medical Alert): PEF <50%. Take rescue inhaler immediately, call provider or 911.
Controller vs Reliever: Controllers (ICS, LABA+ICS combo) are taken DAILY to prevent inflammation. Relievers (SABA) are used as needed for acute symptoms. Patient education: Do NOT overuse SABA (>2x/week indicates poor control).

🧩 NGN Case Study: Acute Asthma Exacerbation

Client Scenario

Ms. Priya Sharma, 16-year-old female, arrives in the emergency department with her mother. She reports sudden onset of severe wheezing and chest tightness after playing soccer in cold weather. She has a history of moderate persistent asthma and uses an albuterol inhaler "a few times a week." Mother reports Priya has been using her inhaler "constantly" for the past 2 hours with little relief. Priya is unable to speak in full sentences.

Vital Signs Nurse's Notes Provider Orders

📊 Vital Signs & Assessment

  • BP: 132/78 mmHg
  • HR: 118 bpm
  • RR: 32 breaths/min, labored
  • Temp: 37.0°C (98.6°F)
  • SpO₂: 88% on room air
  • Height/Weight: 162 cm, 52 kg
  • Appearance: Tripod positioning, using accessory muscles, diaphoretic
  • Lungs: Diminished breath sounds bilaterally, prolonged expiratory phase, no wheezing audible
  • Peak Expiratory Flow (PEF): 120 L/min (predicted 450 L/min = 27%)
  • Speech: One-word responses only

📝 Nurse's Notes

Patient reports forgetting her daily controller medication (fluticasone/salmeterol) for the past week because she "felt fine." She used her albuterol inhaler approximately 12 puffs in the past 2 hours. No fever, no recent illness. No known allergies. No hospitalizations for asthma previously.

📋 Provider Orders

  1. Oxygen 2L NC, titrate to maintain SpO₂ >92%
  2. Albuterol 2.5 mg + ipratropium 0.5 mg nebulizer every 20 min × 3 doses
  3. Methylprednisolone 125 mg IV now
  4. Magnesium sulfate 2 g IV over 20 minutes
  5. Chest X-ray
  6. Continuous pulse oximetry

Clinical Judgment Questions

Question 1 — Recognize Cues: Which assessment findings indicate this is a LIFE-THREATENING asthma exacerbation? (Select all that apply)

  1. Peak flow 27% of predicted
  2. SpO₂ 88% on room air
  3. Absence of audible wheezing
  4. HR 118 bpm
  5. Inability to speak in full sentences
  6. Use of accessory muscles
Correct: A, B, C, E
Absence of wheezing in severe asthma is a SILENT CHEST — indicates insufficient air movement to produce sound (ominous sign). PEF <30% predicted, SpO₂ <90%, and inability to speak are all signs of severe/life-threatening asthma. HR 118 and accessory muscle use indicate severity but are not specific to life-threatening status.

Question 2 — Analyze Cues: The nurse notes diminished breath sounds with no wheezing. What does this finding MOST likely indicate?

  1. The bronchospasm is resolving and the patient is improving
  2. There is insufficient air movement to produce wheezing — a silent chest
  3. The patient has developed a pneumothorax
  4. The albuterol has successfully opened the airways
Correct: B
A silent chest in severe asthma is an ominous sign indicating minimal air movement due to severe bronchospasm and mucus plugging. This requires immediate escalation (possible intubation). It does NOT indicate improvement. While pneumothorax can cause absent breath sounds, the bilateral diminished sounds with severe asthma point to silent chest.

Question 3 — Prioritize Hypotheses: Which nursing diagnosis should be the nurse's TOP priority?

  1. Impaired gas exchange related to bronchospasm and mucus plugging
  2. Ineffective health management related to nonadherence with controller medication
  3. Anxiety related to difficulty breathing
  4. Risk for infection related to corticosteroid use
Correct: A
SpO₂ 88% with silent chest = life-threatening airway compromise. Impaired gas exchange is the priority. While nonadherence (B) contributed to the exacerbation, it is not the immediate priority. Anxiety (C) is secondary to hypoxia. Infection risk (D) is a long-term concern, not immediate.

Question 4 — Generate Solutions: The nurse is preparing to administer the ordered treatments. Which action should be performed FIRST?

  1. Administer methylprednisolone 125 mg IV
  2. Apply oxygen and administer albuterol/ipratropium nebulizer
  3. Give magnesium sulfate 2 g IV
  4. Draw blood for CBC and basic metabolic panel
Correct: B
Oxygen + bronchodilator is the immediate priority. The patient is hypoxemic (SpO₂ 88%) with severe bronchospasm. Albuterol is the fastest-acting bronchodilator. Steroids (A) take hours to work. Magnesium (C) is adjunctive. Labs (D) can wait.

Question 5 — Take Action: After 3 back-to-back nebulizer treatments, the client's SpO₂ is 91% on 4L NC, she can speak in short phrases, and wheezing is now audible bilaterally. Which is the nurse's BEST next action?

  1. Call the rapid response team — the patient is worsening
  2. Continue monitoring — the patient is showing signs of improvement
  3. Prepare for emergent intubation
  4. Decrease oxygen to 2L NC
Correct: B
Audible wheezing returning is actually a POSITIVE sign — indicates air is now moving through previously obstructed airways. SpO₂ improved from 88% to 91%, and she can now speak in phrases. These are signs of response to treatment. Continue current therapy and monitor closely.

Question 6 — Evaluate Outcomes: At discharge, which statement by the patient indicates a need for FURTHER teaching?

  1. "I will take my controller inhaler every day even when I feel fine."
  2. "I should use my rescue inhaler before exercise in cold weather."
  3. "If my peak flow drops below 50%, I'll call my doctor."
  4. "I only need my controller medicine when I'm having symptoms."
Correct: D
Controller medications (inhaled corticosteroids) must be taken DAILY to reduce airway inflammation and prevent exacerbations. They are NOT rescue medications. Stopping when "feeling fine" leads to exactly this situation — severe exacerbation. Green zone peak flow is >80%, yellow 50-80%, red <50%.
🧠 Case Study Learning Points:
  • Silent chest = dangerous — No wheezing means NO air movement. Call for help.
  • Peak flow <30% = severe — Predicted values matter for objective assessment
  • Wheezing returning = improving — Counterintuitive but true in severe asthma
  • Controllers are preventive — NOT PRN. Daily use reduces inflammation.
  • Oxygen + bronchodilator first — In hypoxemic patients, always address ABCs
