Delegation & Assignment NCLEX Questions: The Rules That Save Lives

By Nnamdi Okorafor, RN · 15 years ER experience · Updated July 2026

Delegation questions are where nursing students lose the most points on the NCLEX. Not because the content is hard — because the rules seem intuitive until you apply them to a real scenario.

Here's the truth: The NCLEX doesn't test whether you know what delegation IS. It tests whether you know what CAN'T be delegated.

The 5 Rights of Delegation

Before you delegate anything, you must check all five. Miss one, and you've made an error — even if the task itself is simple.

RightWhat It MeansNCLEX Trap
Right TaskThe task is within the delegatee's scopeDelegating assessment to a UAP
Right CircumstanceThe patient's condition is stable/predictableDelegating to a UAP when the patient is unstable
Right PersonThe delegatee has the skills and trainingAsking an LPN to start blood transfusions
Right DirectionClear, specific instructions givenVague orders like "check on the patient"
Right SupervisionAppropriate monitoring and follow-upDelegating and disappearing

What CAN Be Delegated to a UAP

  • Vital signs on STABLE patients
  • Bathing and hygiene
  • Ambulation with stable patients
  • Feeding (if patient can swallow safely)
  • Bed making and room tidying
  • I&O documentation (except critical measurements)
  • Transferring using assistive devices

What CANNOT Be Delegated to a UAP

  • Assessment — Only RNs assess. UAPs can collect data (vitals), but interpretation is RN-only.
  • Medication administration — Except in some states where UAPs can give OTC meds with specific training.
  • IV therapy — Starting, managing, or discontinuing IVs.
  • Patient education — Teaching about new medications, procedures, or discharge instructions.
  • Care planning — Developing or modifying the plan of care.
  • Any task requiring clinical judgment — If the answer isn't clear-cut, the RN does it.

What CAN Be Delegated to an LPN/LVN

LPNs have more training than UAPs but less than RNs. They can handle:

  • Medication administration (oral, IM, subcutaneous — check state rules)
  • Wound care for stable wounds
  • Catheterization (in some states)
  • Suctioning (in some states)
  • Data collection on stable patients
  • Reinforcing patient education (but not initial teaching)

LPNs CANNOT: Perform initial assessments, develop care plans, administer IV push medications, or care for unstable patients.

Real NCLEX Scenarios

Scenario 1: The Busy Floor

You have 6 patients and a UAP is available. Which task do you delegate?

A. Check blood glucose on your diabetic patient before lunch
B. Assess a post-op patient who just returned from PACU
C. Administer morning medications
D. Teach a patient about their new insulin regimen

Answer: A
Checking blood glucose is a routine task on a stable patient. The UAP can do this and report the result. Assessment (B), medication administration (C), and patient education (D) are all RN responsibilities.

Scenario 2: The Changing Condition

A UAP reports that your patient's blood pressure is 180/110. The patient was 138/84 this morning. What do you do?

A. Tell the UAP to recheck it in 30 minutes
B. Assess the patient yourself immediately
C. Delegate the recheck to the LPN
D. Have the UAP give the PRN antihypertensive

Answer: B
A significant change in condition requires RN assessment. You need to evaluate the patient, determine the cause, and decide on interventions. You cannot delegate assessment or clinical judgment.

Scenario 3: The Trap Question

Which task is appropriate to delegate to a UAP?

A. Obtain a stool sample from a patient with C. diff
B. Check oxygen saturation on a patient with COPD
C. Perform a blood sugar check on a stable diabetic patient
D. Change a sterile dressing on a surgical wound

Answer: C
This is a classic trap. A, B, and D all involve patients with specific conditions that require clinical judgment (infection control, respiratory assessment, sterile technique). Option C is a routine task on a stable patient — appropriate for a UAP.

The "Unstable Patient" Rule

This is the single most important rule for delegation questions:

If the patient is unstable, unpredictable, or has had a recent change in condition — NOTHING gets delegated to a UAP. The RN handles everything.

Signs of instability include:

  • Vital signs outside normal range
  • New onset pain, confusion, or neurological changes
  • Recent procedure, surgery, or transfer
  • Abnormal lab values
  • Patient or family expressing concern

Assignment Questions: Who Gets the New Patient?

Assignment questions ask you to distribute patients among nurses. The rules:

  • Match complexity to experience. New grads get stable, predictable patients. Experienced nurses get complex or unstable patients.
  • Consider continuity. If a nurse has cared for a patient for 2 days, they usually keep that patient (unless their load is too heavy).
  • Don't overload. A nurse with 2 complex patients shouldn't get a third.
  • Consider special skills. A nurse trained in dialysis should get the dialysis patient.

The 3-Question Framework for Every Delegation Question

When you see a delegation question, ask yourself:

  1. Is the patient stable? If no, RN only.
  2. Does the task require judgment? If yes, RN only.
  3. Is the delegatee trained for this? UAPs do routine tasks. LPNs do stable patient care. RNs do everything else.

Bottom Line

Delegation questions aren't about memorizing lists. They're about understanding the scope of practice for each team member and recognizing when a patient's condition requires RN-level judgment.

When in doubt: If it requires thinking, the RN does it.
