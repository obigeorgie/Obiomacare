Diabetes & Thyroid Deep Dive

DKA, HHS, insulin therapy, thyroid storm, myxedema coma, and nursing management for the NCLEX.

🍬 Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)

Pathophysiology

Absolute insulin deficiency → hyperglycemia → osmotic diuresis → dehydration + electrolyte loss. Body switches to fat metabolism → ketone production → metabolic acidosis.

Common triggers: Infection (most common), missed insulin doses, new-onset T1DM, MI, stroke, trauma, alcohol, drugs (SGLT2 inhibitors, corticosteroids)

Diagnostic Criteria

  • Blood glucose: >250 mg/dL (usually 400-800)
  • Arterial pH: <7.30
  • HCO₃⁻: <18 mEq/L
  • Ketones: Positive serum/urine ketones
  • Anion gap: >10-12 (elevated)
Manifestations:
  • Polyuria, polydipsia, polyphagia (early)
  • Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain (can mimic acute abdomen)
  • Kussmaul respirations (deep, rapid) — compensatory respiratory alkalosis
  • Fruity breath (acetone)
  • Altered mental status (if severe)
  • Dehydration: dry mucous membranes, poor skin turgor, hypotension, tachycardia

Management

Priority Order:
  1. IV Fluids: 0.9% NS 1-1.5 L in first hour (replenish intravascular volume). Then 0.45% NS when glucose drops to 200-250. Add dextrose when glucose <200 to prevent hypoglycemia while continuing insulin.
  2. Regular Insulin: 0.1 units/kg IV bolus, then 0.1 units/kg/hr continuous infusion. Goal: decrease glucose by 50-70 mg/dL/hr. Do NOT stop insulin when glucose normalizes — continue until anion gap closes.
  3. Potassium: CRITICAL. Insulin drives K⁺ into cells. If K⁺ >5.2 → hold K⁺, monitor. If K⁺ 3.3-5.2 → add K⁺ to fluids (20-30 mEq/L). If K⁺ <3.3 → hold insulin, give K⁺ until >3.3.
  4. Bicarbonate: Only if pH <6.9 (controversial, rarely needed).
  5. Monitor: Glucose hourly, electrolytes q2-4h, ABGs, strict I&O, neuro checks.
NCLEX Trap: Do NOT give bicarbonate routinely — can worsen cerebral edema. Do NOT stop insulin when glucose normalizes — must continue until anion gap normalizes. K⁺ may be falsely elevated initially (acidosis shifts K⁺ out of cells) but total body K⁺ is depleted.

🌡️ Hyperosmolar Hyperglycemic State (HHS)

Pathophysiology

Profound hyperglycemia WITHOUT significant ketosis. Insulin deficiency sufficient to cause hyperglycemia but sufficient to prevent ketogenesis. Severe dehydration and hyperosmolarity.

Common in: Type 2 diabetes, elderly, nursing home residents

Diagnostic Criteria

  • Blood glucose: >600 mg/dL (often >1000)
  • Serum osmolality: >320 mOsm/kg
  • pH: >7.30 (no acidosis)
  • HCO₃⁻: >18
  • Ketones: Minimal or absent
  • Anion gap: Normal
Manifestations: Profound dehydration, altered mental status (confusion to coma), seizures, hemiparesis (can mimic stroke), hypotension, tachycardia. NO Kussmaul respirations, NO fruity breath.
Management: Aggressive IV fluids (0.9% NS, then 0.45% NS — slower glucose reduction than DKA to prevent cerebral edema). Low-dose insulin (0.05 units/kg/hr). K⁺ replacement. Mortality is HIGHER than DKA due to underlying comorbidities and delayed recognition.

💉 Insulin Therapy

Types of Insulin

Type Onset Peak Duration
Rapid-acting (lispro, aspart, glulisine) 15 min 1-2 hr 3-5 hr
Short-acting (Regular) 30-60 min 2-4 hr 5-8 hr
Intermediate (NPH) 1-2 hr 4-8 hr 12-18 hr
Long-acting (glargine, detemir) 1-2 hr No peak 20-24 hr
Ultra-long (degludec) 30-90 min No peak >42 hr
Basal-Bolus Concept: Basal insulin (glargine/detemir) covers background needs between meals and overnight. Bolus insulin (rapid-acting) covers mealtime carbs and corrects high glucose.

Hypoglycemia Management

Definition: Blood glucose <70 mg/dL

Manifestations: Shakiness, sweating, tachycardia, hunger, irritability, confusion, seizures, coma
Treatment (15-15 Rule):
  1. Give 15g fast-acting carbohydrate (glucose tablets, 4 oz juice, regular soda, honey)
  2. Recheck glucose in 15 minutes
  3. Repeat if still <70
  4. Once normalized, give snack with protein/fat to maintain

If unconscious: Do NOT give PO. Give glucagon 1 mg IM/SQ or IV dextrose (D50).

NCLEX Trap: After giving glucagon, place patient on side (risk of vomiting/aspiration). Glucagon raises glucose by stimulating glycogenolysis — will NOT work if glycogen stores depleted (starvation, chronic hypoglycemia, alcohol). Always give IV dextrose if available.

🧮 Insulin Calculations (High-Yield for NCLEX)

1. Insulin-to-Carbohydrate Ratio (ICR)

How many grams of carbs 1 unit of insulin covers.

  • 500 Rule: 500 ÷ Total Daily Dose (TDD) = grams of carbs per 1 unit
  • Example: TDD = 50 units → 500 ÷ 50 = 10 g carbs per 1 unit
  • Meal has 60g carbs → 60 ÷ 10 = 6 units bolus
2. Correction Factor (Insulin Sensitivity Factor)

How much 1 unit of insulin lowers blood glucose.

  • 1800 Rule (rapid-acting): 1800 ÷ TDD = mg/dL drop per 1 unit
  • 1500 Rule (regular insulin): 1500 ÷ TDD = mg/dL drop per 1 unit
  • Example: TDD = 50 units, rapid-acting → 1800 ÷ 50 = 36 mg/dL per unit
  • Current glucose 220, target 120 → 220 - 120 = 100 → 100 ÷ 36 = 2.8 → round to 3 units correction
3. Total Bolus Dose = Meal Coverage + Correction

Example: 60g carbs (ICR 1:10) + glucose 220 (target 120, CF 1:36)

  • Meal: 60 ÷ 10 = 6 units
  • Correction: (220 - 120) ÷ 36 = 2.8 → 3 units
  • Total bolus = 9 units
NCLEX Trap: Do NOT correct if glucose is at or below target. Do NOT give correction bolus within 3-4 hours of previous rapid-acting insulin (stacking risk). Always round to nearest whole unit on NCLEX unless told otherwise.
4. Basal Insulin Calculation
  • Basal typically = 40-50% of TDD
  • Example: TDD = 50 units → Basal = 20-25 units (usually given as single daily glargine or split detemir)
  • Bolus = remaining 50-60% of TDD divided across meals
Memory Trick: "CARBS"
Count carbs → Apply ICR (500 rule) → Review glucose → Bolus correction (1800 rule) → Sum total dose

🦋 Thyroid Disorders

Thyroid Storm (Thyrotoxic Crisis)

Definition: Life-threatening exacerbation of hyperthyroidism

Triggers: Infection, surgery, trauma, iodine load, childbirth, withdrawal of antithyroid meds

Manifestations: High fever (>104°F), severe tachycardia/arrhythmias (AFib), hypertension, CHF, agitation, delirium, seizures, coma, GI symptoms (vomiting, diarrhea), jaundice
Management (ABCDE + specific):
  1. Supportive: IV fluids, cooling blankets, acetaminophen (NOT aspirin — displaces T4 from TBG). Treat precipitating cause.
  2. Block synthesis: PTU or methimazole (PTU preferred — also blocks peripheral T4→T3 conversion)
  3. Block release: Iodine (SSKI or Lugol's) — give 1 hour AFTER antithyroid drug (or else feeds hormone synthesis)
  4. Block conversion: Hydrocortisone (also treats possible adrenal insufficiency)
  5. Block effects: Beta-blockers (propranolol — also blocks T4→T3 conversion)
Mnemonic: "Block Synthesis, Release, Conversion, Effects"

Myxedema Coma

Definition: Life-threatening decompensation of severe hypothyroidism

Triggers: Infection, cold exposure, trauma, sedatives, anesthesia, CVA

Manifestations: Hypothermia (hallmark), bradycardia, hypotension, hypoventilation, altered mental status/coma, hyponatremia, hypoglycemia, delayed reflexes (relaxation phase)
Management:
  1. Levothyroxine (T4): IV loading dose (200-400 mcg), then 50-100 mcg/day
  2. Liothyronine (T3): Add if no improvement in 24-48 hours (T4→T3 conversion impaired in severe illness)
  3. Hydrocortisone: Until adrenal insufficiency ruled out
  4. Supportive: Warm gradually (avoid rapid rewarming — peripheral vasodilation → cardiovascular collapse), ventilatory support, treat hyponatremia and hypoglycemia, IV fluids
NCLEX Trap: Myxedema coma mortality is HIGH (30-60%). Hypothermia is a hallmark — do NOT rapidly rewarm. Give hydrocortisone BEFORE levothyroxine if adrenal insufficiency possible (thyroid hormone increases metabolism → can precipitate adrenal crisis).

🧩 NGN Case Study: Diabetic Ketoacidosis

Client Scenario

Ms. Aaliyah Johnson, 22-year-old female, is brought to the emergency department by her roommate. Roommate reports Aaliyah has been vomiting for the past 12 hours, has "fruity breath," and has been "breathing really fast and deep." Aaliyah was diagnosed with Type 1 diabetes at age 14. She admits she has not taken her insulin for the past 2 days because she "ran out and didn't have money for the refill." She also reports having flu-like symptoms and a sore throat for the past 3 days.

Vital Signs Nurse's Notes Lab Results Provider Orders

📊 Vital Signs & Assessment

  • BP: 88/56 mmHg
  • HR: 124 bpm, regular
  • RR: 36 breaths/min, deep and labored (Kussmaul)
  • Temp: 37.8°C (100°F)
  • SpO₂: 97% on room air
  • Weight: 58 kg (usual weight 64 kg)
  • Skin: Dry mucous membranes, poor skin turgor, flushed
  • Neuro: Alert and oriented x3, anxious
  • Abdomen: Diffuse tenderness, hyperactive bowel sounds
  • Respiratory: Clear bilaterally, deep rapid respirations

📝 Nurse's Notes

Patient reports polyuria and polydipsia for the past week. Has been unable to keep food or fluids down for 12 hours. Last insulin dose (lispro and glargine) was 2 days ago. No known drug allergies. Non-smoker. No alcohol or illicit drug use. Family history of autoimmune thyroid disease.

🔬 Lab Results

  • Blood glucose: 682 mg/dL
  • Serum pH: 7.18
  • HCO₃⁻: 12 mEq/L
  • Anion gap: 22
  • Serum ketones: Positive (large)
  • Serum osmolality: 318 mOsm/kg
  • Sodium: 132 mEq/L (corrected Na⁺: 141)
  • Potassium: 5.8 mEq/L
  • Chloride: 98 mEq/L
  • BUN: 38 mg/dL
  • Creatinine: 1.4 mg/dL
  • WBC: 15.4 × 10⁹/L
  • Hgb: 14.2 g/dL

📋 Provider Orders

  1. 0.9% Sodium Chloride 1L IV over 1 hour
  2. Regular insulin 6 units IV push, then 6 units/hr continuous infusion
  3. Basic metabolic panel q2h
  4. Urinalysis with culture
  5. Chest X-ray
  6. Strict intake & output, hourly vital signs
  7. Cardiac monitoring

Clinical Judgment Questions

Question 1 — Recognize Cues: Based on the clinical presentation, which findings confirm the diagnosis of DKA rather than HHS? (Select all that apply)

  1. Blood glucose 682 mg/dL
  2. pH 7.18
  3. Positive serum ketones
  4. Age 22 with Type 1 diabetes
  5. Anion gap 22
  6. Kussmaul respirations
Correct: B, C, E, F
A (glucose >600) could be HHS. D (young age/T1DM) suggests DKA but doesn't confirm it. The definitive DKA findings are: metabolic acidosis (pH <7.30, B), ketosis (C), elevated anion gap (E), and Kussmaul respirations (compensatory for acidosis, F).

Question 2 — Analyze Cues: The nurse notes the potassium level is 5.8 mEq/L. Which is the BEST interpretation of this finding?

  1. The client has adequate total body potassium and does not need replacement
  2. The potassium is elevated due to acidosis shifting K⁺ out of cells, but total body K⁺ is depleted
  3. The client is in renal failure and cannot excrete potassium
  4. Insulin should be held until potassium normalizes
Correct: B
In DKA, acidosis causes intracellular K⁺ to shift extracellularly, creating a falsely "normal" or elevated serum K⁺ despite severe total body depletion. Once insulin and fluids are given, K⁺ re-enters cells and levels drop rapidly. The client WILL need K⁺ replacement once levels fall. Insulin should NOT be held unless K⁺ <3.3.

Question 3 — Prioritize Hypotheses: The nurse is prioritizing interventions. Which action should be performed FIRST?

  1. Begin regular insulin infusion at 6 units/hr
  2. Administer 0.9% NS 1L over 1 hour
  3. Insert Foley catheter for strict I&O monitoring
  4. Draw repeat basic metabolic panel
Correct: B
The client is hypotensive (88/56) with tachycardia, dry mucous membranes, and 6 kg weight loss — indicating severe hypovolemia. Fluid resuscitation must precede insulin administration. Insulin before fluids can worsen hypotension by driving glucose and water into cells. Fluids FIRST, then insulin.

Question 4 — Generate Solutions: Four hours after treatment initiation, the client's glucose is 280 mg/dL, potassium is 3.6 mEq/L, and pH is 7.28. Which is the nurse's BEST next action?

  1. Continue current insulin infusion and add dextrose to IV fluids
  2. Stop insulin infusion since glucose is nearly normal
  3. Increase insulin infusion to achieve faster glucose reduction
  4. Switch to subcutaneous insulin now that glucose is controlled
Correct: A
Do NOT stop insulin when glucose normalizes — must continue until anion gap closes (indicates ketosis resolved). Adding dextrose (D5) to fluids allows continued insulin administration without hypoglycemia. SC insulin should not be started until patient is eating and anion gap is normal.

Question 5 — Take Action: The nurse is reviewing the morning labs on day 2. Which finding requires IMMEDIATE follow-up?

  1. Glucose 145 mg/dL
  2. Potassium 3.1 mEq/L
  3. pH 7.34
  4. Anion gap 14
Correct: B
Potassium of 3.1 mEq/L is dangerously low and can cause life-threatening arrhythmias. In DKA, K⁺ replacement is critical — add KCl to IV fluids immediately and monitor closely. The other values show continued improvement (glucose normalized, pH near normal, anion gap closing).

Question 6 — Evaluate Outcomes: The client is ready for discharge on day 3. Which statement by the client indicates a need for FURTHER teaching?

  1. "I will check my blood sugar 4 times a day and keep a log."
  2. "If my blood sugar is over 250 for two readings, I'll check for ketones."
  3. "I can skip my insulin when I'm not eating much due to illness."
  4. "I'll set up automatic refills so I never run out of insulin again."
Correct: C
NEVER skip insulin during illness — this is exactly how DKA develops (insulin deficiency + stress hormones). During illness, patients may need MORE insulin, not less (sick day rules: check glucose more often, check ketones if glucose >250, never stop basal insulin, stay hydrated, seek care if vomiting or ketones present).
🧠 Case Study Learning Points:
  • Fluids FIRST, then insulin — Hypovolemia takes priority over hyperglycemia
  • Potassium is tricky — Looks high, but total body is LOW; monitor closely during treatment
  • Don't stop insulin at 200 — Continue until anion gap normalizes (ketosis resolved)
  • Add dextrose when needed — D5 allows continued insulin without hypoglycemia
  • Never skip insulin when sick — #1 cause of DKA in known diabetics
