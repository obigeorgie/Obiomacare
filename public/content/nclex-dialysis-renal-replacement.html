Dialysis & Renal Replacement

Hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, CRRT, kidney transplant, and nursing management for the NCLEX.

Hemodialysis (HD)

Principles

Diffusion + Ultrafiltration: Blood passes through dialyzer semipermeable membrane. Waste products (urea, creatinine, K⁺) diffuse from blood to dialysate. Fluid removed by pressure gradient (ultrafiltration).

Vascular Access:

  • AV Fistula (preferred): Artery + vein anastomosis. Maturation 4-12 weeks. Lowest infection risk, longest patency.
  • AV Graft: Synthetic tube. Maturation 2-4 weeks. Higher infection/thrombosis risk than fistula.
  • Central Venous Catheter: Immediate use. Highest infection risk. Used for acute dialysis or when permanent access not ready.
AV Fistula/Graft Care:
  • Assess thrill (palpable vibration) and bruit (auscultated) — indicates patency
  • Do NOT measure BP, draw blood, or start IV in fistula arm
  • Assess for signs of infection, stenosis, thrombosis
  • Elevate arm post-cannulation to reduce bleeding
  • Apply pressure to needle sites 10-15 min after treatment

Complications

Intradialytic Hypotension: Most common complication. Caused by rapid fluid removal, autonomic dysfunction, cardiac issues. Management: Trendelenburg position, reduce ultrafiltration rate, give normal saline bolus, consider albumin. Prevention: Sodium modeling, cool dialysate, assess dry weight accurately.
Muscle Cramps: Caused by rapid fluid/electrolyte shifts. Management: Reduce ultrafiltration, give hypertonic saline or mannitol, massage, stretch.
Disequilibrium Syndrome: Acute cerebral edema from rapid solute removal. Manifestations: Headache, nausea, vomiting, confusion, seizures, coma (during or after dialysis). Prevention: Slow initial dialysis (reduced time, reduced blood flow). Treatment: Mannitol, anticonvulsants, supportive care.
Hemorrhage: From access site or systemic (heparin). Management: Apply direct pressure, reverse heparin with protamine if needed.

Pre-Dialysis Assessment

  • Vital signs (assess for hypotension, fever)
  • Weight (compare to dry weight)
  • Access assessment (thrill, bruit, signs of infection)
  • Review labs (K⁺, Na⁺, BUN, Cr, Hgb, albumin)
  • Assess edema, lung sounds, JVD
NCLEX Trap: Hold antihypertensives before dialysis (risk of hypotension). Check K⁺ before dialysis — if >6.5 or ECG changes, may need urgent dialysis. Heparin is commonly used during HD — assess for bleeding.

Peritoneal Dialysis (PD)

Principles

Diffusion + Osmosis: Dialysate infused into peritoneal cavity. Waste products diffuse from blood (peritoneal capillaries) to dialysate. Fluid removed by osmotic gradient (dextrose in dialysate).

Types:

  • CAPD (Continuous Ambulatory): Manual exchanges 4-5x/day, dwell time 4-6 hours
  • APD (Automated): Cycler machine performs exchanges overnight, last dwell during day

Complications

Peritonitis (most serious):
Manifestations: Cloudy dialysate effluent, abdominal pain, fever, nausea, rebound tenderness
Management:
  • Send effluent for cell count, Gram stain, culture
  • WBC >100 with >50% neutrophils = peritonitis
  • Empiric antibiotics in dialysate (cover gram-positive + gram-negative)
  • Adjust based on culture results
Prevention: Strict aseptic technique, prophylactic antibiotics before invasive procedures (colonoscopy, dental), treat exit site infections promptly.
Exit Site/Tunnel Infection: Erythema, drainage, tenderness at catheter site. Treat with topical + oral antibiotics. May require catheter removal if tunnel infection.
Catheter Malfunction: Outflow failure. Causes: constipation (most common), catheter kinking, omentum wrapping, fibrin clot. Treat constipation first. Heparin flush for fibrin.
Hernia: Increased intra-abdominal pressure. Common at previous surgical sites, umbilicus, inguinal area. May require surgical repair.

PD Nursing Care

  • Assess exit site daily for redness, drainage, tenderness
  • Monitor effluent (should be clear, pale yellow)
  • Weigh daily, monitor BP, assess edema
  • Ensure proper dwell time and drain time
  • Monitor for signs of fluid overload or dehydration
  • Patient education: Aseptic technique, recognizing peritonitis, catheter care
NCLEX Trap: Warm dialysate to body temperature before infusion (prevents abdominal pain, vasodilation). Do NOT use hypertonic (4.25% dextrose) routinely (damages peritoneum). Monitor blood sugar in diabetic patients (dextrose absorption).

Continuous Renal Replacement Therapy (CRRT)

Indications

Hemodynamically unstable patients who cannot tolerate hemodialysis (ICU setting)

  • Acute kidney injury with hemodynamic instability
  • Severe electrolyte disturbances
  • Fluid overload with pulmonary edema
  • Metabolic acidosis
  • Drug overdose (dialyzable toxins)
  • Rhabdomyolysis
Advantages over HD: Slower fluid and solute removal → better hemodynamic tolerance. Continuous → avoids peaks and troughs. Can provide nutritional support without fluid restriction.

Kidney Transplant

Post-Transplant Care

Immediate Post-Op:
  • Monitor urine output (should increase immediately) — diuresis expected
  • Monitor creatinine (should decrease)
  • Assess for bleeding (drain output, hematuria)
  • Monitor BP (hypertension common)
  • Fluids: Match output (may need aggressive hydration)
Immunosuppression:
  • Tacrolimus or cyclosporine: Calcineurin inhibitors. Nephrotoxic, neurotoxic (tremor), hypertension, hyperglycemia. Monitor trough levels.
  • Mycophenolate: Antiproliferative. Bone marrow suppression (CBC monitoring), GI upset.
  • Prednisone: Corticosteroid. Hyperglycemia, weight gain, osteoporosis, infection risk, peptic ulcer.
  • Sirolimus/everolimus: mTOR inhibitors. Hyperlipidemia, delayed wound healing, mouth ulcers.
Rejection:
  • Acute: Fever, oliguria, elevated creatinine, graft tenderness, hypertension. Treat with high-dose steroids or anti-thymocyte globulin.
  • Chronic: Gradual decline in function. No specific treatment.
NCLEX Trap: Transplant patients are immunosuppressed — AVOID live vaccines. Report fever >100.4°F immediately (may be only sign of infection). Monitor for CMV (fever, leukopenia, elevated LFTs) and BK virus (nephropathy). Do NOT give NSAIDs (nephrotoxic + interfere with immunosuppressants).

NGN Case Study: Hemodialysis Access Complication

Client Scenario

Ms. Maria Rodriguez, 54-year-old female, presents to the dialysis clinic for her routine Monday/Wednesday/Friday hemodialysis session. She has ESRD secondary to diabetic nephropathy and has been on HD for 3 years via a left upper arm arteriovenous graft (AVG). She reports her arm has been "swelling more than usual" and the skin over the graft feels "warm and tight." She also reports chills last night but denies fever.

Vital Signs Nurse's Notes Lab Results Provider Orders

Vital Signs & Assessment

  • BP: 158/92 mmHg (pre-dialysis)
  • HR: 96 bpm
  • RR: 18 breaths/min
  • Temp: 37.6°C (99.7°F)
  • Weight: 64.2 kg (dry weight 62.0 kg)
  • Left arm AVG: Erythema, warmth, and tenderness along entire graft course
  • AVG thrill: Palpable but diminished compared to baseline
  • AVG bruit: Audible but high-pitched and discontinuous
  • Edema: Left hand and forearm pitting edema, fingers slightly dusky
  • Access puncture sites: Healing well, no drainage

Nurse's Notes

Patient missed her last dialysis session on Friday because she "didn't feel like coming." She reports poor adherence with fluid restrictions — "I had soup and ice cream over the weekend." She takes amlodipine 10 mg daily, sevelamer 800 mg TID with meals, and epoetin alfa 4,000 units IV TIW. She has a history of AVG stenosis requiring angioplasty 8 months ago.

Lab Results

  • BUN: 78 mg/dL
  • Creatinine: 9.8 mg/dL
  • Potassium: 5.9 mEq/L
  • Phosphorus: 7.2 mg/dL
  • Hgb: 9.8 g/dL
  • Platelets: 198 × 10⁹/L
  • WBC: 11.2 × 10⁹/L
  • INR: 1.1
  • Albumin: 3.2 g/dL
  • Blood cultures: Pending (2 sets drawn)

Provider Orders

  1. Hold hemodialysis until access infection evaluated
  2. Vancomycin 1 g IV loading dose (dose after blood cultures drawn)
  3. Ceftazidime 2 g IV q8h
  4. Doppler ultrasound of AVG
  5. Vascular surgery consult
  6. Transfer to ED for temporary dialysis catheter placement
  7. Strict I&O, daily weights

Clinical Judgment Questions

Question 1 — Recognize Cues: Which findings are MOST concerning for an infected AVG? (Select all that apply)

  1. Erythema and warmth along the graft
  2. Diminished thrill and high-pitched bruit
  3. Left hand edema and dusky fingers
  4. Temperature 37.6°C
  5. Missed dialysis session
  6. AVG stenosis history
Correct: A, B, C, D
Local signs of infection: erythema, warmth, tenderness (A). Diminished thrill + high-pitched/discontinuous bruit (B) suggest graft thrombosis or stenosis — can occur with infection/inflammation. Hand edema and duskiness (C) suggest venous outflow obstruction (stenosis, thrombosis) or steal syndrome. Low-grade fever (D) supports infection. Missed session (E) and history (F) are risk factors but not current signs.

Question 2 — Analyze Cues: The nurse notes the potassium is 5.9 mEq/L and the patient missed her last dialysis. What is the GREATEST immediate risk?

  1. Hypotension during dialysis
  2. Cardiac arrhythmia from hyperkalemia
  3. Pulmonary edema from fluid overload
  4. Metabolic acidosis
Correct: B
K⁺ 5.9 is dangerously elevated. Hyperkalemia can cause life-threatening arrhythmias (peaked T waves → widened QRS → sine wave → VF/arrest). This is the most immediate life threat. While pulmonary edema (C) and acidosis (D) are concerns, they are less immediately dangerous than hyperkalemia. The patient needs emergent dialysis via temporary access.

Question 3 — Prioritize Hypotheses: The provider orders blood cultures before antibiotics. Why are blood cultures drawn BEFORE starting antibiotics?

  1. To determine the exact antibiotic dose needed
  2. To identify the causative organism and ensure appropriate antibiotic selection
  3. To confirm the patient has an infection before starting unnecessary antibiotics
  4. To check antibiotic levels in the blood
Correct: B
Blood cultures identify the specific organism and its antibiotic sensitivities, allowing de-escalation to targeted therapy. In vascular access infections, Staph aureus (including MRSA) and gram-negative organisms are common. Empiric vancomycin + ceftazidime covers both. Cultures should be drawn BEFORE antibiotics to maximize yield — once antibiotics are given, cultures may be falsely negative.

Question 4 — Generate Solutions: The patient asks why she can't just use her AVG for dialysis today. What is the nurse's BEST response?

  1. "Using the infected graft could spread bacteria into your bloodstream and cause sepsis."
  2. "The graft is probably clotted and won't work for dialysis anyway."
  3. "We need to save the graft for later when you're really sick."
  4. "The dialysis machine would be damaged by the infection."
Correct: A
Cannulating an infected vascular access can introduce bacteria directly into the bloodstream, causing bacteremia and sepsis. This is why HD is held when access infection is suspected. The patient needs temporary access (tunnelled catheter or central line) until the infection is treated. The other responses are either inaccurate or don't explain the clinical reasoning.

Question 5 — Take Action: The patient is transferred to the ED for temporary catheter placement. Which assessment finding would require the nurse to intervene IMMEDIATELY during transfer?

  1. Patient reports anxiety about the catheter procedure
  2. Telemetry shows peaked T waves on ECG
  3. Blood pressure increases to 168/98 mmHg
  4. Patient asks for pain medication
Correct: B
Peaked T waves are the first ECG sign of hyperkalemia and can rapidly progress to widened QRS, sine wave, and cardiac arrest. This requires immediate intervention — calcium gluconate to stabilize cardiac membranes, insulin/glucose to shift K⁺ intracellularly, and emergent dialysis. This takes priority over anxiety (A), hypertension (C), or pain (D).

Question 6 — Evaluate Outcomes: Two weeks later, the infection has resolved and the patient returns to dialysis via a new tunneled catheter. Which teaching point is MOST important for preventing future access infections?

  1. "Apply antibiotic ointment to the catheter site daily at home."
  2. "Never touch the catheter dressing or let it get wet."
  3. "Take prophylactic antibiotics before every dialysis session."
  4. "Check your access temperature and appearance daily and report changes immediately."
Correct: D
Patient education and early recognition of infection signs (erythema, warmth, tenderness, fever) are key to preventing access loss. Dressings should be changed by trained staff (B) — patients should NOT manipulate dressings. Antibiotic ointment (A) and prophylactic antibiotics (C) are not standard practice and can promote resistance. Early detection = early treatment = access salvage.
Case Study Learning Points:
  • AVG infection signs: E-W-T-R — Erythema, Warmth, Tenderness, Reduced thrill
  • Never cannulate infected access — Risk of bacteremia and sepsis
  • K⁺ >5.5 = ECG monitoring — Peaked T → widened QRS → sine wave → arrest
  • Cultures BEFORE antibiotics — Maximizes identification of causative organism
  • Hyperkalemia first aid: C-I-K-D — Calcium, Insulin/Glucose, Kayexalate, Dialysis
