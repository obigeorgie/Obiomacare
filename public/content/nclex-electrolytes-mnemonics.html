NCLEX Electrolytes Mnemonics: Memory Aids for Lab Values

Essential memory aids for the most tested electrolyte imbalances on the NCLEX

Sodium (Na⁺) — Normal: 135-145 mEq/L

Hyponatremia (<135 mEq/L)

Mnemonic: SALT LOSS

  • S — Seizures, headache
  • A — Abdominal cramps, Anorexia
  • L — Lethargy, Low muscle tone
  • T — Twitching, Tremors
  • L — Lightheadedness
  • O — Oliguria (late sign)
  • S — Stupor, Seizures (severe)
  • S — Shock (if severe)

Remember: "Sodium follows water." Dilutional hyponatremia = fluid overload. Depletional = fluid loss (vomiting, diarrhea, diuretics).

Hypernatremia (>145 mEq/L)

Mnemonic: SALT OVERLOAD

  • F — Fever, Flushed skin
  • R — Restlessness, Rigidity
  • I — Increased thirst (primary symptom)
  • E — Edema (if hypervolemic)
  • D — Dry mucous membranes

Critical: Correct hyponatremia SLOWLY (<12 mEq/L in 24h). Rapid correction causes osmotic demyelination syndrome (central pontine myelinolysis).

Potassium (K⁺) — Normal: 3.5-5.0 mEq/L

Hypokalemia (<3.5 mEq/L)

Mnemonic: K⁺ LEAVES

  • L — Lethargy, Leg cramps
  • E — EKG changes (U waves, flattened T waves)
  • A — Arrhythmias (PACs, PVCs)
  • V — Vomiting, diarrhea (causes)
  • E — Extreme muscle weakness
  • S — Shallow respirations (respiratory muscle paralysis if severe)

Causes to Remember: Diuretics (loop/thiazide), vomiting, diarrhea, NG suction, insulin, albuterol, steroids.

Hyperkalemia (>5.0 mEq/L)

Mnemonic: MURDER

  • M — Muscle weakness
  • U — Uremia (kidney failure = #1 cause)
  • R — Respiratory paralysis
  • D — Decreased cardiac contractility
  • E — EKG changes (peaked T waves, widened QRS, sine wave)
  • R — Reflexes hyperactive then absent

Critical Action ("K⁺ KILLER"):

  1. C — Calcium gluconate (cardiac membrane stabilization)
  2. I — Insulin + Glucose (shifts K⁺ into cells)
  3. D — Dialysis (definitive if refractory)
  4. K — Kayexalate (sodium polystyrene sulfonate)

Calcium (Ca²⁺) — Normal: 8.5-10.5 mg/dL (ionized: 4.5-5.1 mg/dL)

Hypocalcemia (<8.5 mg/dL)

Mnemonic: CATS

  • C — Convulsions, Chvostek's sign (facial twitch)
  • A — Arrhythmias (prolonged QT)
  • T — Tetany, Trousseau's sign (carpal spasm with BP cuff)
  • S — Seizures, Stridor (laryngospasm)

Hypercalcemia (>10.5 mg/dL)

Mnemonic: STONES, BONES, GROANS, MOANS

  • Stones — Kidney stones
  • Bones — Bone pain, fractures (if malignancy-related)
  • Groans — Abdominal pain, constipation, nausea
  • Moans — Altered mental status, confusion, lethargy

Remember: "Calcium and Phosphorus are inversely related." When one goes up, the other goes down. Check both!

Magnesium (Mg²⁺) — Normal: 1.5-2.5 mEq/L

Hypomagnesemia (<1.5 mEq/L)

Mnemonic: MAGNESIUM DEFICIT

  • M — Muscle weakness, tremors
  • A — Arrhythmias (torsades de pointes!)
  • G — Gait instability
  • N — Nystagmus
  • E — EKG changes (prolonged PR/QT)
  • S — Seizures
  • I — Irritability, insomnia
  • U — Urinary Mg wasting (diuretics)
  • M — Muscle cramps

Key Point: Hypomagnesemia causes hypokalemia and hypocalcemia that DON'T correct until Mg is repleted. Always check Mg with K⁺ and Ca²⁺!

Hypermagnesemia (>2.5 mEq/L)

Mnemonic: MAGNUM (too much!)

  • M — Muscle weakness → paralysis
  • A — Areflexia (absent DTRs)
  • G — GI: nausea, vomiting
  • N — Nausea
  • U — Urinary retention
  • M — Myocardial depression (hypotension, bradycardia)

Phosphorus (P) — Normal: 2.5-4.5 mg/dL

Hypophosphatemia (<2.5 mg/dL)

Mnemonic: 3 P's

  • P — Proximal muscle weakness (respiratory failure risk)
  • P — Paresthesias
  • P — Poor organ function (cardiac, renal, hepatic)

Hyperphosphatemia (>4.5 mg/dL)

Usually asymptomatic; associated with hypocalcemia (inverse relationship). Think kidney failure.

Chloride (Cl⁻) — Normal: 95-105 mEq/L

Usually follows sodium. Remember: "Where Na⁺ goes, Cl⁻ follows."

Key Associations

  • Hyperchloremia: Metabolic acidosis, dehydration, excessive NS infusions
  • Hypochloremia: Vomiting (loss of HCl), metabolic alkalosis, diuretics

NGN Case Study: Electrolyte Emergency

Patient Presentation

Ms. Chen, 68 y/o, is admitted with weakness and palpitations. She takes HCTZ for hypertension and has had diarrhea for 3 days.

  • VS: BP 96/58, HR 112 (irregular), RR 18, Temp 37.0°C, SpO₂ 96%
  • Na⁺: 132 mEq/L | K⁺: 2.8 mEq/L | Ca²⁺: 8.2 mg/dL | Mg²⁺: 1.2 mEq/L
  • EKG: Flattened T waves, occasional PVCs

Which action should the nurse take FIRST?

Correct: B

Rationale: Ms. Chen has severe hypokalemia (2.8 mEq/L) with cardiac manifestations (irregular HR, PVCs). The priority is patient safety — cardiac monitoring detects life-threatening arrhythmias, and IV access is needed for K⁺ replacement. While all interventions may be needed, safety first per the NCSBN framework.

Why not others:

  • A: Oral K⁺ is unsafe with active GI symptoms (diarrhea) and won't act fast enough for this severity
  • C: Calcium gluconate is for hyperkalemia (membrane stabilization), not hypokalemia
  • D: Increasing fluids could worsen hyponatremia and doesn't address the life-threatening K⁺
