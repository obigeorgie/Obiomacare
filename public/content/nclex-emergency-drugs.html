Emergency Drugs for Nurses: NCLEX Quick Reference

Introduction

Emergency drugs are high-yield for the NCLEX. The exam tests indications, contraindications, dosages, and nursing implications. This guide covers the essential emergency medications with the clinical context you need.

Cardiac Emergency Drugs

Epinephrine (Adrenaline)

Uses:

  • Cardiac arrest (ACLS)
  • Anaphylaxis
  • Severe asthma (last resort)
  • Croup (racemic epinephrine)

Dosing:

  • Cardiac arrest: 1 mg IV/IO every 3-5 minutes
  • Anaphylaxis: 0.3-0.5 mg IM (0.3-0.5 mL of 1:1000)
  • IV infusion: 2-10 mcg/min (titrate)

Nursing implications:

  • Causes tachycardia, hypertension, hyperglycemia
  • IV infiltration causes tissue necrosis (use central line if possible)
  • Monitor for ventricular arrhythmias

NCLEX scenario:

"A patient in cardiac arrest has received 2 minutes of CPR and one shock. What is the NEXT medication?"

Answer: Epinephrine 1 mg IV/IO. Follow ACLS algorithm: shock → CPR → epinephrine.

Atropine

Uses:

  • Symptomatic bradycardia (HR < 50 with symptoms)
  • Organophosphate poisoning
  • Preoperative (reduce secretions)

Dosing:

  • Bradycardia: 0.5 mg IV every 3-5 minutes, max 3 mg
  • Organophosphate: 2-4 mg every 5-10 minutes until secretions dry

Nursing implications:

  • Anticholinergic effects: dry mouth, blurred vision, urinary retention, tachycardia
  • Contraindicated: glaucoma, tachyarrhythmias, GI obstruction
  • Minimum effective dose for bradycardia is 0.5 mg (lower doses may paradoxically slow heart)

NCLEX tip: Atropine is NOT for asystole or PEA in ACLS (removed from algorithm).

Adenosine

Uses:

  • Supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) — FIRST LINE

Dosing:

  • 6 mg rapid IV push, followed by 20 mL saline flush
  • If no conversion in 1-2 minutes: 12 mg rapid IV push
  • May repeat 12 mg once

Nursing implications:

  • Must be given RAPID IV push (half-life < 10 seconds)
  • Transient asystole is expected (feels like "heart stopping")
  • Warn patient about chest discomfort, flushing, dyspnea
  • Contraindicated: heart transplant patients (denervated heart), WPW with afib

NCLEX scenario:

"A patient has SVT with HR 180. After vagal maneuvers fail, what is the FIRST medication?"

Answer: Adenosine 6 mg rapid IV push. Then 12 mg if needed.

Amiodarone

Uses:

  • Ventricular tachycardia (VTach) with pulse
  • VFib/pulseless VTach (after epinephrine)
  • Stable wide-complex tachycardia

Dosing:

  • VTach with pulse: 150 mg IV over 10 minutes
  • VFib/VTach arrest: 300 mg IV/IO, then 150 mg
  • Maintenance: 1 mg/min for 6 hours, then 0.5 mg/min

Nursing implications:

  • Hypotension (rate-related, slow infusion)
  • Phlebitis (use central line)
  • QT prolongation (monitor ECG)
  • Pulmonary toxicity (long-term)
  • Thyroid dysfunction (long-term)
  • Hepatotoxicity
  • Blue-gray skin discoloration (long-term)

NCLEX tip: Amiodarone has MANY side effects. Know hypotension and phlebitis for acute use.

Nitroglycerin

Uses:

  • Angina/chest pain
  • Acute coronary syndrome
  • Hypertensive emergency
  • Acute pulmonary edema

Dosing:

  • Sublingual: 0.3-0.4 mg every 5 minutes × 3 doses
  • IV: 5-10 mcg/min, titrate up by 5 mcg/min every 3-5 minutes
  • Paste: 1-2 inches

Nursing implications:

  • Causes hypotension (have patient lie down)
  • Headache is common
  • Tolerance develops with continuous use
  • Contraindications:
  • SBP < 90 mmHg
  • RV infarct (preload dependent)
  • Sildenafil/Viagra use within 24 hours
  • Severe aortic stenosis

NCLEX scenario:

"A patient with chest pain has BP 78/50. Can nitroglycerin be given?"

Answer: NO. SBP < 90 is contraindication. Hypotension will worsen.

Airway/Emergency Drugs

Naloxone (Narcan)

Uses:

  • Opioid overdose/reversal

Dosing:

  • IV/IM/Intranasal: 0.4-2 mg (repeat every 2-3 minutes)
  • May need repeated doses or infusion (long-acting opioids)

Nursing implications:

  • Rapid reversal may cause withdrawal (agitation, vomiting, pain)
  • Duration shorter than many opioids — monitor for re-narcotization
  • Give slowly to avoid severe withdrawal
  • Works on ALL opioids (heroin, fentanyl, morphine, oxycodone)

NCLEX scenario:

"A patient is found unresponsive with RR 6 and pinpoint pupils. What is the FIRST medication?"

Answer: Naloxone. Classic opioid overdose presentation.

Flumazenil (Romazicon)

Uses:

  • Benzodiazepine reversal

Dosing:

  • 0.2 mg IV over 30 seconds, repeat every minute up to 1 mg

Nursing implications:

  • Can precipitate seizures (especially in chronic benzo users or tricyclic overdose)
  • Shorter duration than most benzodiazepines — monitor for re-sedation
  • NOT first-line for sedation reversal (supportive care preferred)

NCLEX tip: Naloxone is used frequently. Flumazenil is rarely used due to seizure risk.

Albuterol

Uses:

  • Bronchospasm, asthma, COPD
  • Hyperkalemia (shifts K+ into cells)

Dosing:

  • Nebulizer: 2.5 mg every 20 minutes × 3, then assess
  • MDI: 90 mcg/puff, 2 puffs every 4-6 hours

Nursing implications:

  • Tremors, tachycardia, anxiety (beta-2 effects)
  • Hypokalemia (shifts K+ into cells)
  • Monitor peak flow and auscultate lungs
  • Spacer improves MDI delivery

Seizure Drugs

Lorazepam (Ativan)

Uses:

  • Status epilepticus
  • Anxiety, agitation
  • Alcohol withdrawal

Dosing:

  • Status epilepticus: 4 mg IV over 2 minutes (may repeat)
  • Alcohol withdrawal: 2-4 mg PO/IM/IV every 4-6 hours

Nursing implications:

  • Respiratory depression (have suction and airway ready)
  • Hypotension
  • Sedation
  • Paradoxical agitation in elderly

Phenytoin (Dilantin)

Uses:

  • Seizure prophylaxis and treatment
  • Status epilepticus (after benzodiazepine)

Dosing:

  • Loading: 15-20 mg/kg IV at max 50 mg/min
  • Maintenance: 5 mg/kg/day divided

Nursing implications:

  • NEVER give IV push — cardiac arrest risk
  • Give slowly (max 50 mg/min) with cardiac monitoring
  • Extravasation causes purple glove syndrome (tissue necrosis)
  • Therapeutic level: 10-20 mcg/mL
  • Fosphenytoin is preferred IV (water-soluble, faster, safer)

NCLEX scenario:

"A patient is receiving phenytoin IV. The nurse notes the patient's heart rate is dropping. What is the FIRST action?"

Answer: Stop the infusion. Phenytoin can cause bradycardia, heart block, and asystole if given too fast.

Dysrhythmia Drugs

Diltiazem (Cardizem)

Uses:

  • Atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response (AFib with RVR)
  • SVT

Dosing:

  • Bolus: 0.25 mg/kg IV over 2 minutes
  • Infusion: 5-15 mg/hr

Nursing implications:

  • Hypotension (most common)
  • Bradycardia, heart block
  • Contraindicated: WPW with afib, wide-complex tachycardia, severe hypotension

Metoprolol (Lopressor)

Uses:

  • Hypertension
  • Tachycardia
  • Post-MI (reduce mortality)

Dosing:

  • IV: 5 mg every 5 minutes × 3

Nursing implications:

  • Bradycardia, hypotension
  • Bronchospasm (caution with asthma/COPD)
  • Mask hypoglycemia symptoms
  • DON'T give if HR < 60, SBP < 100, or acute heart failure

Antidotes

Opioids Naloxone May need repeat doses
Benzodiazepines Flumazenil Seizure risk
Acetaminophen N-acetylcysteine (NAC) Best within 8 hours
Warfarin Vitamin K, FFP, PCC Vitamin K takes hours
Heparin Protamine 1 mg per 100 units heparin
Organophosphates Atropine, pralidoxime Dry secretions = atropine effective
Beta-blockers Glucagon High-dose insulin + glucose also used
Calcium channel blockers Calcium, glucagon, high-dose insulin Multi-modal
Digoxin Digoxin immune Fab (Digibind) For severe toxicity
Methanol/ethylene glycol Fomepizole, ethanol Prevent metabolism
Cyanide Hydroxocobalamin, sodium thiosulfate Smoke inhalation risk
Iron Deferoxamine Chelation
Lead Succimer, EDTA Chelation
Methemoglobinemia Methylene blue Don't use in G6PD deficiency

NCLEX tip: Know acetaminophen → NAC and opioids → naloxone cold. These are the most commonly tested.

ACLS Algorithm Quick Reference

Cardiac Arrest (VFib/VTach)

  1. Start CPR
  2. Give oxygen
  3. Attach monitor/defibrillator
  4. Shockable rhythm?
  • YES (VFib/VTach): Shock → CPR 2 min → Epinephrine → CPR 2 min → Shock → Amiodarone
  • NO (PEA/Asystole): CPR 2 min → Epinephrine → Consider H's and T's

H's and T's (Reversible Causes):

  • Hypovolemia
  • Hypoxia
  • Hydrogen ion (acidosis)
  • Hypo/Hyperkalemia
  • Hypothermia
  • Tension pneumothorax
  • Tamponade (cardiac)
  • Toxins
  • Thrombosis (pulmonary)
  • Thrombosis (coronary)

Bradycardia

  1. Assess for symptoms (hypotension, altered LOC, chest pain, shock)
  2. If symptomatic: Atropine 0.5 mg IV
  3. If ineffective: Transcutaneous pacing or dopamine/epinephrine infusion
  4. Consider underlying cause

Tachycardia

Stable:

  1. Vagal maneuvers
  2. Adenosine (if regular narrow-complex)
  3. Cardizem or beta-blocker (if narrow-complex)
  4. Amiodarone (if wide-complex)

Unstable (hypotension, altered LOC, shock):

  1. Immediate synchronized cardioversion
  2. Sedation if time permits

NCLEX Practice Questions

Question 1:

"A patient with SVT has failed vagal maneuvers. What is the FIRST medication?"

A) Amiodarone
B) Adenosine
C) Cardizem
D) Metoprolol

Answer: B — Adenosine is first-line for SVT. Give 6 mg rapid IV push.

Question 2:

"A patient is receiving phenytoin IV for seizures. The nurse should monitor for which adverse effect?"

A) Hypertension
B) Bradycardia
C) Hyperglycemia
D) Diarrhea

Answer: B — Phenytoin can cause bradycardia, heart block, and asystole if given too fast. Always use cardiac monitor.

Question 3:

"A patient with chest pain has BP 86/52. Which medication is CONTRAINDICATED?"

A) Aspirin
B) Morphine
C) Nitroglycerin
D) Metoprolol

Answer: C — Nitroglycerin causes vasodilation and further hypotension. Contraindicated if SBP < 90.

Question 4:

"A patient is found with pinpoint pupils and respiratory rate of 6. What is the antidote?"

A) Flumazenil
B) Naloxone
C) Atropine
D) Narcan

Answer: B or D — Both are correct. Naloxone is the generic, Narcan is the brand name. Pinpoint pupils + respiratory depression = opioid overdose.

Summary

Emergency drugs require rapid recognition and appropriate intervention.

High-yield for NCLEX:

  1. Epinephrine — cardiac arrest, anaphylaxis
  2. Adenosine — SVT (first line)
  3. Atropine — symptomatic bradycardia
  4. Naloxone — opioid overdose
  5. Nitroglycerin — angina (know contraindications)
  6. Amiodarone — VTach/VFib
  7. ACLS algorithm — know the flow

Practice the scenarios until medication selection is automatic. The NCLEX tests clinical judgment, not just recall.
