NCLEX Endocrine Disorders: Complete Study Guide

Diabetes, thyroid disorders, adrenal disorders, SIADH, and DI — pathophysiology, nursing priorities, and NCLEX traps.

1. Diabetes Mellitus

Type 1 vs. Type 2 Diabetes

FeatureType 1Type 2
OnsetAbrupt, usually childhood/adolescenceGradual, usually adulthood
PathophysiologyAutoimmune destruction of beta cells → absolute insulin deficiencyInsulin resistance + relative deficiency
Body habitusUsually thinUsually overweight/obese
KetosisProne to DKAResistant (HHS instead)
TreatmentInsulin requiredLifestyle, oral agents, may need insulin
Diabetes Nursing Priorities:
• Blood glucose monitoring (pre-meal, bedtime, PRN)
• Foot care inspection daily
• Hypoglycemia recognition and treatment (15-15 rule)
• Sick day management (never stop insulin!)
• HbA1c goal: <7% (individualized)
• Prevent complications: eye, kidney, nerve, cardiovascular

2. Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA) vs. Hyperosmolar Hyperglycemic State (HHS)

DKA vs. HHS Comparison

FeatureDKAHHS
Blood glucose>250 mg/dL>600 mg/dL
Plasma osmolarityVariable>320 mOsm/kg
KetonesPresent (metabolic acidosis)Absent/minimal
pH<7.30>7.30
HCO₃<18 mEq/L>18 mEq/L
OnsetRapid (hours)Gradual (days)
Mental statusAlert to stuporousStupor to coma
TypeType 1Type 2
DKA/HHS Nursing Priorities:
• Aggressive IV fluids (NS first, then ½ NS when glucose <250-300)
• Regular insulin IV drip (start AFTER fluid resuscitation)
• Potassium replacement (insulin drives K+ into cells)
• Monitor glucose hourly — add dextrose when glucose <250 to prevent hypoglycemia while clearing ketones
• Bicarbonate only if pH <6.9
• Treat underlying cause (infection most common)
NCLEX Trap: In DKA, potassium levels may appear NORMAL or HIGH initially (acidosis shifts K+ out of cells), but TOTAL body K+ is LOW. Once insulin is given, K+ drops rapidly. ALWAYS check and replace potassium before starting insulin if K+ <3.3!

3. Hypothyroidism vs. Hyperthyroidism

Thyroid Disorders Comparison

FeatureHypothyroidismHyperthyroidism

Hypothyroidism — "LOW & SLOW"
Everything is decreased: metabolism, HR, temperature, GI motility, energy
NCLEX Trap: Thyroid storm is a LIFE-THREATENING emergency. Signs: fever >104°F, severe tachycardia, hypertension, delirium, vomiting, diarrhea. Give: PTU/methimazole FIRST (blocks synthesis), then iodine (blocks release — given 1 hour AFTER antithyroid drug), propranolol (blocks adrenergic effects), cooling blankets, IV fluids.

NCLEX Trap: Addisonian crisis = adrenal insufficiency emergency. Signs: severe hypotension, shock, vomiting, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia. Give: hydrocortisone IV IMMEDIATELY, aggressive fluids, treat electrolyte imbalances. NEVER stop steroids abruptly — taper gradually to prevent Addisonian crisis.

Remember: SIADH = "SWELLING" (fluid retention, dilutional hyponatremia)
DI = "DRYING" (fluid loss, concentrated serum, hypernatremia)
NCLEX Trap: In SIADH, NEVER correct sodium rapidly (>8-12 mEq/L in 24h). Can cause osmotic demyelination syndrome (central pontine myelinolysis) — irreversible brain damage. In DI, replace fluids but don't restrict — they're losing too much free water.

NGN Case Study: Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)

Client Scenario

James, 19-year-old male, is brought to the ED by his roommate. He has been vomiting for 24 hours and is "breathing funny." He was diagnosed with Type 1 diabetes at age 12 but has been "too busy with college" to check his blood sugar or take insulin regularly.

Vital Signs Physical Assessment Labs

Vital Signs: BP 92/58, HR 128, RR 32 (deep, labored), Temp 37.0°C

Physical Assessment: Lethargic but arousable. Kussmaul respirations (deep, rapid). Fruity breath odor. Dry mucous membranes. Skin turgor poor. Capillary refill 4 seconds. Abdomen soft, diffuse tenderness. No focal neurological deficits.

Labs: Glucose 486 mg/dL, pH 7.18, PaCO₂ 18, HCO₃ 8, Ketones large (serum), Na 132, K 5.8, BUN 28, Creatinine 1.4

Question 1 — Recognize Cues: Which clinical finding is the earliest indicator that DKA is developing?

  • A. Blood glucose >400 mg/dL
  • B. Positive urine ketones
  • C. Kussmaul respirations
  • D. Decreased level of consciousness

Correct: B. Positive urine ketones

Ketone production begins when insulin is insufficient, BEFORE severe hyperglycemia or metabolic acidosis develops. A patient can have positive ketones with a glucose of 250 mg/dL (euglycemic DKA). Kussmaul respirations (C) and altered LOC (D) are late signs of severe acidosis. The earliest detectable sign is ketonuria or ketonemia, which is why checking ketones when glucose is >250 or during illness is critical for Type 1 diabetics.

Question 2 — Analyze Cues: The provider orders: NS 1L bolus, regular insulin 0.1 units/kg IV bolus then 0.1 units/kg/hr continuous infusion. The patient's K⁺ is 5.8 mEq/L. Which nursing action is highest priority?

  • A. Hold insulin until K⁺ is below 5.5 mEq/L
  • B. Start insulin but ensure potassium is checked hourly
  • C. Administer Kayexalate to lower potassium first
  • D. Give insulin with dextrose to prevent hypoglycemia

Correct: B. Start insulin but ensure potassium is checked hourly

Insulin drives K⁺ into cells, which can cause life-threatening hypokalemia. However, the priority is treating the DKA — insulin should not be delayed. K⁺ of 5.8 is elevated but not an absolute contraindication. The nurse should start insulin AND monitor K⁺ closely (hourly). K⁺ replacement is typically started when K⁺ drops below 5.2 or 5.0 (protocol-dependent). Kayexalate (C) is inappropriate — K⁺ will drop with insulin. Dextrose (D) is added later when glucose approaches 200.

Question 3 — Prioritize Hypotheses: Two hours after starting insulin, the patient's glucose is 280 mg/dL and K⁺ is 3.2 mEq/L. Which intervention is most appropriate?

  • A. Continue current insulin rate — glucose is responding
  • B. Add dextrose to IV fluids and increase potassium replacement
  • C. Stop insulin to prevent further glucose drop
  • D. Give IV push D50 to rapidly raise glucose

Correct: B. Add dextrose to IV fluids and increase potassium replacement

When glucose approaches 200-250 in DKA, dextrose (D5 or D10) is added to prevent hypoglycemia while continuing insulin to clear ketones. Insulin should NOT be stopped (C) — the goal is ketone clearance, not just glucose reduction. K⁺ of 3.2 is dangerously low and requires aggressive replacement (40 mEq/L in fluids). D50 (D) would spike glucose and is unnecessary with dextrose-containing fluids.

Question 4 — Generate Solutions: The patient's pH has improved to 7.30, HCO₃ is 16, and anion gap is 14. He is awake and alert. Which criterion indicates DKA has resolved?

  • A. Glucose <200 mg/dL
  • B. pH >7.30 and HCO₃ >18
  • C. Anion gap ≤12
  • D. All of the above

Correct: D. All of the above

DKA resolution criteria: glucose <200, pH >7.30, HCO₃ >18, AND anion gap ≤12. The anion gap is the most important — it measures ketone clearance. A patient can have normal glucose and pH but still have an elevated anion gap, meaning ketosis persists. Insulin infusion should continue until ALL criteria are met, then transition to subcutaneous insulin with overlap.

Question 5 — Take Action: The patient is being transitioned from IV to subcutaneous insulin. Which statement is correct?

  • A. Stop IV insulin when the first SC dose is given
  • B. Continue IV insulin for 1-2 hours after SC insulin is started
  • C. Switch to oral hypoglycemic agents since his glucose is controlled
  • D. Discharge on a sliding scale insulin regimen only

Correct: B. Continue IV insulin for 1-2 hours after SC insulin is started

There must be an overlap period because SC insulin has a delayed onset. Stopping IV insulin immediately (A) risks rebound hyperglycemia. Oral agents (C) are never used in Type 1 diabetes — this patient requires lifelong insulin. Sliding scale only (D) is inadequate — basal-bolus insulin is the standard. The patient needs diabetes education and a sustainable insulin regimen.

Question 6 — Evaluate Outcomes: At discharge, which instruction is highest priority for preventing recurrence?

  • A. "Check your blood sugar 4 times daily"
  • B. "Never skip insulin, even when you're sick"
  • C. "Follow a strict low-carbohydrate diet"
  • D. "Keep a glucagon kit at home"

Correct: B. "Never skip insulin, even when you're sick"

Insulin omission during illness is the most common precipitant of DKA. The "sick day rules" include: NEVER stop insulin (may need MORE insulin during illness), check glucose every 2-4 hours, check ketones if glucose >250, stay hydrated, and seek medical care if unable to keep fluids down. While frequent glucose checks (A) are important, adherence to insulin (B) is the critical factor. Low-carb diet (C) is not the priority. Glucagon (D) is for hypoglycemia, not DKA prevention.
