Fluids & Electrolytes Deep Dive

The #1 topic that fails NCLEX candidates. Master sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and phosphorus with clinical judgment.

💡 Remember This Framework: "Sodium moves water. Potassium stops the heart. Calcium clenches. Magnesium mellows. Phosphorus powers cells." Every electrolyte has a primary clinical action. Know it cold.

🌊 Fluid Compartments & Movement

Body Fluid Distribution

  • Total body water: ~60% body weight (varies by age, body fat, gender)
  • Intracellular fluid (ICF): 2/3 of TBW — potassium is the major cation
  • Extracellular fluid (ECF): 1/3 of TBW — sodium is the major cation
  • Interstitial fluid: 3/4 of ECF
  • Intravascular fluid (plasma): 1/4 of ECF — this is what you measure in labs

Fluid Movement Rules

Water follows sodium. Where Na⁺ goes, water follows (osmosis). This is the foundation of everything.

  • Hypernatremia (high Na⁺) → water shifts OUT of cells → cellular dehydration → cells shrink
  • Hyponatremia (low Na⁺) → water shifts INTO cells → cellular edema → cells swell (dangerous in brain)
  • Hyperglycemia → glucose stays in ECF → draws water from ICF → pseudo-hyponatremia (dilutional)
NCLEX Trap: Never correct sodium faster than 0.5 mEq/L/hr (or 8-12 mEq/L per 24 hrs). Faster correction causes osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS) — central pontine myelinolysis. This is a fatal trap question.

🧂 Sodium (Na⁺) — 136-145 mEq/L

Sodium is the osmotic regulator. It controls water distribution between compartments.

Hyponatremia (Na⁺ <136)

Manifestations (think: cerebral edema):
  • Headache, confusion, lethargy, seizures, coma
  • Nausea, vomiting, muscle cramps, weakness
  • Can progress to brainstem herniation if severe

  2. Seizure precautions for Na⁺ <120
  3. Fluid restriction for dilutional hyponatremia (document I&O meticulously)
  4. 3% hypertonic saline only for severe symptoms — via central line with frequent Na⁺ checks
  5. Raise Na⁺ by NO MORE than 0.5 mEq/L/hr or 8-12 mEq/L/day

Hypernatremia (Na⁺ >145)

Manifestations (think: cellular dehydration):
  • Thirst (strongest early sign), dry/sticky mucous membranes
  • Restlessness, irritability, seizures, coma
  • Fever, flushed skin, muscle twitching

Common causes: Inadequate water intake (elderly, comatose), diabetes insipidus, excessive sweating, osmotic diuresis (mannitol, hyperglycemia), sodium overload (hypertonic saline, sodium bicarbonate)

Priority Nursing Actions:
  1. Free water replacement: D5W or 0.45% NS to replace free water deficit
  2. Calculate deficit: Free water deficit = 0.6 × weight(kg) × (current Na/140 − 1)
  3. Replace slowly — rapid correction causes cerebral edema (just like hyponatremia)
  4. Diabetes insipidus: give DDAVP (desmopressin) if central DI
🧠 Mnemonic — Sodium: "Sodium pulls water. Low = swollen brain (water IN). High = shriveled brain (water OUT)." Both can seize. Both correct slowly.

⚡ Potassium (K⁺) — 3.5-5.0 mEq/L

Potassium is the electrical conductor. It determines cardiac rhythm, muscle contraction, and nerve transmission. This is the most tested electrolyte on NCLEX.

Hypokalemia (K⁺ <3.5)

Manifestations:
  • Cardiac: Palpitations, irregular heartbeat, orthostatic hypotension
  • Muscle: Weakness, fatigue, cramps, constipation/ileus (smooth muscle), shallow respirations (diaphragm)
  • Metabolic: Alkalosis (H⁺ moves out of cells, K⁺ moves in), glucose intolerance
🫀 ECG Changes (LEARN THESE):
  • Flattened or inverted T waves (earliest sign)
  • Prominent U waves
  • ST segment depression
  • Premature ventricular contractions (PVCs)
  • Can progress to ventricular tachycardia/fibrillation

Common causes: Diuretics (loop & thiazides — #1 cause), vomiting/diarrhea/NG suction, insulin administration (drives K⁺ into cells), albuterol, corticosteroids, metabolic alkalosis, refeeding syndrome

Priority Nursing Actions:
  1. Never give IV push potassium. Ever. Max infusion rate: 10 mEq/hr via peripheral line, 20 mEq/hr via central line with cardiac monitoring.
  2. Oral replacement preferred if patient can take PO. Foods: bananas, oranges, potatoes, spinach, dried fruits.
  3. Monitor ECG continuously during IV replacement.
  4. Check Mg²⁺ — hypomagnesemia causes refractory hypokalemia (can't fix K⁺ until Mg²⁺ is fixed).
  5. Assess bowel sounds — ileus is a sign.
NCLEX Trap: A patient on digoxin with hypokalemia is at high risk for digoxin toxicity. Digoxin competes with K⁺ for binding sites — low K⁺ means more digoxin binds to cardiac tissue. Watch for nausea, vomiting, vision changes (yellow/green halos), arrhythmias.

Hyperkalemia (K⁺ >5.0)

Manifestations:
  • Cardiac: Palpitations, chest pain, bradycardia progressing to asystole
  • Muscle: Weakness, ascending paralysis, paresthesias
  • GI: Nausea, diarrhea, abdominal cramping
🫀 ECG Changes (LEARN THESE — LIFE THREATENING):
  • Tall, peaked T waves (earliest sign — "tented T waves")
  • Prolonged PR interval
  • Flattened/absent P waves
  • Widened QRS complex
  • Sine wave pattern (pre-arrest — prepare to code)
  • Ventricular fibrillation → asystole

Common causes: Renal failure (#1), ACE inhibitors, ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics (spironolactone), NSAIDs, cell lysis (burns, crush injuries, chemotherapy), metabolic acidosis (H⁺ moves into cells, K⁺ moves out), excessive IV potassium

Priority Nursing Actions — Hyperkalemia Treatment (KNOW THE ORDER):
  1. Calcium gluconate 10% (1 g IV over 2-3 min) — cardioprotective. Stabilizes cardiac membrane. Works in 1-3 minutes. Does NOT lower K⁺, just protects the heart.
  2. Regular insulin 10 units IV + D50 (50% dextrose) 1 amp — drives K⁺ into cells. Works in 15-30 minutes. Monitor glucose closely.
  3. Albuterol nebulizer — drives K⁺ into cells. Works in 15-90 minutes.
  4. Sodium bicarbonate — for acidotic patients. Drives K⁺ into cells. Works in 15-30 minutes.
  5. Kayexalate (sodium polystyrene sulfonate) — binds K⁺ in gut, eliminates it. Works in 2-6 hours. Monitor for sodium overload and constipation.
  6. Dialysis — definitive treatment for severe/refractory cases. Removes K⁺ directly.
NCLEX Trap: Calcium gluconate is given for cardiac protection, not to lower potassium. The question may ask "What is the priority intervention for hyperkalemia with ECG changes?" — the answer is calcium gluconate, NOT insulin. Insulin lowers K⁺ but takes 15-30 min. Calcium works immediately to prevent arrest.
🧠 Mnemonic — Potassium ECGs: "HYPO = Flat T, U wave, ST down. HYPER = Tall T, wide QRS, no P, sine wave = code."

🦴 Calcium (Ca²⁺) — 8.5-10.5 mg/dL (ionized: 4.5-5.3 mg/dL)

Calcium is the muscle contractor. It controls skeletal muscle contraction, cardiac contractility, nerve transmission, and blood clotting.

Hypocalcemia (Ca²⁺ <8.5)

Manifestations (think: hyperexcitability):
  • Trousseau's sign: Carpopedal spasm with BP cuff inflation (occlude brachial artery for 3 min)
  • Chvostek's sign: Facial twitch when tapping facial nerve
  • Paresthesias (perioral, fingers, toes), muscle cramps, tetany, seizures
  • Prolonged QT interval on ECG → risk for torsades de pointes
  • Laryngospasm (airway emergency)

Common causes: Hypoparathyroidism (post-thyroidectomy), vitamin D deficiency, chronic kidney disease, massive transfusion (citrate binds calcium), pancreatitis, alkalosis, loop diuretics

Priority Nursing Actions:
  1. Airway first. Laryngospasm can occlude airway. Have emergency equipment at bedside.
  2. IV calcium gluconate or calcium chloride (slower than K⁺ treatment — give over 10 min)
  3. Monitor ECG during replacement
  4. Ensure adequate vitamin D (needed for calcium absorption)
  5. Check phosphate levels — high phosphate binds calcium

Hypercalcemia (Ca²⁺ >10.5)

Manifestations (think: "stones, bones, groans, moans, thrones, tones"):
  • Stones: Kidney stones, nephrocalcinosis
  • Bones: Bone pain, fractures, osteoporosis
  • Groans: Constipation, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, pancreatitis
  • Moans: Fatigue, depression, confusion, coma
  • Thrones: Polyuria (nephrogenic DI), dehydration
  • Tones: Weak muscle tone, decreased DTRs, cardiac arrest (shortened QT)

Common causes: Hyperparathyroidism (#1), malignancy (bone metastases, PTHrP), prolonged immobilization, thiazide diuretics, vitamin D toxicity, milk-alkali syndrome

Priority Nursing Actions:
  1. Hydration: 0.9% NS aggressively (3-4 L/day if cardiac status allows) — dilutes calcium and promotes renal excretion
  2. Loop diuretics (furosemide) AFTER hydration — increases calcium excretion
  3. Bisphosphonates (zoledronic acid) — for malignancy-related hypercalcemia. Takes 2-3 days to work.
  4. Calcitonin — rapid onset (hours), short duration. Used for severe hypercalcemia.
  5. Mobilize patient (if immobilized)
  6. Avoid thiazide diuretics, calcium supplements, vitamin D
NCLEX Trap: The #1 nursing priority for hypercalcemia is 0.9% NS hydration. Do NOT give furosemide first — it causes volume depletion which worsens hypercalcemia. Hydrate first, then diurese.

🧘 Magnesium (Mg²⁺) — 1.5-2.5 mg/dL

Magnesium is the natural calcium channel blocker. It calms everything — nerves, muscles, heart.

Hypomagnesemia (Mg²⁺ <1.5)

Manifestations:
  • Tremors, muscle cramps, tetany, seizures
  • Positive Trousseau's and Chvostek's (just like hypocalcemia)
  • Arrhythmias: VT, torsades de pointes
  • Confusion, disorientation

Common causes: Alcoholism (#1), prolonged NG suction, diarrhea, malabsorption, diuretics (loop & thiazides), aminoglycosides, cisplatin, DKA treatment, refeeding syndrome

CRITICAL NCLEX Trap: Hypomagnesemia causes refractory hypokalemia and hypocalcemia. If a patient's K⁺ won't rise despite replacement, check Mg²⁺. You cannot fix K⁺ or Ca²⁺ until Mg²⁺ is normalized.
Priority Nursing Actions:
  1. IV magnesium sulfate 1-2 g over 15 min for severe (cardiac symptoms, seizures)
  2. Monitor DTRs (deep tendon reflexes) — if absent, stop magnesium (toxicity risk)
  3. Monitor BP (hypotension is side effect)
  4. Check K⁺ and Ca²⁺ — replace all three if needed

Hypermagnesemia (Mg²⁺ >2.5)

Manifestations (think: everything slows down):
  • Hypotension, bradycardia (Mg²⁺ is a vasodilator and cardiac depressant)
  • Flushing, warmth
  • Decreased DTRs → absent DTRs → paralysis
  • Drowsiness → respiratory depression → coma
  • Complete heart block, cardiac arrest

Common causes: Renal failure (#1), excessive antacid/laxative use (Mg²⁺-containing), magnesium sulfate overdose (preeclampsia treatment)

Priority Nursing Actions:
  1. Stop all magnesium-containing medications
  2. Calcium gluconate — antidote for magnesium toxicity
  3. Hemodialysis if renal failure
  4. Monitor DTRs, respiratory rate, cardiac rhythm

🔋 Phosphorus (Phos) — 2.5-4.5 mg/dL

Phosphorus is the energy currency. It's essential for ATP, cellular metabolism, and bone mineralization.

Hypophosphatemia (Phos <2.5)

Manifestations:
  • Muscle weakness, rhabdomyolysis
  • Respiratory failure (diaphragm weakness)
  • Cardiac failure, hypotension
  • Confusion, seizures, coma
  • Hemolysis (RBCs need phosphate for membrane integrity)

Common causes: Refeeding syndrome (#1 — when malnourished patients get food, insulin drives phosphate into cells), alcoholism, DKA treatment, phosphate-binding antacids, hyperparathyroidism

NCLEX Trap: Refeeding syndrome can kill. When a malnourished patient (anorexia, chronic alcoholism, prolonged NPO) starts eating, insulin surges and drives phosphate, potassium, and magnesium into cells. This causes catastrophic hypophosphatemia, hypokalemia, and hypomagnesemia. Monitor these electrolytes closely during refeeding.

Hyperphosphatemia (Phos >4.5)

Manifestations: Most symptoms are from hypocalcemia (calcium and phosphate have an inverse relationship). High phosphate binds calcium.

Common causes: Renal failure (#1), excessive phosphate intake (laxatives, enemas), tumor lysis syndrome, hypoparathyroidism

Treatment: Phosphate binders (calcium acetate, sevelamer) with meals, restrict dietary phosphate, dialysis

🎯 Clinical Judgment Case Studies

NCLEX-Style High Acuity

Case 1: Post-Operative Thyroidectomy

A 42-year-old female is 24 hours post total thyroidectomy for papillary carcinoma. She reports perioral tingling and muscle cramps. You check her calcium: 7.2 mg/dL.

What is your priority action?

  1. Call the provider for IV calcium orders
  2. Assess for Trousseau's and Chvostek's signs
  3. Check airway patency and have emergency equipment ready
  4. Administer oral calcium supplements
Answer: 3 — Check airway patency and have emergency equipment ready.

Hypocalcemia post-thyroidectomy (parathyroid damage) can cause laryngospasm and airway obstruction. While all options are appropriate, the ABCs take priority. Tetany can progress to laryngospasm → airway compromise. Once airway is secure, then IV calcium gluconate.
NCLEX-Style Critical Care

Case 2: DKA Treatment

A 19-year-old with T1DM is in DKA. Initial labs: glucose 720, K⁺ 3.1, pH 7.15. The provider orders IV insulin drip and 0.9% NS. You start the infusion.

What is your priority nursing action in the first hour?

  1. Monitor for hypoglycemia as insulin lowers glucose
  2. Replace potassium immediately — the patient is hypokalemic
  3. Assess hourly glucose and prepare to add dextrose when needed
  4. Check ECG and obtain cardiac monitoring
Answer: 4 — Check ECG and obtain cardiac monitoring.

K⁺ of 3.1 with metabolic acidosis puts this patient at high risk for cardiac arrhythmias. Insulin will drive K⁺ even lower (into cells), making this worse. Continuous cardiac monitoring is essential before starting insulin. Yes, potassium replacement is critical too, but cardiac monitoring is the safety-first action.
NCLEX-Style Priority

Case 3: Hyperkalemia with ECG Changes

A patient with ESRD missed dialysis. Vital signs: BP 88/52, HR 48. Labs: K⁺ 7.2. ECG shows peaked T waves, widened QRS, absent P waves.

What is the FIRST medication to administer?

  1. Regular insulin 10 units IV with D50
  2. Sodium polystyrene sulfonate (Kayexalate) PO
  3. Calcium gluconate 1g IV
  4. Sodium bicarbonate IV
Answer: 3 — Calcium gluconate 1g IV.

The ECG shows life-threatening hyperkalemia (widened QRS + absent P waves = impending cardiac arrest). Calcium gluconate works in 1-3 minutes to stabilize the cardiac membrane. It does NOT lower potassium — it buys time. Insulin + D50 is next (shifts K⁺ into cells). Kayexalate and dialysis remove K⁺ but take hours.

🧠 SATA Practice Questions

SATA 1: A nurse is caring for a client with a serum potassium of 6.8 mEq/L. Which actions are appropriate? (Select all that apply.)
  1. Prepare to administer calcium gluconate IV
  2. Place the client on a cardiac monitor
  3. Administer spironolactone as ordered
  4. Prepare insulin and dextrose per protocol
  5. Restrict potassium-containing foods
  6. Encourage the client to drink orange juice
Correct: A, B, D, E

A: Yes — calcium gluconate stabilizes cardiac membranes.
B: Yes — continuous monitoring for lethal arrhythmias.
C: NO — spironolactone is potassium-sparing. Would worsen hyperkalemia.
D: Yes — insulin + dextrose shifts K⁺ into cells.
E: Yes — restrict bananas, oranges, potatoes, etc.
F: NO — orange juice is high in potassium. Would worsen the problem.
SATA 2: A client with heart failure is receiving furosemide 80mg IV BID. Which findings indicate the client may have an electrolyte imbalance? (Select all that apply.)
  1. Muscle weakness
  2. Positive Trousseau's sign
  3. Peaked T waves on ECG
  4. Flattened T waves on ECG
  5. Constipation
  6. Restlessness and irritability
Correct: A, B, D, F

Furosemide causes loss of Na⁺, K⁺, Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, and water.

A: Yes — hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia cause weakness.
B: Yes — hypocalcemia (from loop diuretic) causes Trousseau's.
C: NO — peaked T waves are hyperkalemia. Furosemide causes hypokalemia.
D: Yes — flattened T waves are hypokalemia.
E: NO — constipation is hypercalcemia. Furosemide causes hypocalcemia (diarrhea more likely).
F: Yes — hyponatremia causes restlessness and irritability.
SATA 3: A nurse is caring for a client receiving 3% saline for severe symptomatic hyponatremia. Which nursing actions are appropriate? (Select all that apply.)
  1. Monitor sodium levels every 2 hours
  2. Assess neurologic status frequently
  3. Increase the infusion rate if sodium rises slowly
  4. Report a sodium rise of 2 mEq/L per hour to the provider immediately
  5. Administer via a central venous line
  6. Restrict oral fluid intake
Correct: A, B, D, E, F

A: Yes — frequent monitoring prevents rapid correction.
B: Yes — neuro checks catch cerebral edema or osmotic demyelination.
C: NO — you never increase the rate. Max rise is 0.5 mEq/L/hr. Faster causes ODS.
D: Yes — 2 mEq/L/hr is TOO FAST. Report immediately and slow/stop infusion.
E: Yes — 3% saline is hypertonic and vesicant. Must use central line.
F: Yes — fluid restriction is part of treatment for dilutional hyponatremia.
