GI Surgery, Ostomies & Bariatric Procedures

Ostomy types, bariatric surgeries, stoma care, and postoperative nursing management for the NCLEX.

Ostomy Types

Stoma Assessment — Normal vs. Abnormal

Normal stoma: Beefy red, moist, protrudes 1–2 cm above skin level (colostomy/ileostomy), no bleeding beyond minimal

Abnormal:

  • Pale/blue/black: Ischemia — EMERGENCY, may require revision
  • Retracted below skin level: Stoma retraction — risk of leakage/skin breakdown
  • Prolapse: Stoma telescopes outward — may reduce manually, surgery if severe
  • Stenosis: Narrowed opening — dilate as ordered, may need revision
  • Peristomal skin breakdown: From leakage, allergic reaction, infection
Mnemonic: "SLIDE" — Stoma should be beefy red, Location appropriate, Intact peristomal skin, Dry pouch (no leaks), Edges sealed
Ileostomy Nursing Priority: High risk for fluid/electrolyte imbalance (1,000–1,500 mL/day fluid loss). Monitor for dehydration, sodium depletion, metabolic acidosis (loss of bicarbonate-rich fluid). Empty pouch when 1/3–1/2 full. Cut skin barrier 1/8 inch larger than stoma.
NCLEX Trap: Ileostomy output contains digestive enzymes (lipase, trypsin) that can severely damage skin. Skin barrier is ESSENTIAL. Also, patients with ileostomies are at risk for food blockages — educate about chewing thoroughly, avoiding high-fiber foods initially, and recognizing blockage signs (no output, cramping, abdominal distention, nausea).

ProcedureMechanismKey Nursing Considerations
Roux-en-Y Gastric Bypass (RYGB)Small gastric pouch + Roux limb bypasses duodenum/proximal jejunum. Restrictive + malabsorptiveDumping syndrome risk. Malabsorption of B12, iron, calcium, folate, fat-soluble vitamins. Anastomotic leak risk. Stricture risk.

Adjustable Gastric Band (LAGB)Silicone band around proximal stomach with subcutaneous port. RestrictiveBand adjustments via port. Risk of band slippage, erosion, port infection, esophageal dilation. Less common now due to complications.
Biliopancreatic Diversion with Duodenal Switch (BPD-DS)Sleeve + intestinal bypass. Most malabsorptiveHighest nutritional deficiency risk. Protein malnutrition, fat-soluble vitamin deficiency. Most effective for weight loss but most complex.

Dumping Syndrome

Pathophysiology: Rapid gastric emptying of hyperosmolar food into small intestine → fluid shifts into bowel lumen → hypotension, GI symptoms

Early dumping (15–30 min post-meal):

  • GI: Nausea, cramping, diarrhea, bloating
  • Vasomotor: Flushing, palpitations, tachycardia, hypotension, diaphoresis

Late dumping (1–3 hours post-meal):

  • Reactive hypoglycemia from excessive insulin release
  • Sweating, tremors, confusion, weakness

Prevention/Management: Small, frequent meals. Avoid simple sugars. Combine protein/fat with carbs. Eat dry foods first. Lie down 30 min after meals. Avoid drinking with meals.

Post-Bariatric Nursing Priority: Early ambulation. Deep breathing/incentive spirometry. Monitor for anastomotic leak (tachycardia, fever, tachypnea, abdominal pain, oliguria — EARLY signs before peritonitis). Progressive diet (clear liquids → full liquids → pureed → soft → regular over weeks). Strict vitamin/mineral supplementation for life.
NCLEX Trap: After bariatric surgery, oral NSAIDs are contraindicated (ulcer/staple line risk). Use acetaminophen for pain. Also, RYGB patients need LIFETIME B12, iron, calcium + vitamin D, folate, and multivitamin supplementation. Pregnancy should be delayed 12–18 months post-op.

Anastomotic Leak — EMERGENCY: Most feared complication of GI surgery. Signs: fever, tachycardia, tachypnea, hypotension, abdominal pain/distention, elevated WBC, decreased urine output. Treatment: NPO, IV antibiotics, drainage (IR or surgical), possible reoperation.

Clinical Judgment Case Study

GI Surgery Stoma Care

Case: New Colostomy Patient Education

A 58-year-old female had a sigmoid colostomy 2 days ago for diverticulitis. She is afraid to look at the stoma and refuses to participate in care. Her husband asks if she will ever be "normal" again.

What is the nurse's BEST initial response?

  1. Reassure her that the stoma is temporary and will be reversed soon
  2. Demonstrate stoma care first, then encourage her to participate gradually
  3. Tell her husband that many people live normal lives with ostomies
  4. Ask the wound/ostomy nurse to take over all education
Answer: 2 — Demonstrate stoma care first, then encourage gradual participation.

Body image disturbance is normal after ostomy surgery. The nurse should model acceptance by demonstrating care matter-of-factly, then have the patient touch/hold equipment, then assist with care, then perform independently. Never force the patient to look immediately. Address husband's concerns separately. Note: not all colostomies are temporary — verify surgical plan before promising reversal. Ostomy support groups (UOAA) are valuable resources.
