Heart Failure Deep Dive

Types, classification, pathophysiology, management, devices, and nursing care for heart failure on the NCLEX.

Types of Heart Failure

HFrEF (Heart Failure with reduced Ejection Fraction)

EF ≤40% — Systolic dysfunction. Heart can't contract effectively.

Causes: MI, dilated cardiomyopathy, myocarditis, chronic HTN

Manifestations: S3 gallop, displaced PMI, reduced pulse pressure, cool extremities, symptoms of forward failure (fatigue, weakness, oliguria)

HFpEF (Heart Failure with preserved Ejection Fraction)

EF ≥50% — Diastolic dysfunction. Heart can't relax and fill properly.

Causes: HTN, diabetes, obesity, age, aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy

Manifestations: S4 gallop, symptoms of backward failure (pulmonary congestion, JVD, peripheral edema) without significant systolic dysfunction
NCLEX Trap: HFpEF is common in elderly women with hypertension. Standard HFrEF drugs (ACEi, beta-blockers, ARNI) have NOT shown mortality benefit in HFpEF. Management focuses on controlling BP, rate, volume status.

Left-Sided Heart Failure

Backward effects (pulmonary congestion):

  • Dyspnea, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (PND)
  • Cough, crackles, wheezing (cardiac asthma)
  • Pink frothy sputum (acute pulmonary edema)
  • Pleural effusion
  • Tachypnea, hypoxemia

Forward effects (decreased systemic perfusion):

  • Fatigue, weakness
  • Oliguria, nocturia
  • Altered mental status
  • Tachycardia
Mnemonic: "Lungs are Left" — Left-sided HF backs up into the lungs

Right-Sided Heart Failure

Backward effects (systemic venous congestion):

  • Jugular venous distension (JVD)
  • Hepatomegaly, ascites
  • Peripheral edema (bilateral, dependent)
  • Splenomegaly

Forward effects:

  • Fatigue, weakness
  • Anorexia, nausea (GI congestion)
  • Weight gain (fluid retention)
Mnemonic: "Right = Rest of body" — Right-sided HF backs up into systemic circulation

NYHA Classification

Class Description
I No symptoms with ordinary activity
II Symptoms with ordinary activity; comfortable at rest
III Symptoms with less than ordinary activity; comfortable only at rest
IV Symptoms at rest; any activity worsens symptoms

Pharmacologic Management (HFrEF)

Guideline-Directed Medical Therapy (GDMT)

  • ACE inhibitors/ARBs/ARNI: Reduce afterload, reverse remodeling, mortality benefit
  • Beta-blockers (carvedilol, metoprolol succinate, bisoprolol): Reduce mortality, reverse remodeling. Start LOW, go SLOW.
  • MRA (spironolactone, eplerenone): Reduce mortality, fibrosis. Monitor K⁺ and creatinine.
  • SGLT2 inhibitors (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin): New standard — reduce CV death and hospitalization regardless of diabetes.
  • Diuretics (loop): Symptom relief, volume management. Do NOT improve mortality.
ARNI (Sacubitril/Valsartan — Entresto): Replaces ACEi/ARB in chronic HFrEF. Neprilysin inhibitor + ARB. Reduces mortality and hospitalization vs enalapril. Do NOT use within 36 hours of ACEi (angioedema risk). Contraindicated in pregnancy.

Acute Decompensated Heart Failure

Management:
  1. Position: High Fowler's or sitting upright
  2. Oxygen: High-flow, non-rebreather or BiPAP if needed
  3. IV loop diuretics: Furosemide (bolus or infusion)
  4. Vasodilators: Nitroglycerin (reduce preload), nitroprusside (reduce preload and afterload)
  5. Inotropes (if cardiogenic shock): Dobutamine, milrinone
  6. Morphine: Reduce anxiety, decrease catecholamine surge (use cautiously)
NCLEX Trap: Do NOT give beta-blockers in acute decompensated HF (worsens condition). Do NOT give calcium channel blockers (except amlodipine) in HFrEF. ACEi/ARB/ARNI should be continued if hemodynamically stable.

Devices & Advanced Therapies

Implantable Cardioverter-Defibrillator (ICD)

Indications: Primary prevention — ischemic cardiomyopathy with EF ≤35% despite optimal medical therapy for 3+ months (NYHA II-III). Secondary prevention — survived cardiac arrest or sustained VT.

Nursing Care: Assess for shock delivery. If patient receives shock and is stable → monitor, assess rhythm. If multiple shocks or unstable → call code, treat per ACLS. Keep magnesium available. Avoid electromagnetic interference (MRI restrictions).

Cardiac Resynchronization Therapy (CRT)

Indications: HFrEF with EF ≤35%, QRS ≥150 ms (LBBB morphology), NYHA II-IV despite optimal therapy

Function: Biventricular pacing (RV + LV leads) → synchronized ventricular contraction → improved CO

Left Ventricular Assist Device (LVAD)

Indications: Bridge to transplant, destination therapy, bridge to recovery

Nursing Priorities:
  • Monitor for bleeding (anticoagulation), thrombosis, infection (driveline site)
  • No CPR (risk of dislodgement)
  • Battery management — always have backup batteries
  • No MRI
  • Pulse may be absent (continuous flow device) — use Doppler for BP
  • Assess for suction events (alarm sounds)

NGN Case Study: Acute Decompensated Heart Failure

Client Scenario

Mrs. Eleanor Vance, 78-year-old female, arrives in the emergency department via EMS with severe shortness of breath. Her daughter reports she has been sleeping in a recliner for the past week because she "can't breathe lying flat." She has a history of HFrEF (EF 30%), hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and atrial fibrillation.

Vital Signs Nurse's Notes Provider Orders Medications

Vital Signs & Assessment

  • BP: 186/104 mmHg
  • HR: 118 bpm (irregularly irregular)
  • RR: 32 breaths/min, labored
  • SpO₂: 84% on room air
  • Temp: 37.1°C (98.8°F)
  • Weight: 68 kg (baseline 61 kg one month ago)
  • Lungs: Bilateral crackles to mid-lung fields, pink frothy sputum
  • Heart: S3 gallop, no murmur
  • Extremities: 2+ pitting edema bilateral lower extremities, cool and clammy
  • JVD: 6 cm at 45°

Nurse's Notes

Patient is anxious, sitting upright, tripod positioning. States she ran out of her furosemide 3 days ago because she "couldn't get to the pharmacy." Daughter confirms patient has been eating canned soups and frozen dinners (high sodium). Patient takes all other medications as prescribed: metoprolol succinate, lisinopril, spironolactone, apixaban.

Provider Orders

  1. Oxygen to maintain SpO₂ > 92%
  2. IV access, continuous cardiac monitoring
  3. Furosemide 80 mg IV push now, then 40 mg/hr IV infusion
  4. Nitroglycerin IV infusion titrate 5-200 mcg/min per protocol
  5. Strict intake & output, daily weights
  6. Basic metabolic panel, BNP, troponin, CBC
  7. Chest X-ray
  8. NPO except medications

Home Medications

  • Metoprolol succinate 50 mg daily
  • Lisinopril 20 mg daily
  • Spironolactone 25 mg daily
  • Apixaban 5 mg BID
  • Furosemide 40 mg daily (out for 3 days)

Clinical Judgment Questions

Question 1 — Recognize Cues: After reviewing the client data, which findings require immediate follow-up by the nurse? (Select all that apply)

  1. Weight gain of 7 kg over one month
  2. SpO₂ of 84% on room air
  3. Blood pressure of 186/104 mmHg
  4. Temperature of 37.1°C
  5. Pink frothy sputum
  6. HR of 118 bpm irregularly irregular
Correct: A, B, C, E, F
D is normal. All other findings indicate acute decompensated heart failure with pulmonary edema, severe hypertension, tachycardia with afib, and significant fluid overload.

Question 2 — Analyze Cues: Which pathophysiological process best explains the client's pink frothy sputum?

  1. Right-sided heart failure causing hepatic congestion
  2. Increased pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure causing alveolar flooding
  3. Community-acquired pneumonia with hemoptysis
  4. Pulmonary embolism causing infarction
Correct: B
Pink frothy sputum is classic for acute pulmonary edema. Left-sided heart failure causes increased pulmonary capillary pressure → fluid shifts into alveoli → mixes with air → pink frothy appearance.

Question 3 — Prioritize Hypotheses: The nurse is prioritizing client problems. Which problem should be addressed FIRST?

  1. Fluid volume excess related to medication nonadherence
  2. Ineffective airway clearance related to pulmonary edema
  3. Activity intolerance related to decreased cardiac output
  4. Knowledge deficit related to sodium restriction
Correct: B
ABCs first — the client has SpO₂ 84% with pulmonary edema. Airway and breathing take priority over fluid management, activity, or education. Address oxygenation first, then fluid volume.

Question 4 — Generate Solutions: Which intervention should the nurse implement FIRST?

  1. Administer furosemide 80 mg IV push
  2. Apply high-flow oxygen via non-rebreather mask
  3. Insert Foley catheter for strict I&O
  4. Draw labs including BNP and troponin
Correct: B
Oxygenation is the immediate priority. SpO₂ of 84% is life-threatening. Apply oxygen FIRST, then proceed with other interventions. High Fowler's positioning should also be implemented immediately.

Question 5 — Take Action: Two hours after admission, the client's SpO₂ is 94% on 6L NC, BP is 162/94, and she reports she "feels a little better." The nurse notes she has had 800 mL of urine output since admission. Which is the nurse's BEST next action?

  1. Continue current plan of care and monitor
  2. Request provider to increase furosemide infusion
  3. Decrease oxygen and transition to nasal cannula at 2L
  4. Call the rapid response team
Correct: A
The client is improving: SpO₂ ↑, BP trending down, diuresing well (800 mL in 2 hours). Continue current therapy and monitor. No need to escalate or call rapid response. Oxygen can be weaned gradually once SpO₂ stabilizes > 92% on lower flow.

Question 6 — Evaluate Outcomes: On day 3, the client is ready for discharge. Which statement by the client indicates a need for FURTHER teaching?

  1. "I will weigh myself every morning and call if I gain 2 pounds in one day."
  2. "I can have a can of chicken noodle soup when I'm too tired to cook."
  3. "I'll set up automatic refills so I don't run out of my water pill again."
  4. "I should sleep with my head elevated if I get short of breath at night."
Correct: B
Canned soup is extremely high in sodium (~800-1200 mg per can). Heart failure patients need sodium restriction (typically < 2g/day). This statement indicates the client needs further education about low-sodium food choices.
Case Study Learning Points:
  • ABCs always first — Oxygenation before diuresis in acute pulmonary edema
  • Weight = fluid — 7 kg gain ≈ 7L fluid retention
  • Medication adherence matters — Running out of diuretics is a common cause of decompensation
  • Diet education is ongoing — Hidden sodium in processed foods
  • Pink frothy sputum = emergency — Classic sign of alveolar flooding
