NCLEX Hematology Disorders

Anemias, sickle cell, hemophilia, DIC, and blood transfusions — nursing priorities and NCLEX traps.

1. Anemias

General Anemia Symptoms

Fatigue, pallor, tachycardia, dyspnea on exertion, dizziness, cold intolerance
The body compensates by increasing cardiac output (tachycardia) and respiratory rate.

Iron Deficiency Anemia

Causes: Blood loss (GI, menorrhagia), poor intake, malabsorption
Lab: ↓ Hgb, ↓ MCV (microcytic), ↓ MCH, ↓ Ferritin, ↑ TIBC
Nursing Priorities:
• Iron supplements: take on EMPTY STOMACH with vitamin C (orange juice) for absorption
• Side effects: constipation, dark stools (expected), nausea, abdominal pain
• Avoid taking with antacids, calcium, dairy, coffee, tea (inhibits absorption)
• Increase dietary iron: red meat, dark leafy greens, fortified cereals
• Investigate source of blood loss (colonoscopy for men/postmenopausal women)
NCLEX Trap: Liquid iron stains teeth — have patient drink through a straw and rinse mouth after. ALSO: Ferrous sulfate is the most common and cheapest form, but ferrous gluconate may be better tolerated. Know that IV iron (iron dextran) has risk of anaphylaxis — have epinephrine available.

Vitamin B12 Deficiency (Pernicious Anemia)

Causes: Pernicious anemia (lack of intrinsic factor), gastrectomy, Crohn's disease, vegan diet
Lab: ↓ Hgb, ↑ MCV (macrocytic), ↓ B12 level, positive intrinsic factor antibodies
Unique Symptoms (not seen in other anemias):
• Glossitis (beefy red, sore tongue)
• Paresthesia (tingling in hands/feet)
• Ataxia, loss of position/vibration sense
• Memory changes, dementia (if severe/untreated)
Treatment: IM B12 injections (lifelong for pernicious anemia), or high-dose oral B12 if malabsorption not severe. Sublingual and nasal options also available.
NCLEX Trap: B12 deficiency causes NEUROLOGICAL symptoms that can become PERMANENT if not treated promptly. Folate deficiency also causes macrocytic anemia but WITHOUT neurological symptoms. If a patient has macrocytic anemia + neuropathy, think B12 — NOT folate.

Folate Deficiency

Causes: Poor diet, alcoholism, pregnancy, hemolytic anemia, methotrexate
Lab: ↓ Hgb, ↑ MCV (macrocytic), ↓ folate level, NORMAL B12
Treatment: Oral folic acid 1 mg daily. Dietary sources: leafy greens, legumes, fortified grains. NO neurological symptoms (unlike B12).
NCLEX Trap: NEVER treat B12 deficiency with folate alone — it will correct the anemia but neurological damage will PROGRESS. Always check B12 levels before starting folate in macrocytic anemia.

Aplastic Anemia

Patho: Bone marrow failure → pancytopenia (↓ RBC, ↓ WBC, ↓ platelets)
Causes: Idiopathic, radiation, chemotherapy, benzene, chloramphenicol, viral
Nursing Priorities:
• Bleeding precautions (low platelets)
• Infection precautions (neutropenia)
• Transfusions as needed
• Bone marrow transplant is curative
• Immunosuppressive therapy (ATG, cyclosporine) for non-transplant candidates

2. Sickle Cell Disease

Pathophysiology

Genetic mutation → hemoglobin S → RBCs sickle under low O₂, dehydration, cold, infection, stress → vaso-occlusion → ischemia → pain crisis → organ damage.

Triggers: "HOCDICS"
Hypoxia | Overexertion | Cold | Dehydration | Infection | Climbing altitude | Stress
Nursing Priorities During Crisis:
• O₂ therapy (keep SpO₂ >92%)
• Aggressive hydration (IV fluids — reduces sickling)
• Pain management (opioids around the clock — this is REAL pain, not drug-seeking)
• Warm compresses to painful areas (vasodilation)
• Blood transfusion (simple or exchange) if severe
• Monitor for complications: ACS (acute chest syndrome), stroke, priapism, splenic sequestration
NCLEX Trap: Acute Chest Syndrome (ACS) in sickle cell is a MEDICAL EMERGENCY: chest pain, fever, cough, hypoxemia, new pulmonary infiltrate. Treatment: O₂, antibiotics, bronchodilators, simple or exchange transfusion. ACS is the leading cause of death in sickle cell adults.

3. Hemophilia

Hemophilia A vs B

Hemophilia AHemophilia B
DeficiencyFactor VIIIFactor IX
InheritanceX-linked recessiveX-linked recessive
TreatmentFactor VIII replacementFactor IX replacement
Nursing Priorities:
• Factor replacement for bleeding or before procedures
• RICE (Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation) for joint bleeds
• Avoid IM injections, NSAIDs, aspirin
• Dental precautions
• Genetic counseling
• DDAVP (desmopressin) for mild Hemophilia A (increases factor VIII release)
NCLEX Trap: Hemophilia causes BLEEDING into JOINTS (hemarthrosis) and soft tissues — NOT petechiae or purpura (those are platelet disorders). A child with hemophilia who fell and has a swollen knee: treat the joint bleed with factor replacement, NOT just ice and rest.

4. Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC)

Paradox: Clotting AND Bleeding Simultaneously

Patho: Trigger (sepsis, trauma, obstetric complications) → massive coagulation cascade activation → fibrin clots form everywhere → platelets and clotting factors consumed → bleeding from every orifice and IV site.

Bleeding Signs: Oozing from IV sites, petechiae, purpura, hematuria, GI bleeding, epistaxis, ecchymoses
Clotting Signs: Acrocyanosis, digital ischemia, organ failure (renal, hepatic)
Nursing Priorities:
• Treat the underlying cause (this is KEY)
• Blood products: FFP, platelets, cryoprecipitate, PRBCs
• Heparin (LOW DOSE) if thrombosis predominates — CONTROVERSIAL
• Bleeding precautions
• Monitor for organ failure
• NO IM injections, minimize venipuncture
NCLEX Trap: DIC is NOT treated with anticoagulants alone — the primary treatment is addressing the underlying cause (sepsis, placental abruption, etc.). Giving heparin to a patient with active DIC bleeding is DANGEROUS unless specifically for thrombotic DIC.

5. Blood Transfusions

Transfusion Reactions

AnaphylacticWithin minutesBronchospasm, hypotension, angioedemaSTOP! Epinephrine, O₂, fluids — IgA deficiency is cause
TRALI1-6 hoursAcute respiratory distress, non-cardiogenic pulmonary edemaStop transfusion, O₂, supportive care
TACODuring/shortly afterDyspnea, JVD, HTN, pulmonary edemaSlow/stop, diuretics, O₂
Bacterial contaminationRapidFever, shock, DICStop immediately, broad-spectrum antibiotics, blood cultures
Pre-Transfusion Nursing:
• Verify blood product with another RN (blood type, unit number, expiration)
• Check vital signs before starting
• Remain with patient during first 15 minutes (most reactions occur then)
• Use normal saline tubing (never add medications)
• Prime with NS only (never D5W — causes hemolysis)
NCLEX Trap: For ANY suspected transfusion reaction, the FIRST action is ALWAYS: STOP THE TRANSFUSION and keep the IV line open with normal saline. Then notify the provider. Never flush the blood product through — it worsens the reaction.

NGN Case Study: Acute Blood Transfusion Reaction

Client Scenario

Mr. Johnson, 65-year-old male, is receiving his first unit of PRBCs for symptomatic anemia (Hgb 6.8) secondary to chemotherapy for colon cancer. The transfusion was started 15 minutes ago at 125 mL/hr.

Vital Signs Transfusion Status History

Vital Signs: BP 98/58 (baseline 128/76), HR 118 (baseline 78), RR 24, Temp 38.6°C (baseline 36.8°C), SpO₂ 94%

Transfusion Status: 30 mL infused. Patient reports chills, low back pain, and a "tight" feeling in his chest. Skin is flushed. Urine output in the last hour is 15 mL, dark amber color.

History: Colon cancer stage III, receiving FOLFOX chemotherapy. Type A positive. No prior transfusions. No known allergies.

Question 1 — Recognize Cues: Based on the clinical presentation, which type of transfusion reaction is most likely?

  • A. Febrile non-hemolytic reaction
  • B. Acute hemolytic transfusion reaction
  • C. Allergic reaction
  • D. Transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI)

Correct: B. Acute hemolytic transfusion reaction

Acute hemolytic reaction presents with fever, chills, low back pain (from hemoglobinuria causing renal colic), chest tightness, hypotension, tachycardia, and dark urine (hemoglobinuria). This is a MEDICAL EMERGENCY caused by ABO incompatibility. Febrile reaction (A) causes fever and chills but NOT back pain, hypotension, or hemoglobinuria. Allergic (C) causes urticaria and pruritus. TRALI (D) causes acute respiratory distress within 6 hours, not back pain or hemoglobinuria.

Question 2 — Analyze Cues: What is the nurse's first action?

  • A. Slow the transfusion to 50 mL/hr and notify the provider
  • B. Stop the transfusion immediately, keep IV line open with NS, and notify the provider
  • C. Give acetaminophen 650mg for fever and continue the transfusion
  • D. Draw a CBC and send the blood bag to the lab

Correct: B. Stop the transfusion immediately, keep IV line open with NS, and notify the provider

Any suspected transfusion reaction requires IMMEDIATE cessation of the transfusion. The IV line must be kept open with normal saline (new tubing) to maintain access for emergency medications. Do NOT slow the rate (A) — any amount of incompatible blood worsens the reaction. Acetaminophen (C) is inappropriate — this is not a simple febrile reaction. Labs (D) are important but secondary to stopping the blood.

Question 3 — Prioritize Hypotheses: The provider orders aggressive fluid resuscitation and mannitol. What is the rationale for these interventions?

  • A. To treat hypotension from septic shock
  • B. To maintain urine output and prevent acute kidney injury from hemoglobinuria
  • C. To dilute the incompatible blood in the circulation
  • D. To replace blood volume lost from the reaction

Correct: B. To maintain urine output and prevent acute kidney injury from hemoglobinuria

Free hemoglobin from RBC lysis precipitates in the renal tubules, causing acute tubular necrosis. The goal is to maintain urine output >100 mL/hr to flush hemoglobin through. Mannitol is an osmotic diuretic that increases urine output. Fluids maintain intravascular volume and renal perfusion. This is a classic NCLEX concept — hemolytic reactions threaten the kidneys, and the priority is protecting renal function.

Question 4 — Generate Solutions: Which documentation is most important after a suspected transfusion reaction?

  • A. Record the patient's subjective complaints and vital signs
  • B. Return the blood bag and tubing to the blood bank with a transfusion reaction form
  • C. Document the lot number, unit number, and expiration date of the blood product
  • D. All of the above

Correct: D. All of the above

Complete documentation after a transfusion reaction includes: patient's symptoms and vital signs (A), returning blood bag and tubing to blood bank (B), and documenting all identifiers (C). The blood bank needs the bag and tubing to cross-check for errors (wrong patient, wrong blood type). The reaction workup includes direct antiglobulin test (DAT), plasma free hemoglobin, urinalysis for hemoglobinuria, and repeat type and screen.

Question 5 — Take Action: Mr. Johnson's reaction is confirmed as acute hemolytic due to a clerical error (wrong blood type given). He requires additional transfusion. Which blood product is safest?

  • A. Type A positive PRBCs (his type) with extended crossmatch
  • B. O negative PRBCs (universal donor) until full workup complete
  • C. Type AB positive PRBCs (universal recipient)
  • D. Whole blood from a directed donor

Correct: B. O negative PRBCs (universal donor) until full workup complete

After a hemolytic reaction, the antibody screen may be altered, and extended phenotyping is needed. Until the full investigation is complete, O negative (for females of childbearing age) or O positive (for males/post-menopausal females) is the safest choice. Once the workup is complete, type-specific blood can be given. Type AB (C) is the universal recipient for plasma, not RBCs. Directed donation (D) is not indicated.

Question 6 — Evaluate Outcomes: Two days later, Mr. Johnson's creatinine is 2.1 (baseline 0.9). Which finding would indicate that his kidney function is improving?

  • A. Urine output 20 mL/hr
  • B. BUN 45, Creatinine 2.0
  • C. Urine output 60 mL/hr and creatinine trending down
  • D. Dark, tea-colored urine

Correct: C. Urine output 60 mL/hr and creatinine trending down

Adequate urine output (>0.5 mL/kg/hr or ~30-50 mL/hr for adults) and declining creatinine indicate recovering kidney function. Low urine output (A) suggests ongoing AKI. Stable creatinine (B) doesn't show improvement. Tea-colored urine (D) indicates ongoing hemoglobinuria. The goal is clear, adequate urine output with improving labs. Diuresis with mannitol and fluids should continue until urine clears and creatinine normalizes.
