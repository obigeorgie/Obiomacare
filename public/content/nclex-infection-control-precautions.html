NCLEX Infection Control & Precautions

Standard precautions, transmission-based precautions, PPE donning/doffing, isolation types, and NCLEX priority-setting.

1. Standard Precautions

📋 Standard Precautions (Used for ALL Patients)

✅ Components:
• Hand hygiene (soap/water or alcohol-based sanitizer)
• Gloves when touching blood, body fluids, mucous membranes, non-intact skin
• Gown if soiling likely
• Mask, eye protection, face shield if splash/spray likely
• Safe needle practices (never recap, use sharps containers)
• Respiratory hygiene/cough etiquette
🚨 NCLEX Trap: Hand hygiene is the SINGLE MOST EFFECTIVE way to prevent infection transmission. Alcohol-based sanitizer is fine UNLESS hands are visibly soiled or after C. diff or norovirus — use soap and water for those (alcohol doesn't kill spores).

3. PPE Donning and Doffing

📋 PPE Order

✅ DONNING (Putting On):
1. Hand hygiene
2. Gown (tie neck, then waist)
3. Mask/N95 (mold to nose, check seal)
4. Eye protection (if needed)
5. Gloves (over gown cuffs)
✅ DOFFING (Taking Off) — CRITICAL!
1. Gloves (inside out, ball in opposite gloved hand)
2. Gown (untie waist, then neck, roll away from body)
3. Hand hygiene
4. Eye protection (if used)
5. Mask/N95 (untie bottom, then top; don't touch front)
6. Hand hygiene
🚨 NCLEX Trap: Doffing order is HIGH-YIELD on NCLEX. Remember: gloves come off FIRST, mask comes off LAST (after hand hygiene). If you only remember one thing: the mask is the LAST thing you remove because it's protecting your airway.

🚨 NCLEX Trap: For airborne precautions, the room MUST have negative pressure with ≥12 air exchanges per hour. If a negative pressure room isn't available, use a HEPA filtration unit and keep the door closed. NEVER put an airborne precaution patient in a room with another patient.

🧩 NGN Case Study: Multi-Drug Resistant Organism Exposure

Client Scenario

Mrs. Williams, 78-year-old female, is admitted to a semi-private room on the medical-surgical floor for treatment of a UTI. She has a history of recurrent UTIs, hospitalization 3 weeks ago for pneumonia, and mild dementia. Her roommate is a 65-year-old male post-op colectomy day 2.

Vital Signs History Culture Results

Vital Signs: BP 142/80, HR 88, RR 18, Temp 38.2°C, SpO₂ 94%

History: Recurrent UTIs (3 in past 6 months). Hospitalized 3 weeks ago for pneumonia — received levofloxacin and ceftriaxone. Lives in assisted living. Mild dementia — requires cueing for ADLs. No known allergies.

Culture Results (Pending): Urine culture sent. Previous culture 6 months ago grew E. coli sensitive to all antibiotics.

Question 1 — Recognize Cues: Given Mrs. Williams' history, which risk factor most significantly increases her likelihood of a multi-drug resistant (MDR) organism?

  • A. Her age (78 years)
  • B. Recent hospitalization with broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure
  • C. Residence in assisted living facility
  • D. Recurrent UTIs

Correct: B. Recent hospitalization with broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure

Recent hospitalization (within 3 months) plus exposure to broad-spectrum antibiotics (levofloxacin, ceftriaxone) is the strongest risk factor for MDR organisms. Antibiotics select for resistant bacteria, and hospital environments harbor resistant strains. While age (A), assisted living (C), and recurrent UTIs (D) are risk factors, the combination of recent hospitalization + antibiotics is the most significant predictor of resistance.

Question 2 — Analyze Cues: The urine culture returns: ESBL-producing E. coli. Which isolation precaution is most appropriate?

  • A. Standard precautions only — ESBL is normal flora
  • B. Contact precautions (gown and gloves for room entry)
  • C. Droplet precautions (surgical mask within 3 feet)
  • D. Airborne precautions (N95 respirator required)

Correct: B. Contact precautions (gown and gloves for room entry)

ESBL (Extended-Spectrum Beta-Lactamase) organisms are transmitted by contact with contaminated surfaces, equipment, or hands. Contact precautions require gown and gloves for room entry and dedicated equipment. ESBL is NOT normal flora (A). Droplet (C) is for respiratory pathogens. Airborne (D) is for TB, measles, varicella. This is a high-yield NCLEX topic — know which organisms require which precautions.

Question 3 — Prioritize Hypotheses: Mrs. Williams is currently in a semi-private room with a post-op colectomy patient. What is the nurse's priority action?

  • A. Move Mrs. Williams to a private room with Contact precautions signage
  • B. Educate the roommate about hand hygiene
  • C. Continue current room assignment — the risk is low
  • D. Place BOTH patients on Contact precautions

Correct: A. Move Mrs. Williams to a private room with Contact precautions signage

Patients with MDR organisms should be placed in private rooms when available to prevent transmission to vulnerable roommates. The post-op colectomy patient is immunocompromised (surgical stress, possible antibiotics) and at high risk for acquiring the ESBL organism. Contact precautions signage alerts all staff. While educating the roommate (B) is important, physical separation is the priority. Option C is unsafe. Option D is unnecessary — only the infected/colonized patient needs precautions.

Question 4 — Generate Solutions: The provider orders ertapenem for the ESBL UTI. A nursing student asks why the patient can't receive ceftriaxone. What is the nurse's best explanation?

  • A. "Ceftriaxone is too expensive for this patient"
  • B. "ESBL organisms produce enzymes that destroy most cephalosporins and penicillins"
  • C. "The patient is allergic to cephalosporins"
  • D. "Ertapenem is the standard first-line treatment for all UTIs"

Correct: B. "ESBL organisms produce enzymes that destroy most cephalosporins and penicillins"

ESBL stands for Extended-Spectrum Beta-Lactamase — these enzymes hydrolyze the beta-lactam ring of penicillins, cephalosporins, and monobactams, rendering them ineffective. Carbapenems (ertapenem, meropenem) are the treatment of choice for ESBL infections. This is a high-yield NCLEX concept. Cost (A) is never the reason for antibiotic selection. The patient has no allergy (C). Ertapenem is NOT first-line for all UTIs (D) — it's reserved for resistant organisms.

Question 5 — Take Action: A family member arrives to visit and goes directly to Mrs. Williams' room without stopping at the nurses' station. She is not wearing a gown or gloves. What is the nurse's best action?

  • A. Allow the visit — family members don't need precautions
  • B. Stop the family member and provide education on Contact precautions
  • C. Ask the family member to leave immediately
  • D. Document the breach but take no action

Correct: B. Stop the family member and provide education on Contact precautions

Family visitors should follow the same precautions as staff for their own protection and to prevent carrying organisms out of the room. The nurse should kindly stop the visitor, explain the precautions, and provide gown/gloves. This is an education opportunity, not a punitive situation. Visitors are not banned (C), but they must follow precautions. Documentation (D) without action is inadequate.

Question 6 — Evaluate Outcomes: Mrs. Williams is discharged to the assisted living facility. Which instruction to the facility staff is highest priority?

  • A. "Mrs. Williams must remain in her room for 2 weeks"
  • B. "Continue Contact precautions if she has wound drainage or incontinence"
  • C. "No visitors are allowed until the infection clears"
  • D. "Mrs. Williams cannot participate in group activities"

Correct: B. "Continue Contact precautions if she has wound drainage or incontinence"

In long-term care, Contact precautions for MDR organisms are typically maintained when the patient has drainage, incontinence, or wound care needs. Once these resolve, precautions may be discontinued per facility policy. Isolating the patient indefinitely (A, C, D) is not appropriate and can cause psychological harm. The focus should be on hand hygiene, proper PPE use during care, and environmental cleaning.
