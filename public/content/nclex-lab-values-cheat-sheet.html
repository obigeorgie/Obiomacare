NCLEX Lab Values Cheat Sheet (2026)

The exact lab values the NCLEX tests. Know these cold, and you'll eliminate wrong answers faster.

By Nnamdi Okorafor, RN · 15-year ER nurse · Updated July 2026 · Obioma

Why lab values matter on NCLEX: The exam tests whether you recognize abnormal findings and know what to do about them. You don't need to memorize every lab — but you MUST know the critical values that indicate life-threatening conditions.

Critical: Must Memorize

These values appear on almost every NCLEX. Know the normal range, and what high/low means clinically.

Potassium is the #1 tested electrolyte. Know that K+ > 6.5 causes peaked T-waves and risk of cardiac arrest. K+ < 2.5 causes U-waves and ventricular dysrhythmias. Always check potassium first in cardiac patients.

🟡 Important: Know Trends

NCLEX often tests trends, not just single values. A value "within normal limits" that is trending up or down can be significant.

  • Creatinine rising from 1.0 to 2.0: Acute kidney injury — notify provider
  • WBC dropping from 15,000 to 3,000: Possible sepsis — monitor closely
  • Potassium falling from 4.5 to 3.2: Risk for dysrhythmias — supplement ordered
  • Hgb dropping from 12 to 8: Active bleeding — may need transfusion

How NCLEX Tests Lab Values

The exam uses lab values in three main ways:

  1. Recognition: "Which finding requires immediate intervention?" (K+ = 6.8 → cardiac risk)
  2. Prioritization: "Which patient should the nurse see first?" (Compare abnormal values)
  3. Intervention: "A patient has Na+ = 118. What is the priority nursing action?" (Seizure precautions, slow correction)

Memory trick for sodium: "Salt = 135-145" — if you only remember one thing, remember sodium normal range. It's the most commonly tested electrolyte.
