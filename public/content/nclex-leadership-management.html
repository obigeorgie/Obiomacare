NCLEX Leadership & Management

Delegation, prioritization, conflict resolution, and change management for NCLEX success.

1. Delegation

Five Rights of Delegation (NCSBN)

Right Task, Right Circumstance, Right Person, Right Direction/Communication, Right Supervision
1. Right Task: Appropriate for delegation (stable patient, routine task)
2. Right Circumstance: Appropriate patient condition and setting
3. Right Person: Person has competence to perform
4. Right Direction: Clear, concise instructions; expected outcomes; timeline
5. Right Supervision: Appropriate monitoring, evaluation, feedback

What Can and Cannot Be Delegated

CAN Delegate (to appropriate personnel)CANNOT Delegate
Stable vital signsInitial assessment
Bathing and hygieneNursing diagnosis and care planning
Ambulation and positioningPatient/family teaching (initial)
Feeding (stable patient)Clinical judgment and decision-making
I&O measurementIV push medications
Specimen collectionCentral line care
Glucometer checks (if trained)Discharge planning
Bedmaking and room tidyingEvaluation of care outcomes
NCLEX Trap: "The RN delegates the patient assessment to the LPN" is ALWAYS wrong. Assessment is an RN responsibility that cannot be delegated. "The RN delegates teaching about a new medication to the UAP" is also wrong — teaching requires nursing judgment.

Delegation Decision Tree

Ask These Questions:
• Is the patient stable? (Unstable = RN only)
• Is the task within the person's scope of practice?
• Has the person been trained and verified competent?
• Is the task predictable with minimal risk?
• Can I provide appropriate supervision?
• Does the task require nursing judgment?
NCLEX Trap: Even if a task CAN be delegated, the RN remains ACCOUNTABLE for the outcome. "I delegated it to the UAP, so it's not my responsibility" is WRONG. You are responsible for follow-up and verification.

2. Prioritization Frameworks

Maslow's Hierarchy Applied to Nursing

Priority LevelNeedsNursing Examples
HighestPhysiologicalAirway, breathing, circulation, pain, elimination, sleep, nutrition
Safety/SecurityFall precautions, infection control, medication safety, call light within reach
Love/BelongingFamily involvement, support groups, therapeutic communication
Self-EsteemPromoting independence, acknowledging strengths
LowestSelf-ActualizationEducation, goal setting, discharge planning
Remember: "ABCs before ADLs"
Airway, Breathing, Circulation come before Activities of Daily Living

The "Who Do You See First?" Strategy

Step-by-Step:
1. Look for immediate threats to life: Airway obstruction, hemorrhage, anaphylaxis, cardiac arrest
2. Look for sudden changes: New onset confusion, chest pain, dyspnea, decreased LOC
3. Look for potential complications: Post-op bleeding, signs of infection, DVT
4. Compare stable patients: Who has the greatest need based on diagnosis, treatments, psychosocial factors?
NCLEX Trap: When comparing patients, the one with the GREATEST RISK of rapid deterioration gets priority. A post-op patient with stable vitals but drainage on dressing (possible hemorrhage) takes priority over a patient requesting a bedpan. A patient with new-onset chest pain takes priority over a patient requesting pain medication for chronic back pain.

3. Conflict Resolution

Conflict Management Styles

StyleDescriptionBest Used When
CollaboratingWin-win, both parties' needs metComplex issues, long-term relationships, important decisions
CompromisingBoth give something upTime is limited, equal power, temporary solution acceptable
AccommodatingOne yields to the otherIssue is more important to other person, preserving relationship
AvoidingWithdraw from conflictTrivial issue, emotions too high, need more information
CompetingWin-lose, assertive and uncooperativeEmergency, unpopular decision, patient safety at risk
Nurse-to-Nurse Conflict Resolution Steps:
1. Address privately and professionally
2. Use "I" statements (not "you" accusations)
3. Focus on behavior, not personality
4. Seek to understand the other person's perspective
5. Collaborate on solution
6. Escalate to manager if unresolved and affects patient care

4. Change Management

Lewin's Change Theory

Unfreeze → Change → Refreeze
1. Unfreeze: Prepare for change (communicate need, address resistance)
2. Change: Implement new practices (education, support, pilot programs)
3. Refreeze: Stabilize and sustain (reinforce, evaluate, integrate into culture)
Overcoming Resistance to Change:
• Involve staff in planning
• Communicate clearly and frequently
• Address fears and concerns
• Provide education and training
• Identify and support change champions
• Start with small pilot groups
• Celebrate early wins
• Provide ongoing support

5. Quality Improvement

PDSA Cycle

Plan → Do → Study → Act
• Plan: Identify problem, develop hypothesis, plan intervention
• Do: Implement on small scale
• Study: Collect and analyze data
• Act: Adopt, adapt, or abandon based on results
Other QI Tools:
• Root cause analysis (RCA): "5 Whys" to identify underlying cause
• Failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA): Proactive risk assessment
• Six Sigma: Reduce variation and defects
• Benchmarking: Compare to best performers
• Just culture: Balance accountability with learning from errors

6. Time Management

Strategies for Busy Shifts

Prioritization Techniques:
• Cluster care: Group tasks to minimize room entries
• Delegate appropriately: Free up RN time for assessments and critical thinking
• Use "to-do" lists: Prioritize by urgency and importance
• Anticipate needs: Prepare supplies before entering room
• Minimize interruptions: Batch non-urgent tasks
• Report off on time: Start report prep 30 min before shift end
NCLEX Trap: "The nurse has multiple tasks to complete. Which should be done first?" — Always look for the patient at GREATEST RISK. Time management questions on NCLEX are actually SAFETY questions in disguise.

Clinical Judgment Case Study

Delegation Leadership

Case: Staffing Crisis

You are charge nurse on a busy med-surg unit with 6 patients and only one CNA. You have two new admissions pending, a patient needing blood transfusion, and a post-op patient requesting pain medication. The CNA has 10 years of experience.

What should you delegate to the CNA?

  1. Vital signs on all patients and blood glucose checks
  2. Assisting with the blood transfusion setup
  3. Administering PRN pain medication
  4. Initial admission assessments
Answer: 1 — Vital signs and blood glucose checks.

The "5 Rights of Delegation": Right task, right person, right circumstances, right direction, right supervision. CNAs can perform vital signs, blood glucose, bathing, ambulation, feeding. They CANNOT: administer medications (including blood), perform assessments, or teach. Blood transfusion requires RN assessment and monitoring. Pain medication is nursing judgment. Admission assessments require RN licensure.
