NCLEX Mechanical Ventilation & ARDS

Ventilator modes, settings, ARDS management, sedation, and weaning for critical care nursing and NCLEX.

1. Ventilator Basics

📋 Common Ventilator Modes

ModeDescriptionWhen Used
AC (Assist-Control)Every breath fully supported at set TV; patient can trigger extra breathsInitial ventilation, paralyzed/sedated patients
SIMV (Synchronized IMV)Set number of mandatory breaths; patient breathes spontaneously betweenWeaning, patients with some respiratory drive
PSV (Pressure Support)Spontaneous breathing with pressure assistance for each breathWeaning, spontaneous breathing trials
CPAPContinuous positive airway pressure; all breaths spontaneousNon-invasive (BiPAP/CPAP mask), OSA
BiPAPTwo pressure levels (IPAP/EPAP); supports inspiration and expirationCOPD, CHF, weaning
APRVAirway Pressure Release Ventilation; maintains high pressure with brief releasesARDS, refractory hypoxemia

📋 Key Ventilator Settings

SettingDefinitionNormal/Typical Range
FiO₂Fraction of inspired oxygen21–100% (wean to <40% if possible)
Tidal Volume (Vt)Volume of each breath6–8 mL/kg (lung-protective)
RR (Respiratory Rate)Breaths per minute set by vent12–20/min
PEEPPositive end-expiratory pressure5–15 cmH₂O (ARDS may need higher)
PIP (Peak Inspiratory Pressure)Maximum pressure during inspiration<35 cmH₂O (barotrauma risk if higher)
Plateau PressurePressure at end-inspiration with no flow<30 cmH₂O
🚨 NCLEX Trap: If PIP suddenly increases, think: mucus plug (suction), bronchospasm, pneumothorax, or patient biting tube. If PIP suddenly decreases, think: disconnection, cuff leak, or ET tube displacement. Always check the patient and circuit first.

2. ARDS (Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome)

📋 ARDS Definition & Patho

Berlin Criteria (all must be present):
• Acute onset within 1 week of known insult
• Bilateral infiltrates on CXR (not fully explained by effusions, collapse, or nodules)
• Respiratory failure not fully explained by cardiac failure/fluid overload
• PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio: Mild 200–300, Moderate 100–200, Severe <100
✅ ARDS Patho: Inflammatory response → alveolar-capillary membrane damage → fluid leaks into alveoli → decreased surfactant → atelectasis → V/Q mismatch → refractory hypoxemia → stiff, noncompliant lungs.

📋 ARDS Management

✅ Lung-Protective Ventilation Strategy:
• Low tidal volume: 6 mL/kg (not 10–12 mL/kg)
• Plateau pressure <30 cmH₂O
• PEEP: Sufficient to prevent alveolar collapse (recruitment)
• Permissive hypercapnia: Accept higher PaCO₂ if needed to protect lungs
• Prone positioning: 16+ hours/day for severe ARDS (PaO₂/FiO₂ <150)
• Conservative fluid strategy: Avoid fluid overload
• Neuromuscular blockade: Short-term for severe ARDS (cisatracurium)
• NO routine corticosteroids (controversial; may help if cause is known)
🚨 NCLEX Trap: The priority in ARDS is lung-protective ventilation (low Vt, limit plateau pressure), NOT maximizing oxygenation at all costs. High tidal volumes and pressures cause ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI). ARDS mortality is high; prevention of VILI is key.

3. Ventilator Alarms

📋 High Pressure Alarm

Causes: Mucus plug, biting ET tube, bronchospasm, pneumothorax, coughing, secretions, kinked tubing, water in tubing, decreased lung compliance
✅ Nursing Actions:
1. Check patient first (color, breathing effort, SpO₂)
2. Check for tube kinking or patient biting
3. Suction if secretions suspected
4. Auscultate lungs (pneumothorax = absent breath sounds)
5. Check tubing for water/condensation
6. Notify RT/MD if unresolved

📋 Low Pressure/Disconnect Alarm

Causes: Disconnected tubing, ET cuff leak, displaced tube, circuit leak, patient self-extubation
✅ Nursing Actions:
1. Check all connections
2. Check ET tube position and cuff pressure (20–30 cmH₂O)
3. If self-extubated: call for help, bag-valve-mask, prepare for reintubation
4. NEVER leave alarm silenced without checking cause

4. Sedation & Paralysis

📋 Sedation in Ventilated Patients

✅ Goals: Comfortable, cooperative, synchronized with ventilator, easy to awaken for neuro checks
• Propofol: Short-acting, titratable; lipid emulsion provides 1.1 kcal/mL; monitor triglycerides
• Dexmedetomidine (Precedex): Sedation without respiratory depression; patient arousable
• Midazolam: Short-acting benzodiazepine; accumulates in renal failure
• Daily sedation interruption (DSI): Lighten daily to assess neurologic status
🚨 NCLEX Trap: Propofol causes profound respiratory depression — that's why it's only used in intubated patients on mechanical ventilation. It also causes hypotension. Monitor BP closely. The "propofol infusion syndrome" (rare): metabolic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, cardiac failure, renal failure — stop propofol immediately if suspected.

5. Weaning from Mechanical Ventilation

📋 Weaning Criteria (Must Meet Before Extubation)

✅ Readiness Assessment:
• Reversal of underlying cause of respiratory failure
• Hemodynamically stable (minimal vasopressors)
• Adequate oxygenation: PaO₂/FiO₂ >150–200, PEEP ≤8, FiO₂ ≤40%
• Adequate ventilation: RR <35, no significant respiratory acidosis
• Able to initiate inspiratory effort
• No excessive secretions
• Hemoglobin adequate (>7–10 g/dL)
• Core temp <38°C
• Adequate mental status (follow commands, protect airway)
💡 Spontaneous Breathing Trial (SBT):
• CPAP 5 cmH₂O or T-piece for 30–120 minutes
• Monitor: RR, SpO₂, HR, BP, work of breathing, anxiety, diaphoresis
• Failure signs: RR >35, SpO₂ <90%, HR >140 or change >20%, SBP >180 or <90, increased anxiety/diaphoresis, accessory muscle use
• If passed: consider extubation

📋 Post-Extubation Care

✅ Nursing Priorities:
• Monitor for stridor (post-extubation edema) — racemic epinephrine if present
• Humidified O₂ (stridor risk higher with prolonged intubation)
• Encourage coughing and deep breathing
• Assess swallow before PO intake (SLP consult)
• Monitor for respiratory fatigue (reintubation may be needed)
• Voice rest if hoarse (avoid straining)

6. Common Ventilator Complications

📋 VAP (Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia)

✅ Prevention Bundle (VAP = never event):
• HOB elevated 30–45° at all times
• Daily sedation assessment and spontaneous awakening trials
• Daily oral care with chlorhexidine
• DVT prophylaxis
• Peptic ulcer prophylaxis (controversial)
• Early mobilization
• Subglottic suctioning ETT if available
🚨 NCLEX Trap: The #1 most effective VAP prevention intervention is keeping the head of the bed elevated 30–45°. This prevents aspiration of gastric contents. Oral care with chlorhexidine is also high-yield.
