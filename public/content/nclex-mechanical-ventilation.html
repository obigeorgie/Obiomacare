# Mechanical Ventilation for Nurses: A Practical NCLEX Guide ## Introduction Mechanical ventilation intimidates new nurses. The alarms, the settings, the fear of causing harm — it's overwhelming. But the NCLEX tests only what you NEED to know, not what a respiratory therapist knows. This guide covers the essentials: ventilator modes, alarm management, and patient assessment. Written by an RN who's managed ventilated patients in the ER and ICU. --- ## Ventilator Basics ### What Mechanical Ventilation Does Mechanical ventilation supports or replaces spontaneous breathing. It does NOT treat the underlying disease — it buys time while the primary problem is addressed. **Indications:** - Respiratory failure (hypoxemic or hypercapnic) - Airway protection (decreased LOC, facial trauma) - Apnea or respiratory arrest - Severe metabolic acidosis (Kussmaul breathing exhaustion) --- ## Ventilator Modes (NCLEX-High-Yield) ### Assist-Control (AC) — Most Common **How it works:** Every breath is a full machine-delivered breath. Patient can trigger extra breaths, but each gets the full tidal volume. **Settings you need to know:** - **FiO2:** Fraction of inspired oxygen (21% room air → 100%) - **Tidal Volume (Vt):** 6-8 mL/kg ideal body weight (lung protective) - **Rate:** Backup breaths per minute - **PEEP:** Positive end-expiratory pressure (keeps alveoli open) **When to use:** Most ICU patients. Full support while patient recovers. **NCLEX tip:** In AC mode, if the patient breathes over the set rate, each breath STILL gets the full tidal volume. Risk: respiratory alkalosis if anxious/hyperventilating. --- ### Synchronized Intermittent Mandatory Ventilation (SIMV) **How it works:** Set number of machine breaths. Patient can breathe spontaneously between machine breaths, but those breaths are NOT assisted. **When to use:** Weaning. Gradual transition from full support to spontaneous breathing. **NCLEX tip:** SIMV is harder work for the patient. Don't use in respiratory muscle fatigue. --- ### Pressure Support Ventilation (PSV) **How it works:** Patient initiates every breath. Ventilator provides a set pressure to augment each breath. No set rate. **When to use:** Weaning. Patient must have adequate respiratory drive. **NCLEX tip:** If the patient stops breathing in PSV... they get no breaths. Apnea alarm will sound. Have a backup plan. --- ### Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP) **How it works:** Spontaneous breathing at an elevated baseline pressure. No machine breaths. **When to use:** Sleep apnea, cardiogenic pulmonary edema, weaning. **NCLEX tip:** CPAP in CHF reduces preload and afterload. Improves oxygenation WITHOUT intubation. --- ### Bilevel Positive Airway Pressure (BiPAP) **How it works:** Two pressure levels — higher for inspiration (IPAP), lower for expiration (EPAP). **When to use:** COPD exacerbation, CHF, obesity hypoventilation, weaning. **NCLEX tip:** BiPAP is NON-INVASIVE. Patient must be: - Hemodynamically stable - Able to protect airway - Cooperative enough to tolerate mask - Without facial trauma **Contraindications:** - Decreased LOC (can't protect airway) - Facial trauma/surgery - Severe acidosis (pH < 7.25) - Hemodynamic instability --- ## Key Ventilator Settings ### FiO2 (Fraction of Inspired Oxygen) | FiO2 | Application | |------|-------------| | 21% | Room air | | 24-40% | Nasal cannula, simple mask | | 40-60% | Venti mask, non-rebreather | | 60-100% | Mechanical ventilation | **Goal:** Lowest FiO2 to maintain SpO2 > 92% (88-92% in COPD patients). **Oxygen toxicity:** FiO2 > 60% for > 24 hours can cause alveolar damage. Titrate down as tolerated. --- ### PEEP (Positive End-Expiratory Pressure) **What it does:** Keeps alveoli open at end-expiration. Improves oxygenation. Prevents atelectasis. **Normal range:** 5 cm H2O (physiologic PEEP) **Therapeutic range:** 5-15 cm H2O **High PEEP risks:** - Barotrauma (pneumothorax) - Decreased venous return → decreased cardiac output - Increased intracranial pressure **NCLEX scenario:** > "A ventilated patient's SpO2 drops to 86% despite 100% FiO2. The respiratory therapist increases PEEP from 5 to 12 cm H2O. What should the nurse monitor?" **Answer:** Blood pressure and hemodynamics. High PEEP decreases venous return. Watch for hypotension. --- ### Tidal Volume (Vt) **Lung protective strategy:** 6 mL/kg ideal body weight **Why low tidal volume?** Prevents volutrauma and barotrauma in ARDS. **Plateau pressure:** Should be < 30 cm H2O. Higher = risk of lung injury. --- ## Ventilator Alarms: What They Mean and What to Do ### High Pressure Alarm **Causes:** - Patient coughing or bucking ventilator - Kinked or occluded tubing - Water in tubing - Worsening lung compliance (pulmonary edema, pneumonia) - Bronchospasm - Pneumothorax **First action:** Check the patient, then the circuit. Look for: - Visible distress - Asymmetric chest expansion (pneumothorax?) - Tubing kinks - Water condensation **NCLEX tip:** NEVER silence a high-pressure alarm without identifying the cause. --- ### Low Pressure Alarm **Causes:** - Disconnected circuit - Cuff leak (ET tube or trach) - Tubing leak **First action:** Check connections. If disconnected — reconnect manually (bag valve mask if needed). **NCLEX scenario:** > "The ventilator alarms 'low pressure.' The nurse finds the tubing disconnected from the ET tube. What is the FIRST action?" **Answer:** Reconnect the tubing. The patient was receiving room air (or no air). Immediate reconnection restores ventilation. Then assess the patient. --- ### Apnea Alarm **Causes:** - Patient not breathing (PSV/CPAP modes) - Sedation too deep - Disconnected from ventilator **First action:** Assess patient. Stimulate. If no breathing — manual bag valve mask ventilation. Call for help. --- ### Low Minute Ventilation Alarm **Minute ventilation = Tidal volume × Rate** **Causes:** - Decreased respiratory rate (oversedation, CNS issue) - Decreased tidal volume (weak respiratory muscles) - Circuit leak **First action:** Assess respiratory effort. Check sedation level. ABG if indicated. --- ## Patient Assessment: The Ventilated Patient ### Every 2-4 Hours (or more frequently) 1. **Respiratory assessment** - Rate, rhythm, effort - SpO2 and EtCO2 (if available) - Breath sounds (bilateral? equal?) - Chest expansion (symmetric?) 2. **Ventilator settings** - FiO2, rate, Vt, PEEP, pressures - Verify settings match orders 3. **Tube/line check** - ET tube position (mark at teeth/lip) - Cuff pressure (20-30 cm H2O) - Tubing connections secure - Condensation emptied from tubing 4. **ABG analysis** (per protocol or PRN) - pH, PaCO2, PaO2, HCO3, lactate - Compare to previous --- ### Daily Weaning Assessment **Before considering weaning, patient must meet criteria:** **REQUIRED:** - Original problem resolving - Hemodynamically stable (no or minimal pressors) - Spontaneous respiratory effort present - Adequate oxygenation on acceptable FiO2/PEEP **PREFERRED:** - ABG within acceptable range - Hemoglobin > 8-10 g/dL - No significant electrolyte abnormalities - Core temperature < 38°C - Adequate mental status (follows commands) **Weaning methods:** 1. Spontaneous breathing trial (SBT) — most common 2. Progressive pressure support weaning 3. SIMV rate reduction **NCLEX tip:** The MOST reliable indicator of readiness to extubate is a successful spontaneous breathing trial. --- ## Complications of Mechanical Ventilation ### Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia (VAP) **Prevention bundle:** - Head of bed 30-45 degrees - Oral chlorhexidine care every 12 hours - Daily sedation interruption - Daily spontaneous breathing trial - DVT prophylaxis - Peptic ulcer prophylaxis **NCLEX tip:** All of the above are evidence-based VAP prevention. Know this bundle. --- ### Barotrauma **Types:** - Pneumothorax - Pneumomediastinum - Subcutaneous emphysema **Signs:** - Sudden increase in peak airway pressure - Asymmetric chest expansion - Absent breath sounds on one side - Subcutaneous crepitus - Hypotension, tachycardia (tension pneumothorax) **Action:** If tension pneumothorax suspected — needle decompression THEN chest tube. --- ### Oxygen Toxicity **Risk:** FiO2 > 60% for > 24 hours **Effects:** - Tracheobronchitis - Alveolar damage - ARDS-like picture **Prevention:** Titrate FiO2 to lowest level maintaining SpO2 > 92%. --- ### Ventilator-Induced Lung Injury (VILI) **Mechanisms:** - Volutrauma (overdistention) - Barotrauma (high pressures) - Atelectrauma (repeated opening/closing of alveoli) - Biotrauma (inflammatory response) **Prevention:** Lung protective ventilation — low tidal volumes (6 mL/kg), limit plateau pressure < 30. --- ## NCLEX-Style Practice Questions **Question 1:** > "A patient on AC mode with rate 14, Vt 500, FiO2 40%, PEEP 5 starts breathing at a rate of 28. What is the nurse's FIRST action?" **Answer:** Assess the patient. Increased respiratory rate could indicate: - Pain - Anxiety - Hypoxia - Metabolic acidosis - Worsening condition Check SpO2, auscultate lungs, assess for distress. Then consider sedation or analgesia. --- **Question 2:** > "The ventilator alarms 'high pressure.' The patient is anxious, fighting the ventilator, and has bilateral wheezing. What is the FIRST action?" **Answer:** Manual ventilation with bag valve mask while troubleshooting. Patient is in respiratory distress with bronchospasm. Need to: 1. Manually ventilate (100% O2) 2. Call RT/provider 3. Possible bronchodilator (albuterol) 4. Consider sedation if fighting ventilator --- **Question 3:** > "A patient has been successfully weaned to PSV and is breathing spontaneously. The apnea alarm sounds. The patient is unresponsive. What is the FIRST action?" **Answer:** Manually ventilate with bag valve mask. Then: 1. Call for help 2. Assess airway (is ET tube in place?) 3. Check sedation level 4. ABG 5. Consider reintubation if apnea continues --- **Question 4:** > "Which action is PRIORITY for preventing ventilator-associated pneumonia?" **Answer:** Elevate head of bed to 30-45 degrees. This prevents aspiration of oropharyngeal secretions — the primary mechanism of VAP. --- ## Quick Reference: Ventilator Settings | Parameter | Normal/Lung Protective | Notes | |-----------|------------------------|-------| | Tidal Volume | 6 mL/kg IBW | Prevents volutrauma | | Plateau Pressure | < 30 cm H2O | Prevents barotrauma | | PEEP | 5-15 cm H2O | Prevents atelectasis | | FiO2 | Lowest to keep SpO2 > 92% | Prevents oxygen toxicity | | I:E Ratio | 1:2 to 1:3 | Longer expiration in obstructive disease | | Cuff Pressure | 20-30 cm H2O | Prevents aspiration and tracheal damage | --- ## Summary Mechanical ventilation is supportive care, not treatment. Your job as a nurse: 1. **Monitor** — Patient first, then numbers 2. **Troubleshoot** — Alarms mean something. Find out what. 3. **Prevent complications** — VAP bundle, DVT prophylaxis, pressure injury prevention 4. **Prepare for weaning** — Daily assessment, spontaneous breathing trials 5. **Communicate** — With RT, provider, and family The NCLEX tests your understanding of WHY, not just WHAT. Why low tidal volume? Why head elevation? Why daily SBTs?
