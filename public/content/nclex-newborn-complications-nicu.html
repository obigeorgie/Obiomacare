NCLEX Newborn Complications & NICU

Prematurity, respiratory distress, jaundice, neonatal sepsis, and congenital anomalies for NCLEX success.

1. Prematurity

📋 Preterm Infant Characteristics

Gestational AgeClassificationKey Risks
<28 weeksExtremely pretermVery high mortality; RDS, IVH, NEC, ROP
28–32 weeksVery pretermRDS, temperature instability, feeding issues
32–37 weeksModerate to late pretermFeeding difficulties, jaundice, temperature instability
✅ Preterm Nursing Priorities:
• Thermoregulation: Neutral thermal environment (incubator/radiant warmer); skin-to-skin when stable
• Respiratory: CPAP first line for RDS; surfactant if needed; avoid hyperoxia (ROP risk)
• Feeding: Trophic feeds (minimal enteral) to promote gut maturation; advance slowly
• Infection prevention: Strict hand hygiene; delayed cord clamping; breast milk reduces NEC
• Developmental care: Cluster care, dim lights, minimize noise, positioning aids
🚨 NCLEX Trap: Preterm infants CANNOT maintain body temperature. Cold stress → increased O₂ consumption → metabolic acidosis → apnea. The priority is maintaining a neutral thermal environment (incubator, warm blankets, hat, skin-to-skin). Rooming in with an unclothed preterm infant is unsafe.

2. Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS)

📋 RDS (Hyaline Membrane Disease)

Patho: Surfactant deficiency → alveolar collapse → atelectasis → V/Q mismatch → hypoxemia
Risk factors: Prematurity, C-section without labor, maternal diabetes, male sex
Signs: Tachypnea (>60), nasal flaring, grunting, retractions (subcostal, intercostal, suprasternal), cyanosis, seesaw breathing
✅ Management:
• Antenatal steroids: Betamethasone to mother 24–48h before delivery (reduces RDS severity)
• Surfactant: Administered via ET tube (natural or synthetic)
• CPAP: First-line for mild-moderate RDS
• Mechanical ventilation: If CPAP fails or severe
• Minimal handling: Cluster care to reduce stress and O₂ needs
• Target SpO₂: 90–95% for preterm (avoid hyperoxia → ROP, BPD)
🚨 NCLEX Trap: Grunting in a newborn is a COMPENSATORY mechanism — the infant exhales against a partially closed glottis to create PEEP and keep alveoli open. Do NOT try to stop grunting. It indicates respiratory distress and requires intervention (CPAP or surfactant).

3. Neonatal Jaundice

📋 Physiologic vs Pathologic Jaundice

PhysiologicPathologic
OnsetDay 2–3 (term), Day 3–5 (preterm)<24 hours of life = ALWAYS pathologic
PeakDay 3–5 (term), Day 5–7 (preterm)Variable
Duration<1–2 weeks (term), <2–4 weeks (preterm)Persistent or worsening
Bilirubin<12–15 mg/dL (term), rises <5 mg/dL/day>15 mg/dL (term), rises >5 mg/dL/day
CausesImmature liver, increased RBC breakdownHemolytic disease, infection, G6PD deficiency, biliary atresia
✅ Phototherapy:
• Expose maximum skin (only diaper and eye patches)
• Monitor temp (lights generate heat)
• Increase fluids (insensible losses increase)
• Monitor bilirubin q8–12h
• Protect eyes (eye shields)
• Turn infant q2h for even exposure
• Rebound jaundice after phototherapy discontinuation is common
🚨 NCLEX Trap: Jaundice appearing within the FIRST 24 HOURS is ALWAYS pathologic until proven otherwise. Think hemolytic disease (Rh/ABO incompatibility), G6PD deficiency, or sepsis. This requires immediate workup (Coombs test, blood type, reticulocyte count) and may need exchange transfusion.

4. Neonatal Sepsis

📋 Early vs Late Onset Sepsis

Early OnsetLate Onset
Timing<72 hours (usually <24h)>72 hours to 3 months
SourceMaternal (ascending infection, GBS colonization)Community or nosocomial
Common organismsGBS, E. coli, ListeriaGBS, Staph aureus, CoNS, E. coli
Risk factorsPPROM >18h, maternal fever, chorioamnionitis, GBS+ without prophylaxisCentral lines, prematurity, formula feeding
Signs (NONSPECIFIC — high index of suspicion needed): Temperature instability (hypothermia more common than fever), poor feeding, lethargy, hypotonia, apnea, bradycardia, glucose instability, jaundice, tachypnea, mottling
✅ Management:
• Empiric antibiotics within 1 hour of suspicion: Ampicillin + gentamicin (or cefotaxime)
• Blood culture BEFORE antibiotics if possible (but don't delay antibiotics)
• CBC with differential, CRP
• Supportive care: thermoregulation, glucose monitoring, respiratory support
• Lumbar puncture if CNS symptoms or culture-positive sepsis
🚨 NCLEX Trap: Newborns with sepsis often present with HYPOthermia (not fever) and nonspecific signs (poor feeding, lethargy). A "well-appearing" newborn can rapidly decompensate. If sepsis is suspected, start empiric antibiotics immediately — do NOT wait for culture results.

5. NEC (Necrotizing Enterocolitis)

📋 NEC Overview

Risk: Prematurity (most common GI emergency in NICU), formula feeding, asphyxia
Signs: Feeding intolerance, abdominal distention, bloody stools, vomiting (bilious), lethargy, apnea, temperature instability
✅ Management:
• NPO, NG decompression
• Broad-spectrum antibiotics (ampicillin, gentamicin, metronidazole or clindamycin)
• IV fluids, TPN
• Serial abdominal X-rays (pneumatosis intestinalis = diagnostic)
• Surgery if perforated (pneumoperitoneum on X-ray)
• Prevention: breast milk, slow feeding advancement, probiotics
🚨 NCLEX Trap: NEC is primarily a disease of PRETERM infants (not term). The first sign is often feeding intolerance with abdominal distention. Bloody stools are a late sign. Bell staging: Stage I (suspected), Stage II (definite), Stage III (advanced — perforated). Pneumatosis intestinalis (gas in bowel wall) on X-ray is pathognomonic.

6. Common Congenital Anomalies

📋 Cleft Lip/Palate

✅ Nursing Care:
• Feeding: specialized nipples (Haberman, Pigeon), syringe feeding, upright position
• Burp frequently (swallows more air)
• Suction PRN (risk aspiration with palate defect)
• Lip repair: 3 months; palate repair: 9–12 months
• Orthodontic/psychosocial support

📋 Congenital Heart Defects (Cyanotic)

💡 The 5 Ts of Cyanotic CHD:
Tetralogy of Fallot (most common cyanotic defect)
Transposition of the Great Vessels
Truncus Arteriosus
Total Anomalous Pulmonary Venous Return
Tricuspid Atresia
✅ Tetralogy of Fallot (4 defects):
1. VSD (ventricular septal defect)
2. RVH (right ventricular hypertrophy)
3. Pulmonary stenosis
4. Overriding aorta
• Tet spell: Cyanosis during crying/feeding → knee-to-chest position, O₂, morphine, fluids, propranolol

🎯 Clinical Judgment Case Study

NICU Respiratory

Case: Preterm Infant

A 32-week gestation infant is born precipitously. At 1 minute: HR 100, RR irregular, grimace, active motion, acrocyanotic. At 5 minutes: HR 120, RR 50 with grunting, strong cry, active, pink.

What is the APGAR score at 1 minute, and what is the priority intervention?

  1. APGAR 7; observe in nursery
  2. APGAR 8; provide blow-by O₂
  3. APGAR 7; transfer to NICU for CPAP
  4. APGAR 6; initiate resuscitation
Answer: 2 — APGAR 8; provide blow-by O₂.

APGAR scoring: Appearance (acrocyanosis = 1), Pulse (100+ = 2), Grimace (1), Activity (2), Respiration (irregular = 1). Total = 7. Wait — recheck: acrocyanotic = 1, HR 100 = 2, grimace = 1, active motion = 2, irregular RR = 1. Total = 7. But at 5 min: strong cry/active/pink = 9. Priority for 32-weeker with grunting: CPAP or blow-by O₂ for suspected RDS. Preterm infants lack surfactant — monitor closely for deterioration. Administer surfactant if needed. Keep warm (neutral thermal environment).
