NCLEX Nursing Fundamentals: The Complete Exam Guide

Vital signs, safety, infection control, hygiene, mobility, and comfort. The foundational concepts that appear on every NCLEX — mastered with clinical scenarios.

By Nnamdi Okorafor, RN · 15-year ER nurse · Updated August 2026 · Obioma

Why fundamentals matter on the NCLEX: 15-20% of the exam covers nursing fundamentals. The NGN case studies embed these concepts in clinical scenarios — you need to recognize abnormal vitals, apply infection control, and prioritize safety without thinking.

Vital Signs: Beyond the Numbers

Vital signs aren't just numbers to document — they're your first clue that something is wrong. The NCLEX tests whether you can interpret them, not just measure them.

NCLEX Tip — Orthostatic Vitals: A drop of ≥20 mmHg systolic or ≥10 mmHg diastolic within 3 minutes of standing = orthostatic hypotension. Common causes: dehydration, blood loss, autonomic dysfunction, antihypertensives. First action: lie patient flat, raise legs.

What Abnormal Vitals Tell You

Tachycardia (HR >100)

  • Compensatory: Hypovolemia, anemia, fever, pain, anxiety
  • Pathological: Arrhythmia, heart failure, sepsis, PE
  • Pharmacological: Bronchodilators, stimulants, withdrawal

Your action: Check BP. If hypotensive + tachycardic = shock until proven otherwise.

Bradycardia (HR <60)

  • Athletic: Normal in trained athletes
  • Pathological: Heart block, digoxin toxicity, increased ICP, hypothyroidism
  • Pharmacological: Beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers

Your action: If symptomatic (dizzy, syncope, hypotensive) → atropine 0.5mg IV. If from digoxin → hold dose, check level.

Tachypnea (RR >20)

  • Compensatory: Metabolic acidosis (Kussmaul), anxiety, pain, fever
  • Pathological: Hypoxia, PE, pneumonia, pulmonary edema
  • Neurological: Increased ICP, anxiety disorders

Your action: Check SpO₂ and ABG. If SpO₂ <92% → apply O₂, prepare for escalation.

Infection Control: Standard & Transmission-Based Precautions

Infection control questions are guaranteed on the NCLEX. The exam tests whether you know which precaution for which pathogen and what PPE is required.

Standard Precautions (Universal)

Used for ALL patients, always:

  • Hand hygiene (soap & water or alcohol-based sanitizer)
  • Gloves when touching blood, body fluids, secretions, excretions, broken skin, mucous membranes
  • Gown if clothing may be soiled
  • Mask + eye protection if splash/spray risk
  • Safe needle practices; never recap

Transmission-Based Precautions

TypePathogensRequired PPERoom Requirements
ContactMRSA, VRE, C. diff, RSV, norovirus, scabies, impetigoGloves + gownPrivate room; dedicated equipment
DropletInfluenza, pertussis, mumps, rubella, meningococcus, adenovirus, rhinovirusSurgical mask (within 3 feet)Private room; door may stay open
AirborneTB, measles, varicella (chickenpox/shingles), COVID-19 (in some settings)N95 or PAPRNegative pressure room; door closed

NCLEX Tip — C. diff is the exception: C. diff requires soap and water (not alcohol sanitizer) because alcohol doesn't kill spores. Also requires contact precautions with gown and gloves.

NCLEX Tip — Donning vs. Doffing Order:
Don (put on): Gown → Mask → Goggles → Gloves
Doff (remove): Gloves → Goggles → Gown → Mask
Hand hygiene before AND after.

Patient Placement Priority

When assigning rooms with limited private rooms:

  1. Airborne pathogens get priority (negative pressure rooms)
  2. Same organisms can room together (cohorting)
  3. Immunocompromised patients should NOT room with patients on precautions
  4. Patients with the same droplet pathogen can share a room

Safety: Falls, Restraints & Seizure Precautions

Fall Risk Assessment

All patients are screened for fall risk on admission. High-risk patients get:

  • Bed in lowest position
  • Call light within reach
  • Non-skid footwear
  • Hourly rounding
  • Bed alarm if needed
  • Assist with toileting

NCLEX Tip — Fall Prevention Priority: A confused patient trying to get out of bed = highest priority. Sit with patient, reorient, eliminate restraints if possible (restraints increase fall risk with injury).

Restraint Guidelines

  • Last resort — only when less restrictive measures have failed
  • Requires physician order (renewed every 24 hours)
  • Check circulation every 15 minutes (q2h for non-violent)
  • Never restrain in prone position
  • Document rationale, alternatives attempted, and monitoring

Seizure Precautions

  • Pad side rails
  • Bed in lowest position
  • Oxygen and suction at bedside
  • IV access maintained
  • Never force anything into mouth during seizure
  • Turn patient to side during seizure (protect airway)
  • Time the seizure

Mobility & Body Mechanics

Pressure Injury Prevention

Turn/reposition every 2 hours for bedbound patients. Check:

  • Sacrum, heels, hips, elbows, shoulder blades
  • Use pressure-relieving mattress
  • Keep skin clean and dry
  • Adequate nutrition (protein, vitamin C, zinc)

Body Mechanics for Patient Handling

  • Keep back straight, bend knees
  • Hold weight close to body
  • Don't twist — pivot with feet
  • Use assistive devices (lift, slide board) for patients >35 lbs
  • Get help — never lift alone if unsure

Hygiene & Comfort

Bathing Priorities

  • Perineal care: front to back (prevents UTI)
  • Cleanest to dirtiest: face → arms → chest → back → perineum
  • Complete bed bath for immobile patients
  • Partial bath for patients who can wash themselves

Oral Care

  • Brush twice daily (denture care too)
  • Unconscious patients: side-lying, small amount of water/foam swabs, suction available
  • Ventilated patients: chlorhexidine oral rinse reduces VAP

Specimen Collection & Diagnostic Testing

Blood Culture Collection

  • Two sets from different sites (10ml each)
  • Clean site with chlorhexidine or alcohol (let dry)
  • Don't draw through existing IV line
  • Label at bedside
  • Transport to lab within 2 hours

Urine Specimen Collection

  • Clean catch midstream: Clean perineum, start stream, collect midstream, finish
  • Catheter specimen: Clean port with alcohol, aspirate with sterile syringe, NOT from drainage bag
  • 24-hour urine: Discard first void, collect all urine for 24 hours including final void

Documentation Principles

The NCLEX tests whether you know what to document and when:

  • Objective data only — what you see, hear, feel, measure
  • Quote patient directly for subjective data
  • Document immediately after care (never before)
  • Don't use "appears," "seems," "apparently" — be specific
  • Correct errors with a single line through, write "error," initial, date, time — never use white-out
  • Never document for someone else

Charting by exception: Only document abnormal findings. Common in long-term care. The NCLEX may ask when this is appropriate — it's efficient for stable patients with routine care.

🧩 NGN Case Study: Post-Operative Nursing Priorities

Client Scenario

Mrs. Davis, 72-year-old female, is on the medical-surgical unit post-op day 2 after a total knee replacement. She has a history of hypertension (metoprolol 50mg BID), type 2 diabetes (metformin 1000mg BID), and osteoporosis. She lives alone. Her daughter visited this morning and reports her mother "seems more confused than usual."

Vital Signs Physical Assessment Current Orders

Vital Signs: BP 148/92, HR 96, RR 22, Temp 37.9°C (100.2°F), SpO₂ 93% on room air, Pain 6/10

Physical Assessment: Alert but oriented ×2 (person, place). Mild confusion noted — asks same questions repeatedly. Incision clean, dry, intact with moderate serosanguinous drainage on dressing. Left lower extremity with mild edema, warm, pedal pulse palpable. Lungs clear bilaterally with diminished bases. Last bowel movement was 2 days ago pre-op.

Current Orders: DVT prophylaxis (enoxaparin 40mg SQ daily), physical therapy twice daily, IS 10× every hour while awake, morphine 2mg IV q4h PRN pain, acetaminophen 650mg PO q6h PRN, regular diet, Foley catheter to gravity drainage

Question 1 — Recognize Cues: Which assessment finding should the nurse address first?

  • A. Pain rated 6/10
  • B. SpO₂ 93% on room air
  • C. Temperature 37.9°C
  • D. Last bowel movement 2 days ago

Correct: B. SpO₂ 93% on room air

Airway and breathing take priority over all other needs. SpO₂ of 93% indicates hypoxemia in a post-op patient (likely atelectasis from immobility and shallow breathing). Apply supplemental oxygen, encourage deep breathing/IS use, and assess for pulmonary complications (PE, pneumonia). Pain, low-grade fever, and constipation are important but not immediately life-threatening.

Question 2 — Analyze Cues: The nurse applies 2L NC oxygen and SpO₂ improves to 97%. The patient remains confused. Which factor is the most likely cause of her confusion?

  • A. Hypoxemia from atelectasis
  • B. Post-operative delirium from anesthesia
  • C. Urinary tract infection
  • D. Hypoglycemia from NPO status and metformin

Correct: A. Hypoxemia from atelectasis

The patient's SpO₂ was 93% on room air, indicating significant hypoxemia. Even mild hypoxemia can cause confusion, especially in elderly patients. Now that O₂ is applied and SpO₂ improved, reassess mental status. If confusion persists after oxygenation is corrected, investigate further (check glucose, assess for infection, review medications). Post-op delirium is possible but hypoxemia is the most immediate and likely cause.

Question 3 — Prioritize Hypotheses: The nurse notes the Foley catheter bag has 80mL of dark, concentrated urine over the past 4 hours. What is the priority nursing action?

  • A. Increase IV fluid rate
  • B. Assess for urinary retention and kinked tubing
  • C. Obtain a urine culture
  • D. Document and continue monitoring

Correct: B. Assess for urinary retention and kinked tubing

Low urine output (<30 mL/hr) in a post-op patient is concerning. First, assess for mechanical causes: kinked tubing, clamped catheter, or the catheter displaced from the bladder. Also palpate the bladder for distension. If the catheter is patent and the bladder is empty, consider dehydration or renal issues. If the bladder is distended and the catheter is not draining, the catheter may need repositioning or replacement.

Question 4 — Generate Solutions: The patient reports she "can't sleep" and is requesting her sleeping pill from home. She also mentions she's worried about falling when she gets home. Which nursing interventions are most appropriate? Select all that apply.

  • A. Administer her home sleeping medication
  • B. Implement non-pharmacologic sleep hygiene measures
  • C. Reassure her that falls are unlikely
  • D. Discuss home safety assessment with PT/OT
  • E. Provide education on using a walker and shower chair

Correct: B, D, E

B: Yes — Non-pharmacologic sleep measures (quiet environment, back rub, warm milk) are preferred over hypnotics, especially in elderly patients at risk for falls and delirium.
D: Yes — Addressing her fall concern proactively with PT/OT is appropriate discharge planning. Falls are a major cause of injury in elderly patients with joint replacements.
E: Yes — Assistive devices are standard for post-op joint replacement patients.
A: No — Don't give home medications without provider order. Also, sleeping pills increase fall risk in elderly.
C: No — False reassurance is not therapeutic. Address concerns with concrete interventions.

Question 5 — Take Action: The patient is scheduled for discharge tomorrow. Which discharge instruction is the highest priority?

  • A. "You can drive once you feel comfortable."
  • B. "Call your surgeon if your incision becomes red, warm, or drains pus."
  • C. "Take your blood pressure medication every morning."
  • D. "You can resume your usual diet without restrictions."

Correct: B. "Call your surgeon if your incision becomes red, warm, or drains pus."

Post-op infection is a serious complication. Teaching the patient signs of infection (redness, warmth, purulent drainage, fever) and when to call empowers self-monitoring. While BP medication adherence and diet are important, infection recognition is the highest priority because delayed treatment can lead to septic arthritis or prosthetic joint infection. Option A is incorrect — no driving for 4-6 weeks post TKR (reaction time/braking ability compromised).

Question 6 — Evaluate Outcomes: On day 3, the patient is afebrile, SpO₂ 97% on room air, and pain is 2/10. She ambulated 75 feet with a walker. What is the nurse's priority focus for the remainder of the shift?

  • A. Continue administering PRN morphine every 4 hours
  • B. Ensure bowel regimen is effective
  • C. Focus on discharge teaching and home safety
  • D. Request a psychiatric consult for anxiety

Correct: C. Focus on discharge teaching and home safety

The patient is stable and progressing well. With discharge planned, the priority shifts to ensuring she has the knowledge and resources for safe recovery at home. This includes wound care, medication management, activity restrictions, fall prevention, and when to seek help. Bowel regimen is important but not the priority. Morphine PRN should be minimized as pain improves. No indication for psych consult.
