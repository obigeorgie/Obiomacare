OB Labor and Delivery Stages: NCLEX Study Guide

Introduction

Labor and delivery questions appear on every NCLEX. The exam tests your ability to recognize normal progression, identify complications, and know when to intervene. This guide covers the four stages of labor, fetal heart rate monitoring, and emergency scenarios — with the clinical judgment focus the NGN demands.

Stage 1: Dilation and Effacement

Duration:

  • Nulliparous (first baby): 12-18 hours
  • Multiparous: 6-8 hours

What's happening: Cervix dilates (opens) to 10 cm and effaces (thins) to 100%.

Phase 1: Early (Latent) Labor

Cervical change: 0-3 cm

Contractions:

  • Frequency: Every 5-30 minutes
  • Duration: 30-45 seconds
  • Intensity: Mild to moderate

Patient presentation:

  • Excited, talkative
  • Can walk, talk, breathe through contractions
  • Bloody show common (normal)

Nursing care:

  • Admit if contractions regular and cervical change confirmed
  • If not admitted: Teach when to return (contractions 5 minutes apart, 1 minute long, for 1 hour = 5-1-1 rule)
  • Hydration, ambulation, comfort measures
  • External fetal monitoring (intermittent or continuous)

NCLEX tip: Don't admit too early. Early labor at home reduces interventions and costs.

Phase 2: Active Labor

Cervical change: 4-7 cm

Contractions:

  • Frequency: Every 3-5 minutes
  • Duration: 45-60 seconds
  • Intensity: Moderate to strong

Patient presentation:

  • Focused inward
  • Needs coaching with breathing
  • May request pain medication

Nursing care:

  • Continuous fetal monitoring
  • Pain management (epidural, IV analgesia, non-pharmacologic)
  • Position changes
  • Support person involvement
  • Assess progression every 1-2 hours

Expected progression:

  • Nulliparous: ~1 cm/hour
  • Multiparous: ~1.5 cm/hour

NCLEX red flag: < 1 cm dilation in 2 hours = protracted active phase → evaluate for:

  • Inadequate contractions (assess with intrauterine pressure catheter)
  • Cephalopelvic disproportion (CPD)
  • Malposition (occiput posterior)
  • Need for augmentation (Pitocin)

Phase 3: Transition

Cervical change: 8-10 cm

Contractions:

  • Frequency: Every 2-3 minutes
  • Duration: 60-90 seconds
  • Intensity: Strong to very strong

Patient presentation:

  • Irritable, restless
  • Nausea, vomiting common
  • Shivering
  • Feeling of rectal pressure
  • May say "I can't do this" or "I need to push"

Nursing care:

  • Intense support and coaching
  • DON'T push yet if cervix not fully dilated
  • Prepare for delivery
  • Encourage focal point and breathing
  • Anticipate delivery within 1-3 hours (nulliparous) or <1 hour (multiparous)

NCLEX tip: Transition is the hardest phase emotionally. The patient needs your reassurance more than anything.

Stage 2: Pushing and Birth

Begins: Full dilation (10 cm)
Ends: Birth of baby

Duration:

  • Nulliparous: Up to 3 hours (2 hours with epidural)
  • Multiparous: Up to 2 hours (1 hour with epidural)

Pushing Techniques

Coordinated pushing:

  • Push with contraction
  • Take a deep breath, hold, bear down
  • Push for 6-8 seconds
  • Exhale, take another breath, push again
  • 3 pushes per contraction

Laboring down (with epidural):

  • Wait for fetal descent before pushing
  • May delay pushing 1-2 hours after full dilation
  • Reduces pushing time and fatigue

Nursing care:

  • Coach pushing
  • Monitor fetal heart rate (may drop with pushing — usually recovers)
  • Assess perineum for crowning
  • Prepare for delivery
  • Position changes (squatting, side-lying, hands-and-knees)

NCLEX scenario:

"A nulliparous patient has been pushing for 2.5 hours. The fetal head is visible at the introitus but recedes between contractions. What is the nurse's FIRST action?"

Answer: Notify the provider. This is prolonged second stage (>3 hours nulliparous, >2 hours multiparous). May need vacuum-assisted delivery, forceps, or cesarean section.

Stage 3: Delivery of Placenta

Duration: 5-30 minutes

Signs of placental separation:

  1. Gush of blood from vagina
  2. Lengthening of umbilical cord
  3. Fundus rises and becomes firm, globular
  4. Uterine fundus changes shape

Active management (evidence-based):

  • Oxytocin (Pitocin) IV immediately after delivery
  • Controlled cord traction
  • Uterine massage
  • Reduces postpartum hemorrhage risk

Expectant management:

  • Wait for signs of separation
  • Then deliver placenta by maternal effort
  • Used in low-risk deliveries per patient preference

NCLEX critical action: Inspect placenta for completeness. Retained placental fragments cause postpartum hemorrhage.

Stage 4: Recovery

Duration: First 2-4 hours postpartum

Priorities:

  1. Uterine tone — Firm fundus at or below umbilicus
  2. Bleeding — Moderate lochia (rubra) normal
  3. Vital signs — Watch for hemorrhage, infection
  4. Perineum — Assess for hematoma, laceration
  5. Bladder — Must void within 6-8 hours

BUBBLE-HE assessment:

  • Breasts
  • Uterus (fundus)
  • Bladder
  • Bowel
  • Lochia
  • Episiotomy/perineum
  • Hemorrhoids
  • Emotional status

NCLEX red flag: Fundus boggy and above umbilicus → full bladder or uterine atony. Massage fundus and encourage voiding.

Fetal Heart Rate Monitoring

Normal FHR Patterns

Parameter Normal
Baseline rate 110-160 bpm
Variability Moderate (6-25 bpm fluctuation)
Accelerations Present (≥15 bpm for ≥15 seconds)
Decelerations Early decelerations (normal)

Abnormal FHR Patterns: What They Mean

Late Decelerations — MOST CONCERNING

  • Pattern: FHR drop begins AFTER peak of contraction, returns to baseline AFTER contraction ends
  • Shape: Uniform, gradual
  • Cause: Uteroplacental insufficiency (placenta not delivering enough O2)
  • Action:
  1. Turn patient to left side
  2. Increase IV fluids
  3. Give supplemental O2 (10L via non-rebreather)
  4. Stop Pitocin if running
  5. Notify provider
  6. Prepare for rapid delivery if persistent

NCLEX tip: Late decelerations = placental insufficiency = fetal hypoxia. This is an emergency.

Variable Decelerations

  • Pattern: Abrupt drop, abrupt return, variable shape
  • Cause: Cord compression
  • Action:
  1. Change maternal position (side, knee-chest, Trendelenburg)
  2. Assess for cord prolapse (if severe)
  3. Amnioinfusion if recurrent
  4. Notify provider

NCLEX tip: Variable decels = cord compression. Usually resolves with position change.

Early Decelerations

  • Pattern: FHR drop mirrors contraction (starts with contraction, ends with contraction)
  • Cause: Head compression (normal during pushing)
  • Action: None. Normal finding.

Prolonged Deceleration

  • Definition: Drop ≥15 bpm lasting 2-10 minutes
  • Causes: Cord compression, placental abruption, uterine rupture, hypotension
  • Action: Immediate assessment and intervention based on cause

FHR Variability

Variability Meaning
Absent Fetal sleep cycle, CNS depression, acidosis
Minimal (<6 bpm) Fetal sleep, medications, hypoxia
Moderate (6-25 bpm) Normal, healthy fetus
Marked (>25 bpm) Early hypoxia, fetal stimulation

NCLEX tip: Decreased variability + late decelerations = fetal acidosis. Prepare for delivery.

Complications During Labor

Cord Prolapse

Emergency. Umbilical cord slips past fetal head, compressed between head and pelvis.

Signs:

  • Sudden severe variable deceleration or bradycardia
  • Cord visible at vagina

Immediate action (nurse can do NOW):

  1. Call for help
  2. Push presenting part OFF cord with fingers (knee-chest or Trendelenburg position)
  3. Give O2
  4. Stop Pitocin
  5. Prepare for emergency cesarean

NCLEX tip: This is a "call the provider AFTER you've done the immediate life-saving actions" scenario.

Uterine Rupture

Risk factors: Prior uterine surgery (especially classical cesarean), trauma, prolonged labor with CPD.

Signs:

  • Sudden severe abdominal pain
  • Chest pain (referred)
  • Absent contractions
  • Loss of fetal station
  • Fetal bradycardia
  • Vaginal bleeding (may be minimal)

Action:

  1. Call for help
  2. Large-bore IVs
  3. Fluid resuscitation
  4. Type and crossmatch
  5. Emergency cesarean
  6. Prepare for hemorrhage

Shoulder Dystocia

Definition: Fetal head delivers but shoulders stuck behind pubic symphysis.

Risk factors: Macrosomia, diabetes, maternal obesity, prolonged second stage.

Emergency maneuvers (nurse assists):

  1. McRoberts maneuver (hyperflex maternal thighs to abdomen)
  2. Suprapubic pressure (NOT fundal pressure)
  3. Episiotomy if needed
  4. Assist with internal maneuvers (provider)

NCLEX tip: NEVER apply fundal pressure in shoulder dystocia. Makes it worse.

Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH)

Definition: Blood loss >500 mL vaginal, >1000 mL cesarean within 24 hours

Causes (4 Ts):

  1. Tone (uterine atony) — 70% of cases
  2. Trauma (lacerations, hematoma)
  3. Tissue (retained placenta)
  4. Thrombin (coagulopathy)

Signs:

  • Boggy uterus
  • Heavy bleeding
  • Tachycardia
  • Hypotension (late sign)

Action:

  1. Massage fundus
  2. Oxytocin (first line)
  3. Methergine (if no hypertension)
  4. Hemabate (if no asthma)
  5. Cytotec (rectal or oral)
  6. Large-bore IV, fluid resuscitation
  7. Type and crossmatch
  8. Notify provider

NCLEX tip: Uterine atony = boggy uterus. Massage until firm. May express clots.

Pain Management in Labor

Non-Pharmacologic

  • Breathing techniques
  • Position changes
  • Massage, counterpressure
  • Hydrotherapy (shower, tub)
  • Heat/cold
  • Music, imagery
  • Continuous labor support

Pharmacologic

IV Opioids (short-term relief):

  • Fentanyl, Nubain, Stadol
  • Cross placenta → fetal respiratory depression risk
  • Give 1 hour before expected delivery

Epidural Analgesia:

  • Gold standard for labor pain
  • Provides segmental block (T10-L1 for labor, S2-S4 for delivery)
  • Does NOT cause cesarean section (evidence-based)
  • Risks: hypotension, itching, urinary retention, prolonged second stage

NCLEX tip: After epidural, check blood pressure every 5 minutes × 30 minutes. Hypotension from sympathetic blockade.

NCLEX Practice Questions

Question 1:

"A patient in active labor has contractions every 2-3 minutes. The fetal monitor shows late decelerations. What is the FIRST action?"

A) Increase Pitocin
B) Turn patient to left side
C) Prepare for cesarean
D) Check cervix

Answer: B — Position change is first. Then O2, fluids, stop Pitocin, notify provider. Never increase Pitocin with late decelerations.

Question 2:

"A patient is 8 cm dilated and says she feels the urge to push. What is the nurse's BEST response?"

A) "Go ahead and push with the next contraction"
B) "Let me check your cervix first"
C) "Blow out short breaths and don't push yet"
D) "I'll call the doctor"

Answer: C — Pushing before full dilation can cause cervical edema and impede progress. Panting or blowing helps resist the urge.

Question 3:

"A postpartum patient's fundus is boggy and displaced to the right. What is the FIRST action?"

A) Massage the fundus
B) Encourage voiding
C) Give oxytocin
D) Assess lochia

Answer: B — Displaced to the right = full bladder. Full bladder prevents uterine contraction. Void first, then reassess. If still boggy, massage and give oxytocin.

Question 4:

"A primigravida has been pushing for 2 hours. The fetal head crowns but retracts between contractions. What is the nurse's PRIORITY action?"

A) Encourage harder pushing
B) Apply fundal pressure
C) Notify the provider
D) Prepare for delivery

Answer: C — Prolonged second stage in nullipara (>3 hours). Needs provider assessment. May need operative delivery.

Summary

Labor and delivery nursing is about pattern recognition and timely intervention.

Know these cold:

  1. Normal labor progression and timelines
  2. FHR patterns — especially late decelerations
  3. Emergency interventions (cord prolapse, shoulder dystocia, PPH)
  4. Pain management options and nursing implications
  5. Postpartum assessment (BUBBLE-HE)

The NCLEX will give you scenarios that require clinical judgment, not just recall. Practice thinking through the decision tree: What do I see? What does it mean? What do I do FIRST?
