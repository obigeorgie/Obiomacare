OB Complications

High-risk pregnancy, preeclampsia, hemorrhage, preterm labor, PROM, gestational diabetes, and nursing management for the NCLEX.

Hypertensive Disorders of Pregnancy

Preeclampsia

Definition: New-onset hypertension (≥140/90 on 2 occasions ≥4 hours apart) AND proteinuria OR signs of end-organ dysfunction after 20 weeks gestation

Signs & Symptoms:
  • Hypertension ≥140/90 mmHg
  • Proteinuria ≥300 mg/24h or protein/creatinine ratio ≥0.3
  • Edema (hands, face)
  • Headache (persistent, unrelieved)
  • Visual disturbances (scotomata, blurred vision)
  • RUQ/epigastric pain (liver capsule distension)
  • Hyperreflexia, clonus
  • Oliguria (<500 mL/24h)
  • Elevated creatinine, LDH, AST/ALT, low platelets
Severe Features (any one = severe preeclampsia):
  • BP ≥160/110 (on 2 occasions)
  • Platelets <100,000
  • Creatinine >1.1 mg/dL or doubling
  • LDH elevation, hemolysis
  • AST/ALT >2x normal
  • Pulmonary edema
  • New-onset cerebral or visual symptoms

Management:

  • Delivery is the only cure.
  • Mild, <37 weeks: Close monitoring, steroids for fetal lung maturity if <34 weeks
  • Severe or ≥37 weeks: Delivery within 24-48 hours
  • Magnesium sulfate: Seizure prophylaxis (4-6 g IV load, then 1-2 g/hr)
  • Antihypertensives: Labetalol, hydralazine, nifedipine (goal BP 140-150/90-100)
NCLEX Trap: Do NOT give ACE inhibitors, ARBs, or diuretics in pregnancy. Do NOT give magnesium sulfate if urine output <25-30 mL/hr, absent DTRs, or RR <12 (risk of toxicity). Keep calcium gluconate at bedside (antidote).
Mnemonic: "HELLP" — Hemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelets. A severe form of preeclampsia. Delivery indicated regardless of gestational age if platelets <50,000 or active bleeding.

Eclampsia

Definition: New-onset seizures in a pregnant patient with preeclampsia

Management (ABCDE):
  1. Airway — maintain patent airway, suction, O₂
  2. Breathing — support ventilation
  3. Circulation — IV access, monitor BP
  4. Magnesium sulfate — 4-6 g IV over 15-20 min, then 1-2 g/hr
  5. If MgSO₄ fails → phenytoin or diazepam
  6. Delivery after stabilization — within 24-48 hours

Gestational Hypertension

Definition: BP ≥140/90 after 20 weeks WITHOUT proteinuria or severe features

Management: Monitor for progression to preeclampsia (BP checks, urine protein, labs). Delivery at 37-38 weeks if persistent.

Chronic Hypertension

Definition: HTN present before pregnancy or diagnosed before 20 weeks

Risk: Superimposed preeclampsia (watch for new proteinuria, worsening BP, lab changes)

Goal BP: 120-160/80-105 (avoid excessive lowering → decreased placental perfusion)

Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH)

Definition & Causes

Primary PPH (first 24 hours): Blood loss ≥1000 mL OR blood loss with signs of hypovolemia

Secondary PPH (24 hours - 12 weeks): Abnormal bleeding after initial 24 hours

Mnemonic: "4 T's of PPH"
  • Tone (uterine atony) — MOST COMMON (70-80%)
  • Trauma (lacerations, hematomas, uterine inversion, rupture)
  • Tissue (retained placenta, clots)
  • Thrombin (coagulopathy, DIC)
Uterine Atony Management:
  1. Bimanual uterine massage — first line
  2. Uterotonic medications:
    • Oxytocin (Pitocin) 10-40 units in 1L IV — first-line
    • Methylergonovine (Methergine) 0.2 mg IM — contraindicated in HTN
    • Carboprost (Hemabate) 250 mcg IM q15-90 min (max 8 doses) — contraindicated in asthma
    • Misoprostol (Cytotec) 800-1000 mcg PR — if others contraindicated
  3. Balloon tamponade (Bakri balloon)
  4. Surgical interventions: Uterine artery ligation, B-Lynch suture, hysterectomy (last resort)
NCLEX Trap: Methylergonovine is contraindicated in hypertension/preeclampsia. Carboprost is contraindicated in asthma. Know which uterotonic to give when.

Preterm Labor (PTL)

Definition & Risk Factors

Definition: Regular uterine contractions + cervical change before 37 weeks

Risk factors: Prior preterm birth, multiple gestation, cervical insufficiency, PPROM, infection, smoking, substance use, low BMI, stress

Management (if <34 weeks and no contraindications):
  1. Tocolytics (delay delivery 48 hours for steroids):
    • Nifedipine (calcium channel blocker) — first line
    • Indomethacin (NSAID) — <32 weeks only (contraindicated after 32 weeks → fetal ductus arteriosus closure)
    • Magnesium sulfate — also neuroprotection
    • Terbutaline — NOT first line (maternal cardiac risks)
  2. Corticosteroids (betamethasone 12 mg IM x2, 24h apart) — accelerate fetal lung maturity if <34 weeks
  3. Magnesium sulfate for neuroprotection if <32 weeks (24-32 weeks)
  4. Antibiotics for GBS prophylaxis if indicated
  5. Bed rest (left lateral)
NCLEX Trap: Do NOT give tocolytics if: chorioamnionitis, severe preeclampsia, fetal distress, active bleeding, IUFD. Steroids take 48 hours to mature fetal lungs — delay delivery if possible.

Premature Rupture of Membranes (PROM)

Definitions

  • PROM: Membrane rupture before labor onset (at term: ≥37 weeks)
  • PPROM: Preterm PROM (<37 weeks)
  • Prolonged ROM: >18 hours before delivery
Management:
  • Term PROM: If labor doesn't start spontaneously within 24 hours → induction (reduces infection risk without increasing C-section rate)
  • PPROM:
    • <34 weeks: Expectant management + steroids + antibiotics (prolong latency, reduce infection) + MgSO₄ for neuroprotection
    • ≥34 weeks: Delivery (risk of chorioamnionitis outweighs prematurity risk)
  • Antibiotics: Ampicillin + erythromycin (latency antibiotics) or GBS prophylaxis if indicated
NCLEX Trap: Do NOT perform digital vaginal exam after ROM (increases infection risk). Use sterile speculum exam. Monitor for chorioamnionitis (fever, maternal/fetal tachycardia, uterine tenderness, foul-smelling fluid).

(lollipop) Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM)

Screening & Diagnosis

Glucose Challenge Test (24-28 weeks): 50g glucose → check plasma glucose at 1 hour. If ≥140 (or 130-135 per institution) → proceed to OGTT.

OGTT (100g, 3-hour): Fasting ≥95, 1-hour ≥180, 2-hour ≥155, 3-hour ≥140. Diagnosis if 2+ values abnormal.

Management:
  • Diet and exercise — first line
  • Blood glucose targets: Fasting <95, 1-hour postprandial <140, 2-hour <120
  • Medications if diet fails: Insulin (first-line in pregnancy), metformin, glyburide
  • Fetal monitoring: NSTs starting 32-34 weeks (or earlier if poorly controlled), BPP
  • Delivery timing: 39-40 weeks if well-controlled; 37-38 weeks if poorly controlled or on meds
NCLEX Trap: Oral hypoglycemics cross placenta but are considered safe (metformin, glyburide). Insulin does NOT cross placenta — preferred if hyperglycemia persists. Monitor for macrosomia, polyhydramnios, shoulder dystocia, neonatal hypoglycemia.

NGN Case Study: Severe Preeclampsia & Magnesium Sulfate

Client Scenario

Ms. Jasmine Williams, 28-year-old G1P0 at 34 weeks gestation, is admitted to the labor and delivery unit with a severe headache and visual changes. Her prenatal care has been uncomplicated until her last visit 1 week ago when her BP was noted as 146/92. She was told to monitor at home and return if symptoms worsened. She reports seeing "spots" in her vision and a headache that "won't go away" for the past 6 hours.

Vital Signs Nurse's Notes Lab Results Provider Orders

Vital Signs & Assessment

  • BP: 168/110 mmHg (left arm, patient resting on left side)
  • HR: 96 bpm
  • RR: 20 breaths/min
  • Temp: 37.0°C (98.6°F)
  • SpO₂: 98% on room air
  • Weight: 78 kg (pre-pregnancy weight 68 kg = 10 kg gain)
  • Fundal height: 33 cm
  • FHR: 142 bpm, moderate variability, accelerations present
  • Neuro: Alert, reports scotomata (spots), hyperreflexia 3+ with 2 beats clonus at ankles
  • Edema: 3+ pitting edema bilateral lower extremities, facial swelling
  • Uterine activity: Occasional irregular contractions, not in active labor

Nurse's Notes

Patient reports decreased fetal movement over the past 2 days. She has been checking her blood pressure at home — readings have been 150s/90s. She took acetaminophen for her headache with no relief. No epigastric pain. No nausea or vomiting. No vaginal bleeding. She has no significant medical history. Father of baby is present and supportive.

Lab Results

  • Platelets: 118 × 10⁹/L (low)
  • Creatinine: 1.3 mg/dL (elevated)
  • AST: 68 U/L (elevated)
  • ALT: 72 U/L (elevated)
  • LDH: 320 U/L (elevated)
  • Urinalysis: 3+ protein on dipstick
  • Hgb: 11.2 g/dL
  • WBC: 12.4 × 10⁹/L
  • Glucose: 94 mg/dL

Provider Orders

  1. Magnesium sulfate 4 g IV loading dose, then 2 g/hr continuous infusion
  2. Labetalol 20 mg IV, may repeat 20-80 mg q10 min PRN (max 300 mg)
  3. Continuous fetal monitoring
  4. Strict I&O, hourly neuro checks, hourly reflexes
  5. CBC with platelets, CMP, LDH q6h
  6. Betamethasone 12 mg IM × 2 doses (24 hours apart) for fetal lung maturity
  7. OB and MFM consult — plan for delivery within 24-48 hours

Clinical Judgment Questions

Question 1 — Recognize Cues: Which findings confirm the diagnosis of SEVERE preeclampsia (rather than mild)? (Select all that apply)

  1. BP 168/110 mmHg
  2. Proteinuria 3+
  3. Platelets 118 × 10⁹/L
  4. Visual changes (scotomata)
  5. Hyperreflexia with clonus
  6. 10 kg weight gain in pregnancy
Correct: A, C, D, E
Severe features of preeclampsia: SBP ≥160 or DBP ≥110 (A), thrombocytopenia <100K (C is close but not severe), visual disturbances (D), hyperreflexia/clonus (E), epigastric/RUQ pain, elevated LFTs, pulmonary edema, renal insufficiency (creatinine >1.1). Proteinuria (B) and weight gain (F) are present in mild preeclampsia too. Platelets 118K is low-normal; <100K would be definitive severe feature.

Question 2 — Analyze Cues: The nurse notes the platelet count is 118,000. Why is thrombocytopenia significant in preeclampsia?

  1. It indicates the patient will need a platelet transfusion before delivery
  2. It suggests HELLP syndrome and indicates worsening disease severity
  3. It means the patient cannot receive magnesium sulfate
  4. It is a normal finding in the third trimester of pregnancy
Correct: B
Thrombocytopenia is a sign of HELLP syndrome (Hemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelets) — a severe variant of preeclampsia. Low platelets indicate worsening endothelial damage and consumption. While platelets <50K may require transfusion before neuraxial anesthesia, 118K does not require transfusion. Magnesium can still be given (C). Thrombocytopenia is NOT normal in pregnancy (D).

Question 3 — Prioritize Hypotheses: The provider orders magnesium sulfate. What is the PRIMARY purpose of magnesium in this patient?

  1. To lower the blood pressure
  2. To prevent and treat eclamptic seizures
  3. To promote fetal lung maturity
  4. To prevent preterm labor contractions
Correct: B
Magnesium sulfate is the anticonvulsant of choice for seizure prophylaxis in severe preeclampsia. It reduces the risk of eclampsia by ~50%. Magnesium does NOT lower BP significantly (A) — antihypertensives (labetalol, hydralazine, nifedipine) are used for that. Betamethasone (C) promotes fetal lung maturity. Tocolytics (D) are contraindicated — delivery is the definitive treatment for severe preeclampsia.

Question 4 — Generate Solutions: The nurse is administering the magnesium sulfate loading dose. Which assessment finding would require the nurse to STOP the infusion?

  1. Respiratory rate 14 breaths/min
  2. Patellar reflexes 2+
  3. Urine output 40 mL/hr
  4. Patient reports feeling warm and flushed
Correct: A
Magnesium toxicity monitoring: STOP if RR <12, absent patellar reflexes, or urine output <30 mL/hr (magnesium is renally excreted). RR 14 is approaching danger zone — monitor closely. Patellar reflexes 2+ (B) are normal. UOP 40 mL/hr (C) is adequate. Warmth/flushing (D) are expected side effects.

Question 5 — Take Action: The patient develops a sudden severe headache and her clonus increases to 4 beats. BP is now 176/118. What is the nurse's BEST immediate action?

  1. Increase the magnesium sulfate infusion rate
  2. Call the provider and prepare for emergent delivery
  3. Administer the ordered labetalol and reassess in 30 minutes
  4. Position the patient supine with legs elevated
Correct: B
Worsening neuro status (severe headache, increased clonus) with refractory hypertension = impending eclampsia. The definitive treatment for severe preeclampsia/eclampsia is DELIVERY. Call the provider immediately — this patient likely needs emergent delivery regardless of gestational age. While labetalol (C) and magnesium should continue, delivery is the cure. Do NOT position supine (D) — left lateral is preferred to avoid IVC compression.

Question 6 — Evaluate Outcomes: The patient delivers a healthy infant via cesarean section. On postpartum day 2, the nurse is preparing discharge teaching. Which statement by the patient indicates a need for FURTHER teaching?

  1. "I will monitor my blood pressure at home for the next few weeks."
  2. "I know headaches and vision changes mean I should call my doctor immediately."
  3. "I can stop taking my blood pressure medication now that I've delivered."
  4. "I understand I have a higher risk of preeclampsia in future pregnancies."
Correct: C
Preeclampsia can worsen postpartum — especially in the first 48 hours to 6 weeks. BP often remains elevated and requires continued antihypertensive therapy with gradual tapering. Do NOT stop BP meds abruptly. Home BP monitoring (A), symptom awareness (B), and future risk counseling (D) are all appropriate understanding.
Case Study Learning Points:
  • Severe preeclampsia features: H-E-L-L-P + visual + neuro — Hypertension (>160/110), Elevated LFTs, Low Platelets, Proteinuria, visual changes, hyperreflexia/clonus
  • Magnesium = seizure prophylaxis — NOT for BP control. Monitor: RR >12, reflexes present, UOP >30 mL/hr
  • Definitive treatment = DELIVERY — For severe preeclampsia, deliver regardless of gestational age
  • Postpartum risk continues — Preeclampsia can worsen up to 6 weeks after delivery
  • Betamethasone for <34 weeks — Promotes fetal lung maturity when delivery is anticipated
