Pediatric Cardiac & CHD

Congenital heart defects, cyanotic vs acyanotic lesions, ductal-dependent defects, and pediatric cardiac nursing for the NCLEX.

❤️ Congenital Heart Defects Overview

Mnemonic: "5 T's of Cyanotic CHD" — Tetralogy of Fallot, Transposition of the Great Vessels, Truncus Arteriosus, Total Anomalous Pulmonary Venous Return, Tricuspid Atresia

Acyanotic Defects (L → R Shunt)

Pathophysiology: Oxygenated blood shunts from left (higher pressure) to right (lower pressure) side. No cyanosis initially. Increased pulmonary blood flow.

Examples:

  • Ventricular Septal Defect (VSD) — MOST COMMON CHD. Harsh holosystolic murmur at LLSB. May close spontaneously (small defects).
  • Atrial Septal Defect (ASD) — Wide fixed split S2, systolic ejection murmur. Often asymptomatic until adulthood.
  • Patent Ductus Arteriosus (PDA) — Continuous machinery murmur at LUSB. Prematurity is major risk factor. Treat with indomethacin or ibuprofen (in preemies).
  • Atrioventricular Canal Defect (AVCD) — Common in Down syndrome. Large defect with L → R shunt.
Manifestations: Failure to thrive, tachypnea, diaphoresis with feeding, recurrent respiratory infections, hepatomegaly
Management: Diuretics (furosemide), ACE inhibitors (reduce afterload), surgical repair if symptomatic or large defect. Prevent endocarditis (antibiotic prophylaxis for dental procedures).

Cyanotic Defects (R → L Shunt)

Pathophysiology: Deoxygenated blood shunts from right to left side, bypassing the lungs → systemic cyanosis.

Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) — MOST COMMON cyanotic CHD

4 Defects:

  1. Pulmonary stenosis (right ventricular outflow obstruction)
  2. Ventricular septal defect
  3. Overriding aorta (aorta sits over VSD)
  4. Right ventricular hypertrophy
Manifestations: Cyanosis (worsens with crying), clubbing, polycythemia, "tet spells" (hypercyanotic episodes), harsh systolic ejection murmur at LUSB
Tet Spell Management:
  1. Place infant in knee-to-chest position (increases SVR, reduces R → L shunt)
  2. Administer oxygen (limited benefit but standard)
  3. Give morphine (reduces crying, decreases RR)
  4. IV fluids (increases preload)
  5. Phenylephrine or squatting (increases SVR)
  6. Propranolol (reduces infundibular spasm)
Mnemonic: "PROVe" — Pulmonary stenosis, RV hypertrophy, Overriding aorta, VSD

Transposition of the Great Arteries (TGA)

Pathophysiology: Aorta arises from RV, pulmonary artery from LV. Two parallel circuits — no mixing of blood.

Manifestations: Severe cyanosis within hours of birth, tachypnea, single loud S2, normal heart size
Management: Prostaglandin E₁ (alprostadil) to keep PDA open (maintains mixing). Balloon atrial septostomy (Rashkind procedure) to create ASD. Arterial switch operation (Jatene procedure) within first 2 weeks.

Hypoplastic Left Heart Syndrome (HLHS)

Pathophysiology: Underdeveloped left ventricle, aortic atresia, mitral atresia. Systemic circulation depends on PDA.

Management: Prostaglandin E₁ to maintain PDA patency (life-dependent). Norwood procedure (stage 1) within first week, Glenn procedure (3-6 months), Fontan procedure (2-4 years). Heart transplant is definitive.

💊 Ductal-Dependent Lesions

Prostaglandin E₁ (Alprostadil)

Indication: Maintain ductus arteriosus patency in ductal-dependent CHD (HLHS, TGA, critical coarctation, critical pulmonary stenosis)

Side Effects:
  • Apnea (most common — intubation readiness)
  • Hypotension, flushing
  • Fever
  • Bradycardia
Nursing Priority: Monitor for apnea (may need intubation). Continuous cardiac monitoring. Keep emergency airway equipment at bedside. Monitor BP closely.
NCLEX Trap: PGE₁ is given to KEEP PDA OPEN in ductal-dependent lesions. Indomethacin/ibuprofen is given to CLOSE PDA in preterm infants. These are opposites — know which condition requires which drug.

🔍 Congestive Heart Failure in Infants

Manifestations

Feeding difficulties: Poor feeding, diaphoresis during feeds, fatigue, prolonged feeding time
Respiratory: Tachypnea, retractions, nasal flaring, grunting, wheezing
Cardiac: Tachycardia, gallop rhythm, cardiomegaly, murmur
Other: Hepatomegaly, poor weight gain/failure to thrive, edema (periorbital, sacral), cool extremities

Management

  • Diuretics: Furosemide (loop), spironolactone (K⁺-sparing)
  • ACE inhibitors: Captopril, enalapril (reduce afterload, improve cardiac output)
  • Digoxin: Positive inotrope (narrow therapeutic window — monitor levels)
  • High-calorie formula: 24-30 kcal/oz to promote growth with less volume
  • Frequent small feedings: Decrease energy expenditure
  • Caloric rest: NG feeds if needed
Digoxin Toxicity in Infants: Vomiting, poor feeding, bradycardia, lethargy, arrhythmias. Hold digoxin if HR <90-100 (infants) or <70 (children). Antidote: Digoxin immune Fab.

🩺 Infective Endocarditis Prophylaxis

Indications (AHA Guidelines)

Prophylaxis ONLY for patients with:

  • Prosthetic cardiac valve
  • Prior infective endocarditis
  • Unrepaired cyanotic CHD (including palliative shunts)
  • Repaired CHD with residual defect at/ adjacent to prosthetic patch
  • Cardiac transplant with valvulopathy

Procedures requiring prophylaxis: Dental (manipulation of gingival tissue), respiratory tract (tonsillectomy), infected skin/soft tissue

Single dose amoxicillin 2g PO (or ampicillin/cefazolin if unable to take PO)

🏥 Post-Operative Cardiac Surgery Care

Immediate Post-Op Priorities (First 24-48 Hours)

1. Hemodynamic Stability
  • Arterial line for continuous BP monitoring
  • Central venous pressure (CVP) monitoring (goal varies by procedure)
  • Pulmonary artery pressure if indicated
  • Cardiac output/index monitoring (thermodilution or echo)
  • Monitor for low cardiac output syndrome (LCOS): tachycardia, hypotension, poor perfusion, oliguria, metabolic acidosis
2. Rhythm Monitoring
  • Continuous telemetry — junctional ectopic tachycardia (JET) is common after Tetralogy/ASD repair
  • Heart block after VSD/AV canal repair — may need temporary pacing
  • Have external pacemaker available
3. Ventilation & Oxygenation
  • Mechanical ventilation initially — wean per protocol
  • Avoid hyperoxia in single-ventricle physiology (affects pulmonary vascular resistance)
  • Monitor ABGs, chest X-ray for pneumothorax/effusion
4. Chest Tube Management
  • Monitor output — excessive bleeding (>3 mL/kg/hr for 3 consecutive hours) = return to OR
  • Mediastinal tubes: keep patent, milk if needed, monitor for tamponade
  • Pleural tubes: assess for pneumothorax resolution
  • Chylothorax: Milky/opalescent drainage (especially after coarctation/Fontan) — place NPO, start TPN, octreotide

Complications to Watch For

Cardiac TamponadeBeck's triad: hypotension, JVD, muffled heart sounds; pulsus paradoxusEmergent pericardiocentesis or return to OR
ChylothoraxMilky chest tube drainage, especially after coarctation/FontanNPO, TPN, octreotide, possible thoracic duct ligation

Pain Management & Sedation

  • Morphine or fentanyl IV for procedural/post-op pain
  • Midazolam for agitation/anxiety (wean before extubation)
  • Dexmedetomidine — sedation without respiratory depression
  • Avoid NSAIDs initially — bleeding risk, renal effects, bone healing concerns after sternotomy
  • Non-pharmacologic: positioning, pacifier, swaddling (infants)

Activity & Feeding Progression

  • Activity: Bed rest initially, progress to chair, then ambulation. No lifting >5-10 lb for 6-8 weeks (sternotomy healing)
  • Feeding: Start with trophic feeds (small volume) once hemodynamically stable. High-calorie formula may be needed
  • Diuretics: Furosemide + spironolactone common post-op to manage fluid overload
  • Anticoagulation: Aspirin for shunts/Fontan. Warfarin for mechanical valves
NCLEX Trap: After sternotomy, infants/children should avoid activities that put pressure on the chest (push-ups, contact sports) for 6-8 weeks. Parents often worry about "breaking" the sternum — reassure it heals with wire fixation.
🧠 Post-Op Cardiac Surgery Monitoring: "C-H-E-S-T"
  • Cardiac output — BP, perfusion, UOP, lactate
  • Hemorrhage — chest tube output, Hgb trends
  • ECG — rhythm, heart block, JET
  • SpO₂/ABG — oxygenation, ventilation
  • Temperature — fever (infection), hypothermia (JET treatment)

🧩 NGN Case Study: Ductal-Dependent Congenital Heart Defect

Client Scenario

Baby Emma, 4-day-old female, is brought to the emergency department by her parents. They report she has become increasingly "blue around the mouth" during feedings over the past day and seems to "work hard to breathe." She was born at term via spontaneous vaginal delivery and discharged home at 24 hours. Prenatal care was uncomplicated. Parents are first-time parents.

Vital Signs Nurse's Notes Provider Orders

📊 Vital Signs & Assessment

  • BP: 62/38 mmHg (right arm)
  • HR: 172 bpm
  • RR: 68 breaths/min, labored with nasal flaring and retractions
  • Temp: 36.8°C (98.2°F) axillary
  • SpO₂: 72% on room air
  • Weight: 3.2 kg (birth weight 3.4 kg)
  • Appearance: Cyanotic lips and nail beds, diaphoretic, lethargic
  • Lungs: Clear bilaterally with good air entry
  • Heart: Single S2 sound, no murmur audible
  • Peripheries: Weak pulses, capillary refill 4 seconds
  • Femoral pulses: Weak and delayed compared to brachial

📝 Nurse's Notes

Parents report Emma fed poorly at home, taking only 1-2 ounces per feeding (down from 2-3 oz). She has been sleeping more than usual and is difficult to arouse. No fever. No vomiting. No sick contacts. Mother had negative Group B strep screen. Baby passed newborn screening before discharge.

📋 Provider Orders

  1. Oxygen 100% via non-rebreather mask
  2. IV access, D10 maintenance fluids
  3. Prostaglandin E1 (PGE1) infusion 0.05 mcg/kg/min
  4. Chest X-ray
  5. 4-extremity blood pressures
  6. Echocardiogram (stat)
  7. ABG, complete metabolic panel, CBC, blood culture
  8. Cardiology consult

Clinical Judgment Questions

Question 1 — Recognize Cues: Which findings suggest a DUCTAL-DEPENDENT cardiac lesion? (Select all that apply)

  1. Cyanosis appearing at 4 days of age
  2. Single S2 heart sound
  3. Differential cyanosis (lower SpO₂ than upper)
  4. Weak femoral pulses with delayed capillary refill
  5. Tachypnea with feeding
  6. Clear lung sounds
Correct: A, B, D, E, F
Cyanosis appearing after ductal closure (24-72 hours) is classic for ductal-dependent lesions. Single S2 suggests only one great vessel (seen in Tetralogy of Fallot, truncus arteriosus, pulmonary atresia). Weak femoral pulses suggest coarctation of the aorta or hypoplastic left heart. Tachypnea with feeding and clear lungs suggest pulmonary blood flow obstruction (not pulmonary edema). Differential cyanosis (C) is specific to PPHN or transposition with PDA, but we don't have upper vs lower SpO₂ data here.

Question 2 — Analyze Cues: The nurse notes a single S2 sound and no murmur. Which defect is MOST likely?

  1. Atrial septal defect (ASD)
  2. Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF)
  3. Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA)
  4. Ventricular septal defect (VSD)
Correct: B
Tetralogy of Fallot classically presents with cyanosis at 2-4 months (earlier if severe), a single S2 (A2 only — P2 is soft/absent due to pulmonary stenosis), and may have NO murmur if pulmonary atresia is present. ASD (A) and VSD (D) typically cause acyanotic presentations with murmurs. PDA (C) causes a continuous machinery murmur.

Question 3 — Prioritize Hypotheses: The provider orders PGE1 infusion. What is the PRIMARY purpose of this medication?

  1. To increase cardiac contractility and improve perfusion
  2. To keep the patent ductus arteriosus open and maintain pulmonary/systemic blood flow
  3. To reduce pulmonary vascular resistance and improve oxygenation
  4. To sedate the infant and reduce oxygen consumption
Correct: B
PGE1 (alprostadil) keeps the ductus arteriosus patent. In ductal-dependent lesions, the PDA is the ONLY source of pulmonary blood flow (TOF, pulmonary atresia, tricuspid atresia) or systemic blood flow (coarctation, interrupted aortic arch, hypoplastic left heart). Keeping it open is LIFE-SAVING while awaiting definitive repair.

Question 4 — Generate Solutions: The nurse is preparing to start the PGE1 infusion. Which action is the HIGHEST priority?

  1. Ensure the infant is NPO and prepare for intubation
  2. Verify the concentration and program the infusion pump correctly
  3. Place the infant in knee-chest position
  4. Start supplemental oxygen at 2L NC
Correct: B
PGE1 is a HIGH-ALERT medication with a narrow therapeutic window (0.01-0.1 mcg/kg/min). Dosing errors can cause severe hypotension, apnea, or inadequate ductal patency. Verify concentration, dose calculation, and pump programming. While apnea monitoring (A) and positioning (C) are important, medication safety comes first. Oxygen (D) has already been ordered.

Question 5 — Take Action: Thirty minutes after starting PGE1, the infant's SpO₂ improves to 85% but she develops apnea lasting 15 seconds. What is the nurse's BEST immediate action?

  1. Stop the PGE1 infusion immediately
  2. Stimulate the infant and prepare for possible intubation
  3. Increase the PGE1 dose to improve oxygenation further
  4. Call the provider to request a different medication
Correct: B
Apnea is a known side effect of PGE1 (especially at higher doses and in very young infants). Stimulate the infant (rub back, reposition). If apnea continues or recurs, intubation may be needed. Do NOT stop PGE1 — this could close the ductus and cause cardiovascular collapse. The provider should be notified, but stimulation and airway management are the immediate priorities.

Question 6 — Evaluate Outcomes: The cardiologist confirms Tetralogy of Fallot. The nurse is preparing the parents for transfer to a pediatric cardiac center. Which statement by the mother indicates understanding?

  1. "My baby will need surgery right away to fix the hole in her heart."
  2. "She may need a temporary shunt until she's big enough for full repair."
  3. "Once she has surgery, she'll never need any more cardiac procedures."
  4. "This happened because I didn't take good enough care of myself during pregnancy."
Correct: B
TOF repair is typically done at 3-6 months. In the interim, a palliative shunt (Blalock-Taussig) may be placed to increase pulmonary blood flow. Full repair involves closing the VSD and relieving pulmonary stenosis. Some children need subsequent procedures (pulmonary valve replacement in adolescence/adulthood). CHD is NOT caused by maternal behavior — reassure parents it is not their fault.
🧠 Case Study Learning Points:
  • Cyanosis at 2-4 days = ductal closure — Think ductal-dependent CHD
  • PGE1 keeps PDA open — Life-saving in ductal-dependent lesions
  • Apnea is a known side effect — Monitor closely, don't stop infusion
  • Single S2 + cyanosis = TOF — Pulmonary stenosis masks P2 sound
  • Knee-chest position helps — Increases SVR, reduces right-to-left shunt
