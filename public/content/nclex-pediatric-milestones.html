Pediatric Developmental Milestones: NCLEX Study Guide

Pediatric milestones are high-yield on the NCLEX. Not because the numbers are complex — they're not. But because the exam tests whether you recognize normal…

Introduction

Pediatric milestones are high-yield on the NCLEX. Not because the numbers are complex — they're not. But because the exam tests whether you recognize normal vs. delayed development, and what delays might indicate.

This guide covers the major milestones from infancy through preschool, with NCLEX-style scenarios and red flags. Organized by age and domain for rapid review.

Milestones by Age

1 Month

Gross Motor:

  • Flexed posture, turns head side to side
  • Primitive reflexes present: Moro, rooting, suck, grasp, tonic neck

Fine Motor:

  • Tight fist
  • Reflex grasp

Language:

  • Cries (different cries for different needs)

Social:

  • Brief eye contact
  • Startles to sound

NCLEX red flag: Absent Moro reflex → neurologic issue (birth injury, CNS anomaly)

2-3 Months

Gross Motor:

  • Holds head up 45 degrees prone
  • Bears some weight on legs when held

Fine Motor:

  • Opens hands
  • Briefly holds rattle

Language:

  • Coos, gurgles
  • Quiets to familiar voice

Social:

  • Social smile (6-8 weeks) — critical milestone
  • Follows objects 180 degrees

NCLEX red flag: No social smile by 3 months → autism spectrum concern, vision problem, or attachment issue

4-5 Months

Gross Motor:

  • Rolls front to back (4 months)
  • Sits with support
  • Bear weight on legs, bounces

Fine Motor:

  • Reaches for objects
  • Grasps with whole hand (palmar grasp)
  • Brings hands to midline

Language:

  • Babbles (consonant sounds: ba, da, ma)
  • Turns to sound

Social:

  • Laughs out loud
  • Recognizes familiar faces

NCLEX red flag: Not reaching by 5 months → cerebral palsy, vision impairment, or cognitive delay

6-7 Months

Gross Motor:

  • Sits independently (6 months)
  • Rolls both directions
  • Bear weight on legs, may bounce

Fine Motor:

  • Transfers objects hand to hand
  • Raking grasp
  • Bangs objects together

Language:

  • Babbling with multiple syllables
  • Says "mama" or "dada" indiscriminately

Social:

  • Stranger anxiety begins (6-9 months)
  • Plays peek-a-boo

NCLEX red flag: No sitting by 7 months → significant motor delay. Evaluate for cerebral palsy, hypotonia, genetic syndromes.

9-10 Months

Gross Motor:

  • Crawls (some babies skip this — normal if other milestones on track)
  • Pulls to stand
  • Cruises along furniture

Fine Motor:

  • Pincer grasp (thumb and forefinger) — 9 months, critical
  • Points with finger
  • Puts objects in container

Language:

  • "Mama" and "dada" specifically
  • Understands "no"
  • Imitates sounds

Social:

  • Waves bye-bye
  • Plays pat-a-cake
  • Separation anxiety peaks

NCLEX red flag: No pincer grasp by 10 months → fine motor delay, possible cerebral palsy or genetic condition

12 Months (1 Year)

Gross Motor:

  • Stands independently
  • First steps (may be 12-15 months)
  • Squats to pick up toy

Fine Motor:

  • Pincer grasp well-developed
  • Releases object on request
  • Turns pages of board book

Language:

  • 1-2 words with meaning
  • Follows 1-step command
  • Imitates sounds and actions

Social:

  • Imitates activities (brushing hair, talking on phone)
  • Shows affection
  • Separation anxiety still present

NCLEX red flag: No words by 12 months → hearing evaluation, speech delay, autism screening

15-18 Months

Gross Motor:

  • Walks independently (15 months)
  • Climbs stairs with help
  • Can run (18 months)
  • Throws ball

Fine Motor:

  • Builds tower of 2-4 blocks
  • Scribbles
  • Uses spoon (messily)
  • Removes socks and shoes

Language:

  • 10-20 words (18 months)
  • Points to body parts
  • Follows 2-step commands

Social:

  • Temper tantrums begin
  • Shows independence ("me do it")
  • Plays alongside other children (parallel play)

NCLEX red flag: Not walking by 18 months → significant motor delay, evaluate for CP, muscular dystrophy, genetic conditions

2 Years (24 Months)

Gross Motor:

  • Runs well
  • Kicks ball
  • Climbs furniture
  • Walks up/down stairs holding rail

Fine Motor:

  • Builds tower of 6 blocks
  • Turns pages one at a time
  • Unscrews lid
  • Uses spoon well

Language:

  • 50+ words
  • 2-word phrases ("more milk")
  • 50% intelligible to strangers

Social:

  • Imitates adult behaviors
  • Shows defiance (says "no")
  • Parallel play
  • Separation anxiety decreases

NCLEX red flag: No 2-word phrases by 24 months → speech/language evaluation, autism screening

3 Years (36 Months)

Gross Motor:

  • Pedals tricycle
  • Walks up stairs alternating feet
  • Jumps in place
  • Stands on one foot briefly

Fine Motor:

  • Copies circle
  • Uses scissors
  • Dresses self with help
  • Builds tower of 9-10 blocks

Language:

  • 3-word sentences
  • Asks "what" and "where" questions
  • 75% intelligible
  • Knows first and last name

Social:

  • Parallel play → associative play
  • Takes turns (with prompting)
  • Shows affection for friends
  • Understands "mine" and "yours"

NCLEX red flag: Unclear speech by 3 years → speech evaluation. Strangers should understand 75%.

4 Years (48 Months)

Gross Motor:

  • Hops on one foot
  • Throws ball overhand
  • Climbs well
  • Walks downstairs alternating feet

Fine Motor:

  • Copies cross and square
  • Draws person with 2-4 body parts
  • Uses fork well
  • Buttons clothes

Language:

  • 4-5 word sentences
  • Tells stories
  • Understands "same" and "different"
  • Speech 100% intelligible

Social:

  • Cooperative play
  • Prefers social play
  • Wants to please friends
  • Understands rules

NCLEX red flag: Cannot draw a person by 4 years → fine motor delay or cognitive concern

5 Years (60 Months)

Gross Motor:

  • Skips
  • Races
  • Somersaults
  • Swings, climbs, slides confidently

Fine Motor:

  • Copies triangle
  • Prints some letters
  • Dresses independently
  • Ties shoelaces (may be 6)

Language:

  • 5-6 word sentences
  • Uses past tense
  • Understands time concepts
  • Speaks clearly in full sentences

Social:

  • Wants to be like friends
  • Follows rules
  • Can distinguish fantasy from reality
  • Shows independence

Primitive Reflexes: When They Disappear

Reflex Appears Disappears If Persists
Moro (startle) Birth 4-6 months CNS dysfunction
Rooting Birth 4 months Frontal lobe issue
Palmar grasp Birth 4-6 months Cerebral palsy
Tonic neck (fencing) Birth 4-6 months Cerebral palsy
Plantar grasp Birth 8-10 months Cerebral palsy
Parachute 8-9 months Never (protective) Motor delay if absent

NCLEX tip: Persistence of primitive reflexes beyond normal age = upper motor neuron problem.

Developmental Red Flags by Age

Red Flags at Any Age

  • Loss of previously acquired skills (regression)
  • No eye contact
  • No response to name
  • No social reciprocity
  • Flapping, rocking, or repetitive behaviors
  • Extreme sensory sensitivity

2-Month Red Flags

  • No visual fixation
  • No response to sound
  • Extreme floppiness or stiffness
  • No Moro reflex

6-Month Red Flags

  • No social smile
  • Not bringing hands to midline
  • Not turning to sound
  • Persistent fisting

12-Month Red Flags

  • No babbling
  • No gestures (pointing, waving)
  • No response to name
  • Not crawling or dragging one side

18-Month Red Flags

  • No words
  • No pretend play
  • No pointing to show things
  • Cannot walk independently

2-Year Red Flags

  • No 2-word phrases
  • No imitation
  • Cannot follow simple instructions
  • No interest in other children

3-Year Red Flags

  • Unclear speech
  • No 3-word sentences
  • No pretend play
  • Cannot separate from parent

NCLEX Practice Questions

Question 1:

"A 10-month-old is seen in the clinic. The mother reports the infant can pull to stand, cruise along furniture, and says 'mama' specifically. Which additional milestone should the nurse expect?"

A) Walks independently
B) Pincer grasp
C) Points with finger
D) Uses spoon

Answer: B and C — Pincer grasp and pointing are both 9-10 month milestones. Walking independently is 12-15 months. Spoon use is 15-18 months.

NCLEX tip: Questions may have multiple correct answers (SATA format). Select ALL that apply.

Question 2:

"A mother brings her 6-month-old for a well-child visit. She states the infant is not yet sitting independently but can roll front to back and babbles. What is the nurse's BEST response?"

A) "This is normal. Some babies sit later."
B) "We should schedule developmental testing."
C) "Sitting should be achieved by 6 months. Let's monitor closely."
D) "Have you been doing tummy time?"

Answer: C — Sitting independently is expected by 6-7 months. While rolling and babbling are appropriate, not sitting warrants close monitoring. Not yet an emergency, but should be watched. A is too dismissive. B is premature without further monitoring.

Question 3:

"A 15-month-old is not yet walking independently. The child crawls, pulls to stand, and cruises. Which action is MOST appropriate?"

A) Reassure the parent this is normal
B) Refer to physical therapy
C) Assess further and monitor
D) Order genetic testing

Answer: C — 15 months is the early end for walking. Some normal children walk at 15 months, others at 18 months. Cruising and pulling to stand are good signs. Monitor and reassess at 18 months. If not walking by 18 months, then refer.

Question 4:

"Which finding in a 12-month-old requires IMMEDIATE follow-up?"

A) Not walking independently
B) No words with meaning
C) Not sitting independently
D) No pincer grasp

Answer: C — Not sitting by 12 months is a significant motor delay. Walking (A) is not expected until 12-15 months. No words (B) warrants evaluation but not emergent. No pincer grasp (D) is concerning but sitting is more fundamental.

Summary

Pediatric milestones are about patterns, not isolated numbers. The NCLEX tests:

  1. Knowing normal — What should a 12-month-old do?
  2. Recognizing delay — When is "late" truly delayed?
  3. Prioritizing concerns — Which delay is most serious?
  4. Knowing when to refer — Monitor vs. evaluate now

Key principle: Motor milestones proceed head-to-toe (cephalocaudal) and center-to-extremities (proximodistal). Delays follow this pattern.

Another key principle: Regression (losing skills) is ALWAYS abnormal and requires immediate evaluation.
