NCLEX Pharmacology: 15 Drug Classes Every Nurse Must Know

By Nnamdi Okorafor, RN · 15 years ER experience · Updated July 2026

Pharmacology is where nursing students panic. Hundreds of drugs, endless side effects, and every question feels like a trick. Here's the truth: The NCLEX tests drug CLASSES, not individual medications. Know the class, and you know 90% of what you need.

These 15 drug classes appear on every NCLEX exam. For each, I'll give you the prototype, mechanism, key side effects, and what the NGN actually tests.

Cardiovascular Drugs

1. ACE Inhibitors

Prototype: Lisinopril

Mechanism: Block conversion of angiotensin I to II → vasodilation + decreased aldosterone

Uses: Hypertension, heart failure, post-MI

Side effects to know:
  • Dry cough (most common reason for discontinuation)
  • Hyperkalemia — check K+ levels regularly
  • Angioedema — rare but life-threatening. Swelling of tongue/lips → stop drug immediately
  • First-dose hypotension — give at bedtime
NGN scenario: Patient on lisinopril reports persistent dry cough. What's your action? Contact provider to switch to ARB (like losartan).

2. Beta Blockers

Prototype: Metoprolol

Mechanism: Block beta-1 receptors → decreased HR, contractility, BP

Uses: Hypertension, angina, heart failure, post-MI, arrhythmias

Side effects to know:
  • Bradycardia & hypotension — hold if HR <60 or SBP <90
  • Fatigue, depression
  • Masks hypoglycemia symptoms — diabetic patients may not feel shakiness/sweating
  • Bronchospasm — caution with asthma/COPD (non-selective like propranolol)
NGN scenario: Patient on metoprolol has BP 92/58, HR 52. What do you do? Hold the dose, notify provider. Monitor for signs of heart block.

3. Diuretics (Loop)

Prototype: Furosemide

Mechanism: Inhibit Na+/K+/2Cl- cotransport in loop of Henle → massive diuresis

Uses: Heart failure, pulmonary edema, renal failure, hyperkalemia

Side effects to know:
  • Hypokalemia — monitor K+, give supplements, encourage bananas/orange juice
  • Hypotension
  • Ototoxicity — especially with aminoglycosides
  • Dehydration
NGN scenario: Patient on furosemide has K+ 3.2. What's your priority? Administer K+ supplement, monitor cardiac rhythm, check for muscle weakness.

4. Statins

Prototype: Atorvastatin

Mechanism: Inhibit HMG-CoA reductase → decreased cholesterol synthesis

Uses: Hyperlipidemia, cardiovascular prevention

Side effects to know:
  • Myopathy/rhabdomyolysis — muscle pain, weakness, dark urine → stop drug, check CK
  • Elevated liver enzymes — check LFTs periodically
  • Contraindicated in pregnancy

Anticoagulants

5. Heparin

Mechanism: Potentiates antithrombin III → inactivates thrombin and factor Xa

Uses: DVT/PE prophylaxis and treatment, ACS, before surgery

Side effects to know:
  • Bleeding — monitor aPTT (therapeutic: 1.5-2.5x normal)
  • Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) — platelets drop >50% from baseline
  • Antidote: Protamine sulfate
NGN scenario: Patient on heparin infusion has aPTT 90 seconds. What do you do? Hold infusion, notify provider, assess for bleeding. Therapeutic is 1.5-2.5x normal (~45-70 sec).

6. Warfarin

Mechanism: Inhibits vitamin K-dependent clotting factors (II, VII, IX, X)

Uses: Long-term anticoagulation (AFib, mechanical valves, recurrent DVT/PE)

Side effects to know:
  • Bleeding — monitor INR (therapeutic 2.0-3.0)
  • Teratogenic — contraindicated in pregnancy
  • Many drug interactions — antibiotics increase effect; rifampin decreases
  • Antidote: Vitamin K (slow) or FFP (fast)
NGN scenario: Patient on warfarin wants to start eating more leafy greens. What do you teach? Keep vitamin K intake CONSISTENT, not low. Sudden increase decreases warfarin effect.

Diabetes Medications

7. Insulin

Rapid-acting: Lispro, Aspart (onset 15 min, peak 1 hr)
Short-acting: Regular (onset 30 min, peak 2-3 hr)
Intermediate: NPH (onset 1-2 hr, peak 4-8 hr)
Long-acting: Glargine, Detemir (onset 1-2 hr, no peak)

Side effects to know:
  • Hypoglycemia — #1 risk. Give 15g fast carbs, recheck in 15 min
  • Lipodystrophy — rotate injection sites
  • Weight gain
NGN scenario: Patient on NPH at bedtime has fasting glucose 52 mg/dL. What do you do? Give 15g fast carbs, recheck in 15 min, hold tonight's NPH dose, notify provider.

8. Metformin

Mechanism: Decreases hepatic glucose production, increases insulin sensitivity

Uses: Type 2 diabetes (first-line)

Side effects to know:
  • Lactic acidosis — rare but fatal. Risk with renal impairment, dehydration, contrast dye
  • GI upset — take with food
  • Hold 48 hours before/after contrast dye if eGFR <30
  • Contraindicated if eGFR <30

Respiratory Drugs

9. Beta-2 Agonists

Prototype: Albuterol

Mechanism: Stimulate beta-2 receptors → bronchodilation

Uses: Asthma, COPD exacerbation

Side effects to know:
  • Tachycardia, tremors, nervousness
  • Hypokalemia — shifts K+ into cells
  • Paradoxical bronchospasm — rare, stop drug

10. Corticosteroids (Inhaled/Systemic)

Prototype: Prednisone (systemic), Fluticasone (inhaled)

Mechanism: Anti-inflammatory, immunosuppressive

Side effects to know:
  • Inhaled: Oral candidiasis (rinse mouth after use), hoarseness
  • Systemic (long-term): Cushing's syndrome, hyperglycemia, osteoporosis, immunosuppression, weight gain, mood changes
  • NEVER stop systemic steroids abruptly — taper to prevent adrenal crisis
NGN scenario: Patient on prednisone for 3 weeks wants to stop because they feel better. What do you teach? Taper slowly. Abrupt stop causes adrenal insufficiency — fatigue, hypotension, hypoglycemia, shock.

GI Drugs

11. Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs)

Prototype: Omeprazole

Mechanism: Inhibit H+/K+ ATPase → block gastric acid secretion

Side effects to know:
  • Increased infection risk (C. diff, pneumonia) — reduced acid allows bacterial growth
  • Hypomagnesemia — check Mg2+ with long-term use
  • Bone fractures — long-term use decreases calcium absorption
  • Give 30 min before meals

Antibiotics

12. Aminoglycosides

Prototype: Gentamicin

Mechanism: Inhibit bacterial protein synthesis

Side effects to know:
  • Nephrotoxicity — monitor BUN/Cr, urine output
  • Ototoxicity — monitor hearing, tinnitus
  • Neurotoxicity — monitor for neuromuscular blockade
  • Check peak and trough levels

13. Vancomycin

Mechanism: Inhibits bacterial cell wall synthesis

Uses: MRSA, C. diff (oral only for C. diff)

Side effects to know:
  • Red Man Syndrome — flushing, rash, hypotension from rapid infusion. Infuse over 1-2 hours
  • Nephrotoxicity — monitor levels
  • Ototoxicity
  • Check trough levels (before next dose)
NGN scenario: Patient getting vancomycin develops red flushing of face and neck during infusion. What do you do? Slow infusion rate, monitor BP. This is Red Man Syndrome, not anaphylaxis. Premedicate with antihistamine for future doses.

Psychiatric Drugs

14. SSRIs

Prototype: Sertraline

Mechanism: Inhibit serotonin reuptake

Side effects to know:
  • Serotonin syndrome — if combined with other serotonergic drugs (tramadol, MAOIs, St. John's Wort)
  • SIADH/hyponatremia — especially in elderly. Check Na+
  • Sexual dysfunction
  • Takes 2-4 weeks for therapeutic effect
  • Don't stop abruptly — taper to prevent discontinuation syndrome

15. Benzodiazepines

Prototype: Lorazepam

Mechanism: Enhance GABA → CNS depression

Side effects to know:
  • Respiratory depression — especially with opioids or alcohol
  • Sedation, confusion, falls — especially in elderly
  • Physical dependence
  • Antidote: Flumazenil (for overdose, not for dependence)
NGN scenario: Patient on lorazepam and morphine becomes increasingly somnolent with RR 8. What do you do? This is respiratory depression. Hold both drugs, stimulate patient, prepare naloxone (for opioid) and flumazenil if needed, notify provider.

The Pharmacology Study Strategy

Don't memorize individual drugs. Memorize the CLASS:

  1. What does it do? (Mechanism in 1 sentence)
  2. What are the 3 most common side effects?
  3. What's the #1 nursing consideration? (Labs to check, when to hold, antidote)
  4. What drug interactions matter?

Example: ACE Inhibitors

  • What: Block angiotensin → vasodilation
  • Side effects: Dry cough, hyperkalemia, angioedema
  • Nursing: Check K+, hold if hyperkalemic, switch to ARB if cough
  • Interactions: NSAIDs reduce effectiveness, potassium supplements increase hyperkalemia risk

Bottom Line

The NCLEX doesn't expect you to know every drug. It expects you to understand drug classes, recognize dangerous side effects, and apply that knowledge to patient scenarios.

When you see a pharmacology question, ask: "What's the class, what's the danger, and what would I do as the nurse?" That's clinical judgment — and that's what the NGN tests.
