NCLEX Priority Setting: The ABCDE Method That Saves Lives

By Nnamdi Okorafor, RN · 15 years ER experience · Updated July 2026

Prioritization is the #1 skill tested on the Next Generation NCLEX. And it's the #1 skill that separates good nurses from great ones. When everything is urgent and you can only do one thing at a time — what do you do first?

The ABCDE method is the framework ER nurses use when multiple patients need attention, multiple problems exist, and time is running out. It's not just for the NCLEX — it's for every shift you'll ever work.

The ABCDE Method

A — Airway

If the airway is compromised, nothing else matters.

Signs of airway compromise:

  • Stridor, wheezing, or absent breath sounds
  • Inability to speak or swallow
  • Facial/neck swelling or trauma
  • Gurgling or noisy breathing
  • Foreign body obstruction

Action: Open airway (head-tilt chin-lift, jaw thrust), suction, prepare for intubation. Call for help immediately.

B — Breathing

If they're breathing, are they breathing effectively?

Signs of respiratory compromise:

  • O2 sat <90% (or dropping)
  • RR >30 or <8
  • Accessory muscle use
  • Cyanosis (lips, fingertips)
  • Altered mental status from hypoxia

Action: Apply O2, position for ventilation (high Fowler's), monitor, prepare for escalation (BiPAP, intubation).

C — Circulation

Is blood getting where it needs to go?

Signs of circulatory compromise:

  • Hypotension (SBP <90 or MAP <65)
  • Tachycardia >120 (compensatory)
  • Chest pain with ECG changes
  • Pale, cool, clammy skin
  • Decreased urine output (<30 mL/hr)
  • Active bleeding

Action: IV access, fluids, blood products, vasopressors, stop bleeding, ECG monitoring.

D — Disability (Neurological)

What's their mental status? Can they protect themselves?

Use the AVPU scale:

  • Alert — awake and oriented
  • Verbal — responds to voice
  • Pain — responds to painful stimulus only
  • Unresponsive — no response

Also check: pupil response, blood glucose (confusion can be from hypoglycemia), signs of stroke (FAST: Face, Arm, Speech, Time).

Action: Protect airway if altered, check glucose, assess for stroke, prevent falls.

E — Exposure/Everything Else

After ABCs are stable, assess everything else.

This includes:

  • Full head-to-toe assessment
  • Temperature (hyper/hypothermia)
  • Pain
  • Skin integrity
  • Psychosocial needs
  • Lab values and diagnostics

Remember: E comes LAST. A patient in pain is important, but a patient without an airway is dead.

ABCDE + Maslow's Hierarchy

The NCLEX also uses Maslow's hierarchy of needs. Combine it with ABCDE:

Priority LevelNeedsExamples
1 (Immediate)Physiological + Safety (ABCs)Airway, breathing, circulation, hemorrhage
2 (Urgent)Physiological (stable)Pain, elimination, hydration, nutrition
3 (Important)Safety + SecurityFall risk, infection prevention, meds
4 (Routine)Love/BelongingFamily contact, support systems
5 (Deferred)Esteem + Self-ActualizationEducation, discharge planning, goals

Real NCLEX Scenarios

Scenario 1: The Flooded ER

You have 4 patients. Who do you see FIRST?

Patient A: Post-op appendectomy, pain 6/10, stable vitals

Patient B: COPD exacerbation, O2 sat 86% on 2L, RR 32

Patient C: Chest pain, troponin 0.8, BP 148/92, HR 88

Patient D: Dehydration from vomiting, requesting antiemetic

Answer: Patient B
Patient B has compromised breathing (O2 sat 86%, RR 32) — B in ABCDE. Patient C has chest pain but stable vitals (C is concerning but not immediately life-threatening). Patient A is post-op pain (E — pain management). Patient D is nausea (E — comfort).

Scenario 2: The NGN Case Study Trap

A nurse receives report on 4 patients. Which patient should the nurse see FIRST?

Patient A: Fresh post-op, BP 92/58, HR 118, complaining of incisional pain

Patient B: Diabetic, blood glucose 52, alert and asking for juice

Patient C: Pneumonia, O2 sat 91% on 4L NC, RR 24

Patient D: Scheduled for discharge, needs medication education

Answer: Patient A
Patient A is in shock (hypotension + tachycardia = circulatory compromise = C in ABCDE). The pain is secondary to the hemodynamic instability. Patient B has hypoglycemia but is alert and self-correcting (juice). Patient C has respiratory compromise but is stable enough for now. Patient D can wait.

Scenario 3: The "They're All Stable" Trap

All 4 patients are stable. Who do you see FIRST?

Patient A: New onset chest pain, troponin pending

Patient B: First-time IV antibiotic due now

Patient C: Foley catheter due to be removed per protocol

Patient D: Discharge teaching needed

Answer: Patient A
When all patients are stable, use the "potential to become unstable" rule. Patient A has new chest pain with pending troponin — highest risk of becoming unstable. Patient B is scheduled but not urgent. Patient C and D are routine.

The 3 Rules of NCLEX Prioritization

Rule 1: ABCs Always Win

If any patient has an airway, breathing, or circulation problem, they win. No exceptions. Pain, anxiety, and comfort needs always come after ABCs are stable.

Rule 2: Actual Beats Potential, But Potential Beats Routine

Actual problems (active bleeding) > Potential problems (risk for bleeding) > Routine needs (scheduled meds).

But: A patient with new chest pain (potential MI) beats a patient who needs a scheduled med.

Rule 3: The Patient Who Can't Wait

Ask yourself: "If I don't see this patient first, what could happen in the next 15 minutes?"

  • If the answer is "death or permanent injury" → see them first
  • If the answer is "increased discomfort" → they can wait
  • If the answer is "delayed recovery" → they can wait

Common NCLEX Prioritization Traps

TrapWhy It's WrongWhat to Do Instead
"The patient in pain goes first"Pain is important but not life-threateningCheck ABCs first
"The loudest patient goes first"Anxiety ≠ acuityUse objective data (vitals, labs)
"The newest admission goes first"New doesn't mean unstableAssess stability, not recency
"The doctor's orders determine priority"Orders guide care, but nursing judgment determines sequenceUse clinical judgment to sequence interventions
"The sickest patient always goes first"A stable patient with a chronic condition may wait behind an unstable patient with an acute problemAssess stability, not diagnosis

The Mental Shortcut for Exam Day

When you see a prioritization question, run through this checklist in 10 seconds:

  1. Airway compromised? → That patient wins
  2. Breathing compromised? → That patient wins
  3. Circulation compromised? → That patient wins
  4. No ABC issues? → Who could become unstable?
  5. Everyone stable? → Who has the most acute need?

Practice this until it's automatic. On exam day, you won't have time to overthink — you'll need to recognize the pattern and move.

Bottom Line

Prioritization isn't about memorizing rules. It's about developing clinical judgment — the ability to look at a situation, identify what matters most, and act decisively.

The ABCDE method gives you a framework. Practice gives you speed. Experience gives you confidence. The NCLEX tests whether you've done the work.
