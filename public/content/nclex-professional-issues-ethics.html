NCLEX Professional Issues & Ethics

Nursing ethics, scope of practice, legal issues, malpractice, and patient rights for NCLEX success.

1. Ethical Principles

📋 Core Ethical Principles

PrincipleDefinitionNursing Application
AutonomyRight to self-determinationInformed consent, respecting patient's decisions even if you disagree
BeneficenceDo goodActing in patient's best interest, advocating for them
NonmaleficenceDo no harmPreventing harm, double effect doctrine
JusticeFairness, equitable distributionEqual treatment regardless of background, resource allocation
FidelityFaithfulness, keeping promisesFollowing through on commitments, maintaining confidentiality
VeracityTruthfulnessHonest communication with patients
🚨 NCLEX Trap: When autonomy conflicts with beneficence (patient refuses life-saving treatment), autonomy USUALLY wins for competent adults. The patient has the right to make bad decisions. Exception: imminent harm to others (suicide, homicide, communicable disease reporting).

3. Legal Issues in Nursing

📋 Malpractice Elements

💡 4 Elements of Malpractice: "Duty, Breach, Causation, Damages"
1. Duty: Nurse had a duty to the patient
2. Breach: Nurse breached that duty (didn't meet standard of care)
3. Causation: Breach caused the injury
4. Damages: Patient suffered actual harm
✅ Common Areas of Liability:
• Medication errors (wrong dose, wrong patient, wrong route)
• Failure to monitor (missed changes in condition)
• Falls (inadequate precautions)
• Documentation errors (if not documented, it didn't happen)
• Failure to report (to provider, supervisor)
• Assault and battery (procedures without consent)
• False imprisonment (restraints without proper orders/precautions)

📋 Restraints

✅ Legal Requirements:
• Provider order required (renewed every 24 hours)
• Least restrictive alternative must be attempted first
• Continuous monitoring
• Release and reapply every 2 hours (check circulation, ROM, nutrition, toileting)
• Document rationale, alternatives attempted, patient response
• NEVER tie restraints to bed rails (can cause strangulation if rail lowers)
• NEVER place in prone position with restraints (asphyxiation risk)
🚨 NCLEX Trap: A nurse CANNOT apply restraints without a provider order EXCEPT in an immediate safety emergency (patient about to fall, pull out tubes, assault someone). In that case, apply and obtain order ASAP. Standing orders for restraints are NOT acceptable.

4. Patient Rights

📋 Patient's Bill of Rights

✅ Key Rights:
• Informed consent and refusal
• Privacy and confidentiality (HIPAA)
• Access to medical records
• Dignity and respect
• Pain management
• Cultural and spiritual preferences
• Complaint without retaliation
• Advance directives honored
• Know identity and credentials of caregivers
• Know costs of care

📋 HIPAA

✅ Protected Health Information (PHI):
• Name, DOB, SSN, MRN
• Diagnosis, treatment, medications
• Photos, videos
• Can share with healthcare team for treatment
• Can share with insurance for payment
• MINIMUM NECESSARY standard applies
🚨 NCLEX Trap: You CANNOT discuss patient information in public areas (elevators, cafeteria). You CANNOT access records of patients not in your care (curiosity is NOT a valid reason). You CAN share with family ONLY if patient has authorized it. In emergencies, disclose what's necessary for treatment.

5. Advance Directives

📋 Types

TypePurposeKey Points
Living WillStates wishes for end-of-life careOnly applies when terminal or permanently unconscious; cannot appoint proxy
Durable Power of Attorney for HealthcareAppoints healthcare proxyProxy makes decisions when patient cannot; most flexible
DNR/DNIDo not resuscitate/intubateMust be honored; does NOT mean "do not treat"
POLST/MOLSTPhysician orders for life-sustaining treatmentPortable, follows patient across settings
🚨 NCLEX Trap: A DNR order means NO CPR — but the patient still receives ALL other treatments (medications, comfort care, antibiotics). A DNR patient with a new infection should still receive antibiotics. "DNR" does NOT mean "do not treat."

6. Mandatory Reporting

📋 What Must Be Reported

SituationReport To
Child abuse/neglectCPS, law enforcement
Elder abuse/neglectAdult Protective Services
Domestic violence (varies by state)Law enforcement, social work
Communicable diseasesPublic health department
Gunshot wounds, stab woundsLaw enforcement
Impaired healthcare providerState board of nursing, employer
✅ Key Principle: You do NOT need proof to report suspected abuse — reasonable suspicion is enough. Good faith reporting is protected by law. Failure to report can result in criminal and professional penalties.

7. Ethical Dilemmas

📋 Common Scenarios

ScenarioEthical IssueBest Action
Patient refuses blood transfusion (Jehovah's Witness)Autonomy vs beneficenceHonor refusal for competent adult; document thoroughly; explore alternatives
Family wants to continue life support, patient previously said noAutonomy vs family wishesFollow advance directive; involve ethics committee if conflict persists
Pregnant teen requests confidentialityConfidentiality vs mandatory reportingVaries by state; know your state's laws on minor consent and reporting
Patient requests assistance with suicideAutonomy vs nonmaleficenceAssisted suicide is illegal in most states; provide palliative care, psychiatric support
Nurse observes colleague making errorsFidelity vs loyaltyAddress directly first; if continues, report to supervisor; patient safety is priority
