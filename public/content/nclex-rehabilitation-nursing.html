Rehabilitation Nursing

Stroke recovery, traumatic brain injury, spinal cord injury, amputation, and restorative nursing care for the NCLEX.

Stroke Rehabilitation

Phases of Stroke Recovery

  • Acute (0–7 days): Medical stabilization, prevent complications, early mobilization, swallow screening
  • Subacute (1 week–6 months): Intensive rehab, functional recovery (most rapid gains), neuroplasticity window
  • Chronic (>6 months): Maintenance, compensation, community reintegration

Common Post-Stroke Impairments

  • Motor: Hemiparesis/hemiplegia, spasticity, dyscoordination
  • Sensory: Neglect (usually left-sided), proprioception loss, visual field cuts
  • Cognitive: Apraxia, aphasia (expressive, receptive, global), memory deficits
  • Emotional: Depression (30–50%), pseudobulbar affect, emotional lability
  • Functional: Dysphagia, urinary incontinence, falls risk

Left Hemisphere vs. Right Hemisphere Stroke

Mnemonic: "Left = Language, Right = Reckless" — Left hemisphere strokes affect language (aphasia). Right hemisphere strokes cause spatial neglect and impulsive behavior.
Stroke Nursing Priority: Fall prevention (hemiparesis, neglect). Position affected extremities to prevent contractures. Screen for dysphagia before ANY oral intake. Communication strategies for aphasia (pictures, simple commands, allow time). Depression screening. Caregiver education and support.

Spinal Cord Injury (SCI)

Complete vs. Incomplete SCI

  • Complete: No motor or sensory function preserved in sacral segments S4–S5
  • Incomplete: Some preserved function below injury level. Syndromes:
    • Central cord: Upper extremities weaker than lower (cervical hyperextension). Common in elderly with cervical spondylosis
    • Anterior cord: Motor loss + pain/temperature loss; proprioception preserved (anterior spinal artery occlusion)
    • Brown-Séquard: Ipsilateral motor + proprioception loss; contralateral pain/temperature loss (hemisection)
    • Posterior cord: Proprioception/vibration loss; motor and pain/temperature preserved (B12 deficiency, tabes dorsalis)

Autonomic Dysreflexia — EMERGENCY

Occurs in: T6 and above injuries (below-level noxious stimulus triggers sympathetic outflow without brain modulation)

Signs: Sudden severe hypertension (systolic >200–300), pounding headache, bradycardia, flushing/sweating above lesion, pale/cool skin below lesion, nasal stuffiness, blurred vision, anxiety

Common triggers: Bladder distention (#1 cause), bowel impaction, tight clothing, pressure injury, ingrown toenail, UTI, labor

Immediate action:

  1. Sit patient upright (lower blood pressure in head)
  2. Loosen tight clothing
  3. Check bladder — catheterize if distended
  4. Check bowel — disimpact if needed
  5. Check skin — remove pressure sources
  6. If BP remains elevated, antihypertensives (nifedipine, nitroglycerin)
NCLEX Trap: Autonomic dysreflexia is a MEDICAL EMERGENCY. Do NOT treat the headache with typical analgesics — treat the CAUSE (usually bladder or bowel). Also, SCI patients have NO sensation below injury but CAN have pain in areas with preserved sensation (at-level neuropathic pain).

SCI Complications & Prevention

  • Respiratory: C1–C4 = ventilator dependent; C5–C6 = weak diaphragm, reduced vital capacity. Monitor ABGs, encourage deep breathing, assisted cough techniques
  • DVT/PE: Anticoagulation prophylaxis, compression stockings, early mobilization
  • Pressure injuries: Turn every 2 hours, pressure-relieving cushion, skin checks
  • Spasticity: Baclofen, tizanidine, stretching, botulinum toxin
  • Neurogenic bowel: Bowel program (digital stimulation, suppositories, scheduled timing)
  • Neurogenic bladder: Intermittent catheterization (gold standard), prevent overdistention
  • Heterotopic ossification: Around large joints. NSAIDs or radiation for prevention
  • Osteoporosis: Below-level bone loss. Weight-bearing if possible, calcium/vitamin D

(prosthetic) Amputation

Postoperative Care

  • Positioning: Do NOT elevate residual limb on pillow (promotes hip flexion contracture for BKA; abduction contracture for AKA). Lay flat or prone periodically
  • Residual limb shaping: Elastic wrap (figure-8 pattern, distal-to-proximal, even pressure) or shrinker sock
  • Phantom limb pain: Normal, occurs in 50–80%. Treat with mirror therapy, TENS, medications (gabapentin, pregabalin, tricyclics)
  • Phantom limb sensation: Non-painful sensation of limb presence — normal, no treatment needed
  • Contracture prevention: Daily stretching, prone lying (for hip extension), proper positioning
Amputation Nursing Priority: Monitor for hemorrhage (reinforce dressing, do NOT remove initial dressing). Check residual limb circulation (color, temperature, capillary refill). Desensitization techniques (tapping, massage). Psychological support (grief, body image). Early prosthetic fitting when wound healed.
NCLEX Trap: Phantom limb pain is REAL pain (not psychological). Do NOT dismiss it. Also, NEVER place a BKA patient's residual limb on a pillow — this causes hip flexion contracture that prevents prosthetic fitting.

Mild TBI: GCS 13–15. Moderate: GCS 9–12. Severe: GCS 3–8

TBI Nursing Priority: Prevent secondary brain injury. Maintain cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP >60 mmHg). Avoid hypotension, hypoxia, hyperthermia, hyperglycemia. Head of bed 30°. Seizure prophylaxis (first 7 days). Monitor ICP if indicated. Aggressive pulmonary toilet. Nutrition support (enteral preferred). Minimize stimulation.

Clinical Judgment Case Study

Rehabilitation Stroke

Case: Hemiparesis Safety

A 65-year-old with right-sided hemiparesis after a left MCA stroke is being prepared for discharge. The spouse will be the primary caregiver. The patient uses a walker but drags the right foot.

What is the priority home safety teaching?

  1. Remove all throw rugs and install grab bars in the bathroom
  2. Teach the spouse to assist with all transfers
  3. Recommend a wheelchair for all mobility
  4. Order an AFO (ankle-foot orthosis) to prevent foot drop
Answer: 1 — Remove throw rugs and install grab bars.

Falls are the #1 cause of injury in stroke survivors. Environmental modifications are the most effective fall prevention strategy. Throw rugs are a major trip hazard. Grab bars (shower, toilet) are essential. AFO helps with foot drop but doesn't prevent all falls. Wheelchair is overly restrictive — encourage safe ambulation with assistive devices. Spouse assistance is good but the patient needs independence strategies too. OT home evaluation is ideal.
