(fall) Fall Risk Factors

Intrinsic (Patient) Factors

  • Age >65 (especially >85)
  • History of falls — strongest predictor
  • Impaired mobility — weakness, gait instability, balance problems
  • Cognitive impairment — dementia, delirium, confusion
  • Sensory deficits — vision, proprioception
  • Orthostatic hypotension
  • Incontinence/urgency — rushing to bathroom
  • Medications — sedatives, antihypertensives, diuretics, opioids

Extrinsic (Environmental) Factors

  • Clutter, cords, wet floors
  • Poor lighting
  • Bed too high or wheels not locked
  • Lack of grab bars in bathroom
  • Improper footwear (socks without grips)
  • Unfamiliar environment

High-Risk Times for Falls:

  • First 24-48 hours of hospitalization
  • First time getting out of bed
  • During the night (toileting)
  • After medication changes (especially sedatives)
  • During procedures/transfers

Hendrich II Fall Risk Model

Designed for acute care. More predictive than Morse in hospital settings. Score ≥5 = high risk.

Risk Factor Points

Interpretation:

  • 0-4: Low risk
  • ≥5: High risk — implement fall protocol

Fall Prevention Strategies

Universal Precautions (All Patients)

  • Bed in lowest position with wheels locked
  • Call light within reach
  • Personal items within reach
  • Non-slip socks or proper footwear
  • Adequate lighting
  • Clear pathways
  • Hourly rounding (toilet, pain, position, possessions)

High-Risk Interventions

Intervention Purpose
Bed alarm Alerts staff when patient attempts to get up unassisted
Chair alarm Same for chair/wheelchair
Floor mats Cushion fall if patient gets out of bed
Sitter/companion One-on-one observation for confused patients
Yellow socks/gown Visual cue to all staff: high fall risk
signage Reminds patient to call for help
Scheduled toileting Prevents urgent rushes to bathroom

Mnemonic: FALL PREVENTION

  • F — Footwear (non-slip)
  • A — Alarms (bed/chair)
  • L — Lighting (adequate)
  • L — Low bed position
  • P — Pathways (clear)
  • R — Rounding (hourly)
  • E — Education (patient/family)
  • V — Vital signs (orthostatic)
  • E — Eyes (vision check)
  • N — Non-slip socks
  • T — Toileting (scheduled)
  • I — Identify (yellow socks/sign)
  • O — Orient (reorient confused patients)
  • N — Nurses (adequate staffing)

Restraints & Alternatives

CMS Regulations (Medicare/Medicaid)

Restraints are a LAST RESORT. They require:

  • Provider order within 1 hour of application
  • In-person evaluation within 24 hours
  • Continuous monitoring
  • Release and reapplication every 2 hours
  • Range of motion exercises at least every 2 hours
  • Order renewal every 24 hours (4 hours for chemical restraints)

Alternatives to Restraints (Try These First!)

Problem Alternative Interventions
Pulling at tubes/lines Mitten gloves, tube securement devices, distraction
Getting out of bed Bed alarm, low bed, floor mat, sitter
Wandering Camouflage door, alarmed doors, supervised activity
Aggression De-escalation, environmental changes, 1:1 observation
Agitation Music, family presence, reorientation, comfort measures

Types of Restraints

  • Physical: Wrist/ankle soft restraints, vest restraints, mitts
  • Chemical: Medications given primarily for discipline or staff convenience (NOT for treating a medical condition)
  • Environmental: Side rails (4 side rails up can be considered a restraint if patient cannot get out)

NEVER:

  • Use restraints as punishment
  • Use restraints for staff convenience
  • Restrain in prone position
  • Leave restrained patient unattended

Seizure Precautions

Environmental Safety

  • Bed in lowest position
  • Side rails padded (if used)
  • Oxygen and suction at bedside
  • Oral airway available
  • Remove sharp objects, glasses

During a Seizure

DO:

  • Note time seizure started
  • Protect from injury (clear area, pad head)
  • Turn to side (recovery position)
  • Loosen restrictive clothing
  • Stay with patient

DO NOT:

  • Restrain the patient
  • Put anything in the mouth
  • Give oral medications/food/water during seizure

When to Call Emergency

  • Seizure lasts >5 minutes (status epilepticus)
  • Repeated seizures without recovery
  • First-ever seizure
  • Injury occurs
  • Pregnancy
  • No rescue medication available

Suicide Precautions

Risk Factors

  • Previous suicide attempt
  • Depression, hopelessness
  • Substance use
  • Recent loss (job, relationship, health)
  • Access to lethal means
  • Social isolation
  • Male gender, age >65 or 15-24

Safety Interventions

Level Interventions
Standard Regular checks, remove sharps, assess mental status
High Risk 1:1 observation, remove all potential hazards, no sharps including plastic
Maximum Constant visual observation, sitter, safety gown only, strip search if indicated

What to Remove

  • Sharps (razors, scissors, needles)
  • Belts, shoelaces, drawstrings
  • Glass containers
  • Electrical cords
  • Plastic bags
  • Medications not immediately needed

NGN Case Study: Fall Risk Assessment

Patient Presentation

Mrs. Williams, 78 y/o, is admitted for pneumonia. She lives alone and fell twice in the past month. She uses a walker at home but is currently too weak to ambulate.

  • VS: BP 142/88, HR 92, RR 22, SpO₂ 90% on RA
  • Mental status: Oriented but forgets to use call light
  • Medications: HCTZ, lorazepam PRN for anxiety
  • IV: Normal saline at 75 mL/hr
  • Assessment: Weak, needs assistance to stand

What is the priority nursing intervention?

Correct: B

Rationale: Mrs. Williams has multiple fall risk factors: age >65, history of falls, forgets call light, on lorazepam, has IV. The priority is a comprehensive fall prevention plan:

  • Bed alarm alerts staff if she tries to get up
  • Hourly rounding addresses toileting needs before she gets up alone
  • Low bed, non-slip socks, clear pathways

Why not others:

  • A: Restraints are last resort and require a provider order. Alternatives must be tried first.
  • C: 4 side rails up can be considered a restraint and increases fall injury risk if she climbs over.
  • D: Far from nurses' station decreases observation and increases risk.
