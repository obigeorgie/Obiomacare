NCLEX Sensory Disorders

Eye conditions, ear disorders, glaucoma, cataracts, and hearing/vision loss for NCLEX success.

1. Eye Conditions

Glaucoma — "The Silent Thief of Sight"

Patho: Increased intraocular pressure (IOP) → optic nerve damage → irreversible vision loss.

Open-Angle GlaucomaAngle-Closure Glaucoma
OnsetGradual, painlessSudden, painful
SymptomsPeripheral vision loss, halos around lightsSevere eye pain, headache, blurred vision, halos, nausea/vomiting, red eye, fixed mid-dilated pupil
Emergency?No — chronic managementYES — acute angle-closure is an ophthalmologic emergency
TreatmentEye drops (prostaglandins, beta-blockers, alpha-agonists), laser, surgeryAcetazolamide, mannitol, topical drops, laser iridotomy
Nursing Priorities:
• Monitor IOP (normal 10-21 mmHg)
• Eye drop administration: teach punctal occlusion (press inner corner for 1-2 min to prevent systemic absorption)
• Beta-blocker eye drops: check pulse (systemic absorption can cause bradycardia)
• Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (acetazolamide): monitor for metabolic acidosis, hypokalemia
• For angle-closure: dark room, semi-Fowler's, pain management, prepare for laser iridotomy
NCLEX Trap: Acute angle-closure glaucoma is a MEDICAL EMERGENCY. Do NOT give miotics (pilocarpine) in the affected eye FIRST — the pupil is already mid-dilated and fixed. Treatment priority is lowering IOP with acetazolamide, mannitol, and topical agents, then laser iridotomy.

Cataracts

Symptoms: Cloudy/painless vision, difficulty driving at night, glare, halos around lights, faded colors
Nursing Priorities:
• Surgery is definitive treatment (phacoemulsification + IOL implant)
• Post-op: eye shield at night, avoid bending/straining, no rubbing eye
• Eye drops: antibiotic + steroid (prevent infection and inflammation)
• Avoid heavy lifting for 1-2 weeks
• Report sudden pain, vision loss, or discharge immediately
• Driving restrictions until cleared by ophthalmologist

Retinal Detachment

Symptoms: Floaters, flashes of light (photopsia), curtain/shadow over visual field
Risk factors: High myopia, trauma, diabetes, post-cataract surgery
Nursing Priorities:
• EMERGENCY — vision loss can be permanent if not repaired promptly
• Bed rest with eye patch (prevent further detachment)
• Position patient so detachment is LOWEST (gravity helps)
• No straining, coughing, or Valsalva
• Surgical repair: pneumatic retinopexy, scleral buckle, vitrectomy
• Post-op: positioning critical (face-down for gas bubble in vitrectomy)

Macular Degeneration

Dry (Atrophic): Gradual central vision loss, drusen deposits. No cure — AREDS vitamins may slow progression.
Wet (Exudative): Sudden distortion, straight lines appear wavy. Anti-VEGF injections (bevacizumab, ranibizumab) into vitreous.
Nursing Priorities:
• Amsler grid monitoring at home (catches early wet conversion)
• Low vision aids and resources
• Smoking cessation (major risk factor)
• AREDS2 supplements (for intermediate dry AMD)
• Anti-VEGF injections: monitor for endophthalmitis (infection inside eye)

Eye Drop Administration Techniques

Correct Technique:
• Wash hands
• Tilt head back, pull down lower lid to form pocket
• Instill drop into pocket (NOT directly onto cornea)
• Release lid, close eye gently (don't squeeze)
• Apply gentle pressure to inner corner (punctal occlusion) for 1-2 minutes
• Wait 5-10 minutes between different eye drops
• If both drops and ointment: drops first, ointment last
• Never touch dropper tip to eye

2. Ear Conditions

Ménière's Disease

Classic Triad: Vertigo (hours, not minutes), tinnitus, sensorineural hearing loss + aural fullness
Patho: Endolymphatic hydrops (excess fluid in inner ear)
Nursing Priorities:
• During attack: bed rest, dim lights, antiemetics, antihistamines (meclizine), benzodiazepines
• Low-sodium diet (reduces fluid retention)
• Avoid caffeine, alcohol, nicotine
• Stress management
• Fall prevention (vertigo)
• Surgical options if refractory: endolymphatic sac decompression, vestibular nerve section, labyrinthectomy

Otitis Media vs Otitis Externa

Otitis MediaOtitis Externa (Swimmer's Ear)
LocationMiddle earExternal ear canal
Common causeViral/bacterial (S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae)Pseudomonas (water exposure)
PainDull ache, worse when lying downSevere pain with tragus/pinna movement
SignsBulging TM, fever, decreased hearingEdema, erythema, purulent drainage, "cauliflower ear"

NCLEX Trap: For otitis externa, do NOT insert cotton swabs or any objects into the ear canal. "Keep ear dry" means using earplugs when showering/swimming. For severe cases with canal swelling, place a wick to allow drops to flow down to the infected area.

Hearing Loss

TypeCauseTreatment
ConductiveImpacted cerumen, otosclerosis, foreign body, perforated TMRemove obstruction, hearing aids, surgery
SensorineuralPresbycusis, noise exposure, ototoxic drugs, acoustic neuroma, Meniere'sHearing aids, cochlear implant (if severe), avoid ototoxic drugs

Ototoxic Drugs to Know:
• Aminoglycosides (gentamicin, tobramycin)
• Loop diuretics (furosemide, ethacrynic acid)
• Platinum-based chemo (cisplatin, carboplatin)
• Aspirin (high doses, reversible)
• Vancomycin (at high trough levels)
NCLEX Trap: Sensorineural hearing loss from ototoxic drugs is often PERMANENT. Monitor hearing before and during treatment. Tinnitus or high-frequency hearing loss may be early signs. Report immediately if patient reports new hearing changes.

3. Sensory Safety and Communication

Nursing Care for Vision/Hearing Impaired

Vision Impairment:
• Announce your presence when entering room
• Describe environment and activities
• Maintain consistent room setup
• Use large print, high contrast, adequate lighting
• Orient to room (bed, bathroom, call light)
• Guide by letting patient hold your arm (don't pull them)
• Keep pathways clear
Hearing Impairment:
• Face the patient, speak clearly, reduce background noise
• Don't shout (distorts speech)
• Use written communication if needed
• Check hearing aid function (battery, insertion, volume)
• Speak at normal pace — slow exaggerated speech is harder to lip-read
• Get patient's attention before speaking
• Rephrase rather than repeat if not understood

Clinical Judgment Case Study

Sensory Eye

Case: Acute Vision Loss

A 62-year-old reports sudden, painless vision loss in the right eye — "like a curtain came down." She has a history of hypertension and diabetes. The affected pupil is dilated and non-reactive.

What is the nurse's immediate action?

  1. Check blood glucose to rule out diabetic retinopathy
  2. Place the patient supine and notify ophthalmology immediately
  3. Apply a patch to the affected eye and schedule follow-up
  4. Administer acetazolamide to reduce intraocular pressure
Answer: 2 — Place supine and notify ophthalmology immediately.

"Curtain coming down" + dilated non-reactive pupil = retinal detachment or central retinal artery occlusion (CRAO) — both are ophthalmologic emergencies. Time is vision — CRAO causes irreversible damage within 90-120 minutes. Keep patient supine (may improve perfusion). Do NOT patch (needs continuous monitoring). Do NOT delay for glucose check. Acetazolamide is for glaucoma, not this presentation. Emergency ophthalmology consult for possible laser/surgical intervention.
