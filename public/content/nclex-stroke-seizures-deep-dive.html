Stroke & Seizures Deep Dive

Ischemic vs hemorrhagic stroke, TIA, seizure types, status epilepticus, and nursing management for the NCLEX.

Stroke (Cerebrovascular Accident)

Ischemic Stroke (85% of strokes)

Pathophysiology: Occlusion of cerebral artery → interruption of blood flow → ischemia → infarction

Causes: Thrombosis (atherosclerosis), embolism (AFib, carotid plaque, DVT with PFO), lacunar (small vessel disease)

Manifestations: Depends on affected vessel/territory
  • MCA (most common): Contralateral hemiparesis/hemiplegia (face/arm > leg), aphasia (dominant hemisphere), neglect (non-dominant), homonymous hemianopia
  • ACA: Contralateral leg weakness > arm, abulia, urinary incontinence
  • PCA: Visual field defects, alexia, memory deficits
  • Vertebrobasilar: Cranial nerve deficits, ataxia, vertigo, dysphagia, locked-in syndrome (basilar artery)
Acute Management (Time-Critical):
  1. Activate stroke protocol — "Time is brain"
  2. CT scan non-contrast: Rule out hemorrhage (must be done BEFORE thrombolytics)
  3. Thrombolytics (tPA/alteplase): IV if within 4.5 hours of symptom onset, no contraindications
  4. Mechanical thrombectomy: Large vessel occlusion within 6-24 hours (window extended with imaging selection)
  5. Aspirin: Within 24-48 hours if not receiving thrombolytics
  6. Blood pressure: Allow permissive hypertension (up to 220/120) in acute ischemic stroke to maintain perfusion. Lower ONLY if receiving thrombolytics (goal <185/110) or if hypertensive emergency.
NCLEX Trap: Do NOT give aspirin within 24 hours of thrombolytics (hemorrhage risk). Do NOT lower BP aggressively in acute ischemic stroke (reduces perfusion to penumbra). BP goals: If no tPA → allow up to 220/120. If tPA → <185/110 before, <180/105 after.

Hemorrhagic Stroke (15% of strokes)

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH): Bleeding into brain parenchyma. Causes: HTN (most common), amyloid angiopathy, coagulopathy, vascular malformations, tumor.

Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH): Bleeding into subarachnoid space. Most common cause: ruptured aneurysm. "Thunderclap headache" — worst headache of life.

Manifestations: Sudden severe headache ("thunderclap" for SAH), nausea/vomiting, decreased LOC, seizures, focal deficits, meningeal signs (neck stiffness, photophobia), coma
Management:
  • Reverse anticoagulation: PCC, vitamin K (warfarin), idarucizumab (dabigatran), andexanet alfa (Xa inhibitors)
  • BP control: Goal <140-160 systolic (reduces hematoma expansion)
  • ICP management: Head of bed 30°, osmotherapy (mannitol, hypertonic saline), possible ventriculostomy for hydrocephalus
  • Seizure prophylaxis: If ICH with cortical involvement
  • SAH specific: Nimodipine (prevents vasospasm), early aneurysm clipping/coiling, triple-H therapy (hypertension, hypervolemia, hemodilution)
NCLEX Trap: Nimodipine is given for SAH to prevent vasospasm — NOT for BP control (can cause hypotension, give cautiously). Monitor for vasospasm (worsening neuro 4-14 days after SAH — "symptomatic vasospasm").

Post-Stroke Nursing Care

First 24 Hours:
  • Neuro checks q1-2h (GCS, pupillary response, motor function, speech)
  • BP monitoring (permissive hypertension unless tPA given)
  • Strict I&O, avoid hypotonic fluids (cerebral edema risk)
  • NIHSS scoring
  • Swallow screening before ANY oral intake (aspiration risk)
  • DVT prophylaxis (after 24-48h if no hemorrhage)
Prevent Complications:
  • Aspiration precautions — NPO until swallow eval, elevate HOB, thickened liquids if needed
  • Pressure injury prevention — frequent repositioning, special mattress
  • Contracture prevention — ROM exercises, splinting
  • Communication support — speech therapy, communication boards
  • Emotional support — depression common post-stroke

Seizures

Classification

Focal (Partial) Seizures:

  • Focal aware (simple partial): Consciousness preserved. Motor, sensory, autonomic, or psychic symptoms.
  • Focal impaired awareness (complex partial): Altered consciousness. Automatisms (lip smacking, picking at clothes). Post-ictal confusion.
  • Focal to bilateral tonic-clonic: Starts focal, spreads to both hemispheres.

Generalized Seizures:

  • Tonic-clonic (grand mal): Tonic phase (stiffening) → clonic phase (jerking). Post-ictal confusion, fatigue, headache, Todd's paralysis.
  • Absence (petit mal): Brief loss of consciousness (seconds), staring, no post-ictal confusion. Childhood onset.
  • Atonic: Sudden loss of muscle tone → "drop attacks"
  • Myoclonic: Brief jerks

Status Epilepticus

Definition: Seizure >5 minutes OR recurrent seizures without recovery of consciousness between

Medical emergency — neuronal damage after 30 minutes, mortality increases with duration

Management Protocol:
  1. ABCs: Airway, breathing, circulation. Oxygen, suction, position on side. Do NOT force objects into mouth.
  2. Lorazepam 4 mg IV (repeat x1 if needed) — first line
  3. Second-line: Fosphenytoin, valproic acid, or levetiracetam IV
  4. Refractory: Midazolam or propofol infusion, intubation
  5. Identify cause: Glucose (give thiamine BEFORE glucose if malnourished), electrolytes, ABG, tox screen, head CT, antiepileptic levels
NCLEX Trap: Do NOT restrain during seizure (muscle injury). Do NOT put anything in mouth (broken teeth, aspiration). Time the seizure. Protect from injury (clear area, pad headboard). After seizure: recovery position, monitor airway, assess for injury, reassure patient.

Common Antiepileptic Drugs (AEDs)

Drug Key Side Effects Key Monitoring
Phenytoin Gingival hyperplasia, hirsutism, ataxia, SJS/TEN risk Drug levels, therapeutic 10-20 mcg/mL
Carbamazepine Hyponatremia, SJS/TEN, hepatotoxicity, bone marrow suppression CBC, LFTs, Na⁺, levels
Valproic acid Weight gain, tremor, hair loss, hepatotoxicity, pancreatitis, teratogenic LFTs, ammonia, platelets
Levetiracetam Behavioral changes, irritability, somnolence Renal dosing
Lamotrigine Rash (SJS/TEN — slow titration), dizziness Titrate slowly

NGN Case Study: Acute Ischemic Stroke & tPA

Client Scenario

Mr. David Kim, 72-year-old male, is brought to the emergency department by his wife. She states he "suddenly couldn't talk right" and his "right side went weak" about 45 minutes ago while they were having breakfast. He has a history of hypertension, atrial fibrillation (not on anticoagulation due to previous GI bleed), and hyperlipidemia. He was last known well at 07:15 when his wife saw him reading the newspaper normally. Current time is 08:15.

Vital Signs Nurse's Notes Lab Results Provider Orders

Vital Signs & Assessment

  • BP: 188/104 mmHg
  • HR: 88 bpm, irregularly irregular
  • RR: 18 breaths/min
  • Temp: 36.6°C (97.9°F)
  • SpO₂: 96% on room air
  • Weight: 82 kg
  • Appearance: Alert, anxious, aphasic
  • NIHSS Score: 14 (moderate-severe stroke)
  • Facial droop: Present on right
  • Arm drift: Right arm falls rapidly
  • Leg strength: Right leg 2/5, left 5/5
  • Speech: Expressive aphasia — unable to name objects, follows simple commands
  • Visual fields: Right homonymous hemianopsia

Nurse's Notes

Wife reports patient ran out of his amlodipine and atorvastatin 2 weeks ago. He was supposed to start apixaban after his GI bleed resolved 6 months ago but "never got around to it." No headache, no trauma, no seizure activity witnessed. No recent surgery. No family history of stroke. Non-smoker. Occasional alcohol use.

Lab Results

  • Glucose: 142 mg/dL
  • Platelets: 245 × 10⁹/L
  • INR: 1.0
  • PTT: 32 seconds
  • Creatinine: 1.1 mg/dL
  • Hgb: 13.8 g/dL
  • WBC: 7.8 × 10⁹/L

Provider Orders

  1. CT head without contrast (STAT)
  2. CT angiography head/neck
  3. Telemetric monitoring
  4. IV access × 2, labs sent
  5. If CT negative for hemorrhage: alteplase (tPA) 0.9 mg/kg IV (max 90 mg) — 10% bolus over 1 min, remainder over 60 min
  6. BP goal <185/110 before tPA; <180/105 after tPA
  7. Neuro checks q15 min × 2 hr, q30 min × 6 hr, q1h × 16 hr

Clinical Judgment Questions

Question 1 — Recognize Cues: Which findings support the diagnosis of acute ischemic stroke (rather than hemorrhagic) at this point? (Select all that apply)

  1. Sudden onset focal neurological deficits
  2. Absence of severe headache
  3. Atrial fibrillation with subtherapeutic anticoagulation
  4. NIHSS score of 14
  5. Normal INR of 1.0
  6. Right-sided weakness with aphasia
Correct: A, B, C, E, F
Sudden focal deficits (A) are classic for both ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke. Absence of severe headache (B) favors ischemic (hemorrhagic typically presents with "thunderclap" headache). A-fib without anticoagulation (C) is a major risk factor for cardioembolic ischemic stroke. Normal INR (E) means no anticoagulation effect. Right weakness + aphasia (F) suggests left MCA territory ischemia. NIHSS (D) quantifies severity but doesn't differentiate stroke type — CT is required.

Question 2 — Analyze Cues: The CT scan shows no hemorrhage. The neurologist confirms the patient is a candidate for IV tPA. What is the DEADLINE for administering tPA from last known well?

  1. 3 hours
  2. 4.5 hours
  3. 6 hours
  4. 24 hours
Correct: B
IV alteplase (tPA) is FDA-approved for acute ischemic stroke within 3 hours of symptom onset. The AHA/ASA guidelines extend this to 4.5 hours in eligible patients (age <80, NIHSS <25, not on anticoagulants, no prior stroke + diabetes). This patient meets extended criteria (age 72, NIHSS 14, INR 1.0). Mechanical thrombectomy has up to 24-hour windows in selected patients.

Question 3 — Prioritize Hypotheses: The BP is 188/104. Which is the nurse's BEST action BEFORE tPA administration?

  1. Administer labetalol 10 mg IV push to lower BP below 180/105
  2. Administer labetalol to lower BP below 185/110
  3. Hold tPA until BP normalizes with oral medications
  4. Proceed with tPA — blood pressure is acceptable
Correct: B
tPA requires BP <185/110 BEFORE administration and <180/105 AFTER. Labetalol 10-20 mg IV, hydralazine 10-20 mg IV, or nicardipine infusion are options. Do NOT delay tPA excessively — the clock is ticking. Goal is rapid BP control to meet tPA criteria while minimizing door-to-needle time.

Question 4 — Generate Solutions: The tPA infusion is running. Which action is MOST important during and after the infusion?

  1. Keep the head of bed flat to maximize cerebral perfusion
  2. Monitor for signs of bleeding, especially neurological deterioration
  3. Administer aspirin 325 mg immediately to prevent clot progression
  4. Insert a Foley catheter to monitor urine output
Correct: B
The major risk of tPA is hemorrhagic transformation. Monitor closely for sudden headache, nausea, vomiting, decreased LOC, or new neuro deficits — these are red flags for intracranial bleeding. Stop tPA immediately and obtain STAT CT if suspected. Head of bed at 30° (not flat, A) is standard. Aspirin (C) is delayed 24 hours post-tPA until repeat CT confirms no hemorrhage. Foley (D) is not a priority during tPA.

Question 5 — Take Action: Twenty minutes into the tPA infusion, the patient suddenly becomes very drowsy and his right-sided weakness worsens. What is the nurse's FIRST action?

  1. Complete the tPA infusion and notify the provider
  2. Stop the tPA infusion immediately and call the stroke team
  3. Increase the tPA rate to finish faster
  4. Recheck the blood pressure
Correct: B
Sudden neurological deterioration during tPA = suspected intracranial hemorrhage until proven otherwise. STOP the infusion immediately, call the stroke team/neurologist, and obtain emergent CT head. Do NOT complete the infusion (A) — this could worsen bleeding. BP check (D) is important but secondary to stopping the thrombolytic.

Question 6 — Evaluate Outcomes: The patient completes tPA uneventfully. Twenty-four hours later, repeat CT shows no hemorrhage. Which statement by the patient indicates understanding of secondary stroke prevention?

  1. "I don't need blood thinners because the tPA broke up the clot."
  2. "I'll start my apixaban as prescribed and keep all my follow-up appointments."
  3. "I can stop taking my blood pressure pills once I'm feeling better."
  4. "Stroke won't happen again now that I've had tPA."
Correct: B
This patient has atrial fibrillation — a major risk factor for recurrent cardioembolic stroke. Anticoagulation (apixaban) is essential for secondary prevention. BP control, statin therapy, and lifestyle modifications are also critical. tPA treats the ACUTE stroke but does NOT prevent recurrence. Stopping medications (C) or declining anticoagulation (A) puts the patient at high risk for another stroke.
Case Study Learning Points:
  • Time is brain — tPA window: 4.5 hours from last known well
  • BP must be <185/110 before tPA — Use labetalol, hydralazine, or nicardipine
  • Monitor for bleeding — Sudden neuro decline = stop tPA, STAT CT
  • No aspirin for 24 hours — Must confirm no hemorrhage on repeat CT
  • AFib needs anticoagulation — tPA doesn't replace long-term stroke prevention
