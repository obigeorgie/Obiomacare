Wound Care and Pressure Injuries: NCLEX Study Guide

Introduction

Wound care is fundamental nursing. The NCLEX tests wound assessment, dressing selection, pressure injury staging, and complication recognition. This guide covers the essential knowledge with clinical scenarios.

Wound Healing Phases

Phase 1: Hemostasis (Minutes to Hours)

  • Vasoconstriction
  • Platelet aggregation
  • Clot formation
  • Fibrin mesh

Nursing implication: Protect the clot. Don't disturb fresh wounds.

Phase 2: Inflammatory (Days 1-6)

  • Redness, heat, swelling, pain
  • White blood cells migrate to wound
  • Neutrophils first (24-48 hours)
  • Macrophages follow (48+ hours)

Normal signs: Mild erythema, edema, warmth
Abnormal signs: Increasing pain, purulent drainage, foul odor, spreading redness

Phase 3: Proliferative (Days 3-21)

  • Granulation tissue forms
  • Wound contracts
  • Epithelial cells migrate across wound bed
  • Collagen synthesis

Signs of healing: Pink, bumpy granulation tissue; decreasing wound size

Phase 4: Maturation (Weeks to Months)

  • Collagen remodeling
  • Scar formation
  • Wound gains strength

Timeline:

  • 1 week: 3-5% of original strength
  • 2 weeks: 15-20%
  • 3 weeks: 30-40%
  • 1 year: 70-80% (never 100%)

NCLEX tip: A wound is NEVER as strong as uninjured tissue. This matters for activity restrictions.

Wound Assessment: TIME

Letter Meaning Assessment
T Tissue Viable vs. non-viable (necrotic, slough)
I Infection/Inflammation Signs of infection, bioburden
M Moisture Too dry? Too wet? Balanced?
E Edge Advancing or non-advancing?

Wound Drainage Types

Type Description Significance
Sanguineous Bloody, red Normal first 24-48 hours
Serosanguineous Pink, thin Normal during healing
Serous Clear, yellow Normal
Purulent Thick, yellow/green, foul odor INFECTION
Hemorrhagic Bright red, frank blood BLEEDING

NCLEX scenario:

"A post-op patient has wound drainage that is thick, green, and foul-smelling. What is the FIRST action?"

Answer: Notify provider and obtain wound culture. This is purulent drainage indicating infection.

Pressure Injury Staging (NPIAP 2019)

Stage 1

  • Intact skin with non-blanchable erythema
  • May be difficult to see on dark skin
  • Feels: Warm, firm, boggy, or painful

Key point: Skin is NOT broken. This is a warning sign.

Stage 2

  • Partial-thickness skin loss
  • Shallow open ulcer
  • Pink/red wound bed
  • NO slough or bruising

May present as:

  • Shallow crater
  • Intact or ruptured serum-filled blister

Stage 3

  • Full-thickness skin loss
  • Subcutaneous fat visible
  • May have slough or eschar
  • NO bone, tendon, or muscle visible

May include: Undermining or tunneling

Stage 4

  • Full-thickness tissue loss
  • Bone, tendon, or muscle exposed
  • May have slough or eschar on edges
  • Often with undermining or tunneling

Unstageable

  • Full-thickness wound
  • Covered with slough or eschar
  • Wound base cannot be seen

Action: Debride to stage. Do NOT stage until base is visible.

Deep Tissue Pressure Injury (DTPI)

  • Intact or non-intact skin
  • Localized area of persistent non-blanchable deep red, maroon, or purple discoloration
  • Or epidermal separation revealing dark wound bed or blood-filled blister

Key point: This is deeper than it looks. The damage is at the muscle/bone level.

NCLEX scenario:

"A patient has an area on the coccyx that is intact but deep purple. It does not blanch. How is this staged?"

Answer: Deep Tissue Pressure Injury (DTPI). Do NOT stage as Stage 1. The purple color indicates deep tissue damage.

Medical Device-Related Pressure Injury

  • Caused by medical device (oxygen tubing, Foley, NG tube, cast)
  • Stage according to wound appearance
  • Document shape that matches device

Pressure Injury Risk Assessment: Braden Scale

Factor Score 4 Score 3 Score 2 Score 1
Sensory perception Completely limited Very limited Slightly limited No impairment
Moisture Constantly moist Very moist Occasionally moist Rarely moist
Activity Bedfast Chairfast Walks occasionally Walks frequently
Mobility Completely immobile Very limited Slightly limited No limitations
Nutrition Very poor Probably inadequate Adequate Excellent
Friction/Shear Problem Potential problem No apparent problem —

Scoring:

  • 15-18: Mild risk
  • 13-14: Moderate risk
  • 10-12: High risk
  • ≤9: Very high risk

NCLEX tip: Lower score = higher risk. Most hospitals use <18 as threshold for interventions.

Pressure Injury Prevention

Turn and Reposition

  • Every 2 hours in bed
  • Every 1 hour in chair
  • Use draw sheet to prevent shear
  • 30-degree lateral position (not 90-degree side-lying)

Support Surfaces

  • Low-air-loss mattress (high risk)
  • Alternating pressure mattress
  • Gel or foam overlays
  • DONUT cushions are CONTRAINDICATED (cause venous congestion)

Nutrition

  • High protein (1.2-1.5 g/kg/day)
  • Adequate calories
  • Vitamin C (collagen synthesis)
  • Zinc (wound healing)
  • Hydration

Skin Care

  • Keep clean and dry
  • Moisturize dry skin
  • Barrier cream for incontinence
  • Inspect skin daily

Wound Dressings

Hydrocolloid

  • Use: Stage 1-2, partial-thickness, clean wounds
  • Advantage: Maintains moist environment, self-adherent
  • Disadvantage: Can't use on infected wounds, difficult to assess wound

Hydrogel

  • Use: Dry wounds, necrotic wounds (autolytic debridement)
  • Advantage: Adds moisture, soothing
  • Disadvantage: Requires secondary dressing

Foam

  • Use: Moderate to heavy drainage, Stage 2-4
  • Advantage: Absorbent, maintains moist environment
  • Disadvantage: May dry out wound bed

Alginate (Calcium Alginate)

  • Use: Moderate to heavy drainage, infected wounds, tunneling wounds
  • Advantage: Highly absorbent, conforms to wound, hemostatic
  • Disadvantage: Requires secondary dressing

Transparent Film

  • Use: Stage 1, IV sites, superficial wounds
  • Advantage: Allows visualization, waterproof, autolytic debridement
  • Disadvantage: No absorbency, can cause skin stripping

Collagen

  • Use: Chronic, non-healing wounds
  • Advantage: Promotes granulation
  • Disadvantage: Expensive

Silver (Antimicrobial)

  • Use: Infected wounds, biofilm
  • Advantage: Broad antimicrobial
  • Disadvantage: Can stain skin, prolonged use may delay healing

Negative Pressure Wound Therapy (NPWT/VAC)

  • Use: Large wounds, dehisced wounds, Stage 3-4, grafts
  • Mechanism: Negative pressure promotes:
  • Granulation tissue
  • Wound contraction
  • Removes exudate
  • Reduces edema

Nursing care:

  • Check seal every shift
  • Empty canister when 2/3 full
  • Monitor drainage amount and type
  • Never clamp tubing (causes pressure buildup)
  • Keep on continuous (not intermittent) for first 48 hours

Debridement Methods

Method Description Use
Autolytic Body's own enzymes Mild slough, stable wound
Enzymatic Topical enzymes (Santyl) Slough, non-viable tissue
Mechanical Wet-to-dry dressings, irrigation Moderate slough
Sharp Scalpel, scissors Extensive necrosis
Surgical OR debridement Extensive necrosis, infection
Biosurgical Medical maggots Selective debridement

NCLEX tip: Wet-to-dry is NON-SELECTIVE debridement. It removes viable tissue too. Not recommended for clean granulating wounds.

Wound Complications

Infection Signs

  • Increasing pain
  • Erythema spreading
  • Warmth
  • Purulent drainage
  • Foul odor
  • Fever, elevated WBC
  • Delayed healing

Action: Culture, notify provider, consider antibiotics.

Dehiscence

  • Wound edges separate
  • May see fascia or organs
  • Action:
  1. Cover with sterile saline-moistened dressing
  2. Have patient lie still with knees bent
  3. Notify provider immediately
  4. Do NOT reapproximate edges
  5. NPO — may need OR

Evisceration

  • Organs protrude through wound
  • SURGICAL EMERGENCY
  • Action:
  1. Cover with sterile saline-moistened dressing
  2. Do NOT push organs back in
  3. Have patient lie supine with knees bent
  4. Notify provider/surgeon immediately
  5. NPO, IV access, type and cross

NCLEX scenario:

"A post-op patient's abdominal incision opens, and loops of intestine are visible. What is the FIRST action?"

Answer: Cover with sterile saline-moistened dressing. This prevents drying and contamination. Then notify surgeon. Do NOT push intestine back in.

NCLEX Practice Questions

Question 1:

"A patient has a pressure injury on the coccyx. The wound bed is red, partial-thickness, with no slough. What stage is this?"

A) Stage 1
B) Stage 2
C) Stage 3
D) Unstageable

Answer: B — Partial-thickness skin loss, pink/red wound bed, no slough = Stage 2.

Question 2:

"A patient has a wound covered with thick black, leathery tissue. The wound base cannot be seen. How is this staged?"

A) Stage 3
B) Stage 4
C) Unstageable
D) Deep tissue injury

Answer: C — Full-thickness wound covered with eschar = unstageable. Must debride to stage.

Question 3:

"A patient is bedbound and incontinent. Which intervention is MOST important for pressure injury prevention?"

A) Massage reddened areas
B) Use donut cushion
C) Reposition every 2 hours
D) Apply talcum powder

Answer: C — Repositioning every 2 hours is the single most important intervention. A is wrong (massage damages tissue). B is wrong (donut causes venous congestion). D is wrong (talcum cakes and causes irritation).

Question 4:

"A patient's surgical wound opens, and the patient says, 'Something popped.' The wound edges are separated but no organs are visible. What is this called?"

A) Evisceration
B) Dehiscence
C) Hematoma
D) Infection

Answer: B — Dehiscence = wound edges separate. Evisceration = organs visible.

Summary

Wound care is about assessment, staging, and appropriate interventions.

Key NCLEX points:

  1. Pressure injury staging — know the criteria cold
  2. Braden Scale — lower score = higher risk
  3. Prevention — turn every 2 hours, proper surfaces, nutrition
  4. Dressing selection — match to wound characteristics
  5. Complications — know dehiscence vs. evisceration
  6. Healing phases — hemostasis → inflammatory → proliferative → maturation

Practice the staging scenarios until they're automatic. The NCLEX loves pressure injury questions.
